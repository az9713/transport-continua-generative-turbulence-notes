5. Generative models of turbulence: sampling the steady state and losing the small scales

A three-dimensional turbulence solver must pass a transition before it produces its first useful state (10 to 20 minutes on 16 CPU cores in TurbDiff's dataset), and an autoregressive surrogate that replaces it must take hundreds of error-accumulating steps to cross the same transition. A generative model can instead sample the statistically steady state directly, provided that state has a well-defined distribution and the model reproduces it at every scale. This chapter states when that target exists (ergodicity and stationarity), describes how TurbDiff realizes it and what its metrics actually measure, and then derives why a diffusion model built on white Gaussian noise sees the small scales least: for the course path $X_t=t z+(1-t)\boldsymbol\xi$ each Fourier mode has signal-to-noise ratio $t^2\hat S(\kappa)/((1-t)^2\hat S_\xi)$, the crossover wavenumber $\kappa_\times(t)$ moves outward as $(t/(1-t))^{2/\lambda}$, and the exponential tail of the dissipation range makes those modes visible for only an exponentially short part of generation time. The answer to "is the generated turbulence right?" is therefore a protocol of physical statistics with a reference-to-reference baseline, and a detector of the wavenumber at which the generated spectrum leaves the reference.

5.1 What distribution should a generative model of turbulence sample?

Let $X\in\mathbb R^{3\times N^3}$ denote one sample: the three velocity components on an $N^3$ grid (TurbDiff also carries pressure, which changes nothing below). Physical flow time is written $t_{\mathrm f}$, so that $t\in[0,1]$ remains the generation time of the course, with $t=0$ noise and $t=1$ data. A solver started from an initial field $X^{(0)}$ inside a geometry and boundary specification $\mathcal B$ produces a trajectory $X(t_{\mathrm f})$. For each $t_{\mathrm f}$ the state is random only through whatever is random in $X^{(0)}$ and in the forcing, so the natural object is the law of $X(t_{\mathrm f})$ given $X^{(0)}$ and $\mathcal B$.

Lienen et al. replace this time-dependent family by one distribution. Their Eq. 2 defines $p_{\ge t_{\mathrm f}}$ as a long-time average along the solver trajectory: the limit as $T\to\infty$ of the average over $t_{\mathrm f}'\in[t_{\mathrm f},T]$ of the point mass at $X(t_{\mathrm f}')$, which assigns to each set of states the fraction of time the trajectory spends in it. Their argument, stated in their Section 4.1, is that after a transition of length $t_{\mathrm{turb}}$ the flow is fully developed and, because turbulence is often assumed to be ergodic, this distribution no longer depends on the initial state. They therefore define the task as finding a model $p_\theta$ with

$$p_\theta(\mathbf X\mid\mathcal B)\approx p_{\ge t_{\rm turb}}(\mathbf X\mid\mathcal B).\tag{5.1}$$

This is their Eq. 3. The right-hand side is a marginal: a one-time distribution over flow states, conditioned on geometry but not on any earlier state. It contains no information about how a state evolves; it answers questions such as "what is the distribution of vortex sizes behind this object", not "where will this vortex be in 5 ms". The alternative, an autoregressive surrogate that learns $X(t_{\mathrm f})\mapsto X(t_{\mathrm f}+\Delta t_{\mathrm f})$, must start from the solver's smooth initial state and roll forward until turbulence develops, which, in the paper's words, "can require hundreds of steps and lead to catastrophic error accumulation". A marginal model skips the transient entirely.

Training data for (5.1) are snapshots of solver runs taken after $t_{\mathrm{turb}}$. Whether a finite set of snapshots from one run represents the marginal is a question about time averages, and it has a precise answer under a decay assumption. Let $g$ be any scalar statistic of the state (one velocity component at one cell, a shell energy, a vortex count), and write $G(t_{\mathrm f})=g(X(t_{\mathrm f}))$. Call $G$ stationary if its finite-dimensional laws are invariant under shifts of $t_{\mathrm f}$; then it has a constant mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$ and autocovariance $C(h)=\mathrm{Cov}(G(t_{\mathrm f}),G(t_{\mathrm f}+h))$ depending only on the lag $h$. Define the integral time scale $\bar\tau=\sigma^{-2}\int_0^\infty C(h)\,dh$ whenever $C$ is integrable.

Proposition 5.1 — time averages of a decorrelating stationary statistic. Let $G$ be stationary with integrable autocovariance and $\bar G_T=T^{-1}\int_0^TG(t_{\mathrm f})\,dt_{\mathrm f}$. Then $\mathbb E\bar G_T=\mu$ and

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar G_T)=\frac2T\int_0^T\Bigl(1-\frac hT\Bigr)C(h)\,dh\le\frac2T\int_0^\infty|C(h)|\,dh,\qquad \lim_{T\to\infty}T\,\mathrm{Var}(\bar G_T)=2\sigma^2\bar\tau.\tag{5.2}$$

Proof. Linearity of expectation gives the mean. Fubini's theorem, valid because $|C|\le\sigma^2$ is bounded on the square, gives $\mathrm{Var}(\bar G_T)=T^{-2}\int_0^T\int_0^TC(t_1-t_2)\,dt_1dt_2$, using $C(-h)=C(h)$. The set of pairs with $|t_1-t_2|\in[h,h+dh]$ has area $2(T-h)\,dh$, which yields the first expression. Bounding $1-h/T\le1$ and $C\le|C|$ gives the inequality. For the limit, $T\,\mathrm{Var}(\bar G_T)=2\int_0^\infty\mathbf 1_{h\lt T}(1-h/T)C(h)\,dh$; the integrand is dominated by $|C|$ and converges pointwise to $C(h)$, so dominated convergence gives $2\int_0^\infty C=2\sigma^2\bar\tau$. ∎

The proposition makes the ergodicity assumption operational for every statistic that will be used in evaluation: a record of length $T$ estimates $\mu$ with the variance of $T/(2\bar\tau)$ independent samples, the count that Essentials (8.17) and course chapter 6 use for data budgets. It does not prove that every trajectory produces the same long-time distribution in (5.1). The proposition concerns one scalar statistic of a process already assumed stationary, whereas the independence from $X^{(0)}$ that Lienen et al. attribute to ergodicity is a property of the whole state distribution: the time averages of all statistics along almost every trajectory must converge to the same values. That hypothesis is assumed here, as in the paper, and is not derived.

5.2 When does the ergodic marginal stop being the right target?

Equation (5.1) rests on four separate assumptions, and each can fail in a way that a generative model would silently absorb. The first is that the transient has ended. States before $t_{\mathrm{turb}}$ have a law that depends on $t_{\mathrm f}$ and on $X^{(0)}$; including them in the training set mixes a time-dependent family into the target. The paper's evaluation selects reference samples from the second half of each simulation for this reason (read in the arXiv HTML), which is a practical cut, not a test of stationarity. Training uses a different cut: the paper's Appendix A discards the first 25 ms of each run and reports that the transition lasts roughly 22 to 45 ms depending on the object, so a run with a 45 ms transition keeps 20 ms of transition states, about 4 % of its retained 475 ms. A test is to compare a running statistic such as domain-averaged TKE between the two halves of the retained window against the scatter predicted by (5.2).

The second assumption is stationarity of the forcing and boundaries. Decaying turbulence, a ramped inflow or a periodic gust produce flows whose one-time law changes with $t_{\mathrm f}$; there is then no single $p_{\ge t_{\rm turb}}$, and a model trained on pooled snapshots learns a time average of different distributions. The third is the decay of correlations in Proposition 5.1. A flow with two long-lived states (a wake that locks to one side for the duration of a run, a recirculation bubble with hysteresis) is stationary but not ergodic over the record: time averages converge to the mean of the state the run happens to occupy.

Worked example 5.1 — a stationary statistic that is not ergodic. Let $Q=\pm1$ with probability $1/2$ each, drawn once at $t_{\mathrm f}=0$, and $G(t_{\mathrm f})=aQ+w(t_{\mathrm f})$ with $a\gt0$ and $w$ stationary, zero-mean, independent of $Q$, with integrable autocovariance $C_w$. Then $G$ is stationary with ensemble mean zero and $C(h)=a^2+C_w(h)$, which is the constant $a^2$ plus an integrable function and so is not integrable. Proposition 5.1 does not apply, and indeed $\bar G_T\to aQ$: one run reports mean $+a$ or $-a$, never the ensemble mean $0$. A generative model trained on many snapshots of one run learns the conditional law given $Q$, not (5.1). TurbDiff has several independent runs but only one per geometry, so for a given geometry its data represent both states only if the switching time is shorter than the record; several runs per geometry would remove that condition.

The fourth assumption is not ergodicity at all. TurbDiff conditions on geometry and evaluates on objects absent from training (27/9/9 split by object). Ergodicity justifies sampling the steady state of one geometry from snapshots of that geometry; generalizing to a new geometry is a claim about the smoothness of $\mathcal B\mapsto p_{\ge t_{\rm turb}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal B)$, tested only by the held-out objects. Finally, a marginal model cannot answer multi-time questions. Two processes can share every one-time law while having different trajectory laws, a distinction made precise for samplers in MIT chapter 3, Section 3.7. Temporal correlations, Lagrangian statistics and forecasts need a conditional model of $X(t_{\mathrm f}+\Delta t_{\mathrm f})$ given $X(t_{\mathrm f})$, which is the setting of Lazy Diffusion in Section 5.9.

Worked example 5.2 — how far apart are TurbDiff's reference samples? Each run lasts 0.5 s and is saved every 0.1 ms (5,000 states). The evaluation draws 16 states equidistantly from the second half (read in the arXiv HTML), a window of 0.25 s, so consecutive references are about 0.016 to 0.017 s apart ($0.25/16$ or $0.25/15$, depending on whether both ends of the window are used). The channel is 0.4 m long with inflow 20 m/s, so one flow-through time is $0.4/20=0.02$ s. The references are therefore spaced by about 0.8 flow-through times. The paper describes them as completely uncorrelated, and its Section 6.2 gives a separate figure: states 22 saved steps apart, 2.2 ms, "become approximately uncorrelated", so the reference spacing is about 7 to 8 times that distance ($0.0156/0.0022$ to $0.0167/0.0022$). By (5.2) the description holds for a statistic whose integral time is small compared with 0.017 s. The paper does not state which statistic gives the 2.2 ms figure and does not report a separate integral time for the largest wake structures, so the description must be checked for those structures against their own autocorrelation.

5.3 How does TurbDiff realize the marginal?

Both papers use symbols that the course reserves for other quantities, so they are translated on entry. Their wavenumber $k$ is written $\kappa$ here ($k$ is turbulent kinetic energy); their fluid velocity $\mathbf u$ is written $\mathbf v$ ($u_t$ is the velocity of a probability flow); their Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ or $\varepsilon$ is written $\boldsymbol\xi$ ($\varepsilon$ is the dissipation rate); TurbDiff's number of diffusion steps is $N_s$ ($N$ is the grid side); Lazy Diffusion's physical time $t$ is $t_{\mathrm f}$. Its noise schedule keeps the name $\beta(\tau)$, so the fitted exponent 5.2 of the Essentials model spectrum and the β-plane parameter 20 of its first system are written as numbers. The noising clock $\tau$, with $\tau=0$ at the data, and the diffusion states $x_\tau$, $x_0$ follow the course convention. Quotations keep the papers' symbols.

The data are large-eddy simulations run with OpenFOAM: a channel of $0.4\times0.1\times0.1$ m with inflow at 20 m/s, viscosity $\nu=10^{-5}$ (units m²/s) and a stated Reynolds number $2\times10^5$, discretized on $192\times48\times48$ cells, so every cell is a cube of side $0.4/192\approx2.08$ mm. There are 45 simulations, each with a different object in the channel, split 27/9/9 into training, validation and test geometries. Each run covers 0.5 s with a state saved every 0.1 ms, 5,000 states per run, about 2.2 TB in total; the stored fields are velocity and pressure. Being LES, the data resolve eddies above a filter scale and model the rest; any spectral statement about TurbDiff therefore refers to the LES-resolved range, not to a DNS dissipation range.

The generative model is a DDPM with $N_s=500$ steps (the course reserves $N$ for the grid side). The paper describes its noise schedule as one in which the log-signal-to-noise ratio falls linearly between $1\times10^3$ and $1\times10^{-5}$. Read literally those are values of the logarithm; the released code settles the meaning. Its function log_snr_linear_beta_schedule in turbdiff/models/ddpm.py solves for the cumulative signal factor $\bar\alpha_n$ of step $n$ so that the scalar SNR $\bar\alpha_n/(1-\bar\alpha_n)$ runs from $10^3$ at the first step to $10^{-5}$ at the last with its logarithm linear in the step index. Exercise 5.2 uses this schedule. The denoiser is a 3D U-Net with four downsampling levels and full attention at the coarsest level. Training uses batch 6 for 10 epochs with RAdam and a learning rate decaying from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$. The data are normalized by the maximum velocity norm and maximum pressure magnitude (read in the arXiv HTML), so the normalized fields have mean square below one.

Geometry enters in two ways, which together make TurbDiff a conditional generator of $p(X\mid\mathcal B)$ rather than an unconditional one. A learned embedding of each cell's type is concatenated to the input. Dirichlet values are imposed by inpainting, the paper's Eq. 4: at every reverse step, cells whose values are fixed by the boundary specification are not denoised by the network but resampled from the exact forward posterior given their known clean value, so the boundary cells follow the forward process of their prescribed data while the network denoises only the fluid cells. The forward noising itself is the same for every geometry; conditioning changes only what the reverse process knows. Conditioning therefore does not change the per-mode SNR of Sections 5.5–5.6, and the posterior quantities of Section 5.8 are read as posteriors given $\mathcal B$. A separate limitation applies to this dataset. The channel has walls, an inlet, an outlet and an object, so its velocity field is neither periodic nor statistically homogeneous. The SNR ratio in (5.3) needs only that the noise is white and independent of the data, but the decoupling of modes in Proposition 5.3, and with it Propositions 5.7, 5.8 and 5.10, rests on homogeneity and does not hold for the whole channel. Sections 5.5–5.8 therefore model a homogeneous field; they do not describe TurbDiff's channel mode by mode.

The paper lists three limitations: cost grows cubically with resolution per dimension and sampling is slow, the network is not equivariant to symmetries of the problem, and geometry is represented only to zeroth order by the cell-type grid. Training of a small analogue on the course's data is the subject of labs/lab2_generative_turbulence_3d.py; this chapter supplies the evaluation that the lab's output must pass.

5.4 What do TurbDiff's metrics measure?

Because a generated sample has no ground-truth twin, every metric must compare distributions or statistics. The paper's Eq. 5 defines the Wasserstein-2 distance between two empirical sample sets $P,P'$ with respect to an underlying distance $d$: $W_2(P,P')=(\min_{q\in\Gamma(P,P')}\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim q}d(x,y)^2)^{1/2}$, where $\Gamma$ is the set of couplings. (The paper writes $\mu,\nu$ and $\gamma$; here $\nu$ is the viscosity and $\gamma$ the schedule exponent of Section 5.9.) Two choices of $d$ then follow.

The first is spectral, and it is not the course's energy spectrum. Read in the arXiv HTML, TurbDiff's Eq. 6 first forms the cell kinetic-energy field $E_{\mathbf i}=\frac12\|\mathbf v_{\mathbf i}-\bar{\mathbf v}_{\mathbf i}\|_2^2$, half the squared deviation of each cell's velocity from its mean, then takes the 3D Fourier transform of this scalar field over a cuboid and integrates the squared magnitude over spheres $\|\boldsymbol\kappa\|=\kappa$. The result is the power spectrum of the scalar field $\tfrac12|\mathbf v'|^2$, a fourth-order statistic of the velocity. The course's $E(\kappa)$ (chapter 3; Essentials (11.2)–(11.3)) is built from the Fourier coefficients of the velocity itself, is second order, and integrates to $k$. The two share a name and a shell integration but respond differently to errors: the TKE-field spectrum depends on the relative arrangement of energetic regions (the two-point correlation of $\tfrac12|\mathbf v'|^2$), though not on where the whole pattern sits, whereas $E(\kappa)$ is sensitive to how energy is distributed over scales. A model can match one and miss the other.

The paper's Eq. 7 then defines the distance between two samples as the $L^2$ distance between their log spectra, $d(X^{(1)},X^{(2)})=\|\log E_{X^{(1)}}-\log E_{X^{(2)}}\|_2$, and states that this distance is denoted $W_{2,\rm TKE}$. Read alone, that sentence names Eq. 7 itself. Read with its section, the tabulated quantity is a sample-set $W_2$ with Eq. 7 as its ground distance: Section 6.3 opens by stating that sample quality is quantified by the $W_2$ distance to solver samples, writes Eq. 5 with $d$ unspecified, says "It remains to specify the underlying distance function $d$", and calls Eq. 7 "our first choice of distance". The released code confirms this reading: the class WassersteinTKE in turbdiff/models/metrics.py forms the matrix of distances between generated and reference samples and returns the $W_2$ value of that matrix. The evaluation compares 16 generated states $X^{(i)}$ with 16 reference states $Y^{(j)}$ per object, with equal weights, and for equal weights the optimal coupling is a one-to-one matching (Birkhoff's theorem), so $W_{2,\rm TKE}^2=\tfrac1{16}\min_\pi\sum_{i=1}^{16}d(X^{(i)},Y^{(\pi(i))})^2$ over permutations $\pi$. Two distances therefore nest: an $L^2$ distance between the log spectra of two states and a Wasserstein matching between two sets of states. Neither regards a spectrum as a distribution over $\kappa$. Because a transform over the $192\times48\times48$ domain would resolve higher frequencies along the long side than along the short ones, Eqs. 9–10 evaluate the spectral distance on three $48^3$ blocks (behind the object, mid-channel, near the outlet), combine the three as a root sum of squares, and compute each block's squared distance as $\int_1^{23}(\log E_a-\log E_b)^2\,d\kappa$. The upper limit 23 lies one below 24, the Nyquist index of a $48^3$ block.

Proposition 5.2 — what the log-spectrum distance sees. Let $E_a=g\,E_b$ for a positive function $g$ on $[1,23]$. Then $\|\log E_a-\log E_b\|_2^2=\int_1^{23}(\log g)^2\,d\kappa$. In particular a uniform rescaling $g\equiv c$ gives $|\log c|\sqrt{22}$; a deficit confined to the top $m$ wavenumber units with $g\le e^{-1}$ there contributes at least $m$ to the squared distance; and the distance is unchanged by any change of the Fourier phases of the field whose spectrum is taken.

Proof. $\log E_a-\log E_b=\log g$ pointwise, and the interval has length 22. Where $g\le e^{-1}$, $(\log g)^2\ge1$. The spectra depend on squared moduli of that field's Fourier coefficients only, so its phases do not enter. ∎

Two consequences follow. Every wavenumber unit counts equally in the logarithm, so a collapse of the smallest resolved scales, which carries little energy, can dominate the distance; that is the property wanted for detecting collapse. Conversely, which phases are invisible depends on the spectrum. For the course's $E(\kappa)$ they are the phases of the velocity coefficients, so two fields with equal modal powers and scrambled phases have distance zero. For Eq. 6 they are the phases of the transform of $\tfrac12|\mathbf v'|^2$, and the integration over spheres also discards direction. The paper states the limit as invariance "to wrong positioning or orientations of flow patterns", and adds that the spectrum neglects pressure. That is why it introduces a second metric. For each simulation it approximates each cell's marginal velocity distribution by an isotropic Gaussian and clusters the cells by k-means into regions $\mathcal R$, with the closed-form $W_2$ distance between Gaussians as the clustering distance. The Gaussians end there. Eq. 8 compares two samples by the exact $W_2$ distance between their sets of cell vectors in each region, each vector holding normalized velocity, vorticity and pressure, and combines the squared distances weighted by region size; Appendix E splits regions above 512 cells because that exact computation scales as $O(n^3)$ in the cell count. The released code (class WassersteinMetric) nests this per-sample distance in an outer $W_2$ matching over the sample pairs, so $W_{2,\mathcal R}$ has the same two-level form as $W_{2,\rm TKE}$. A third, task-level quantity is the location of the maximum of the mean TKE behind the object.

The reported numbers for three rows of Table 1 are: TurbDiff $W_{2,\rm TKE}=3.9\pm0.4$, $W_{2,\mathcal R}=1.38\pm0.04$, sampling time 20.63 s; DilResNet-22, an autoregressive network started from a solver state and rolled out for 22 steps (2.2 ms), $2.15\pm0.06$ and $1.240\pm0.001$; DilResNet-init, the same network started from the noised initial condition and rolled out for 200 steps, $60\pm47$ and $4.6\times10^8$. TurbDiff locates the TKE maximum with RMSE $5.9\pm1.8$ cells, about 1.2 cm ($5.9\times2.08$ mm $=12.3$ mm). The solver needs about 10 minutes of compute to pass the transition, so TurbDiff is about 30 times faster to a first realistic sample ($600/20.63\approx29$); that ratio crosses hardware, since model times were measured on an NVIDIA A100 and solver time on 16 cores of an Intel Xeon E5-2630. The scores must be read with each model's input in view. DilResNet-22 has the lower value on both distances (2.15 against 3.9, 1.240 against 1.38), and it starts from a true turbulent state. The paper calls it "a very strong baseline because it already starts with a sample from the distribution that we want to sample from", and attributes its scores to training on the smallest time step and to 22 roll-out steps that "still have tolerable error accumulation". The model that has to generate turbulence from the initial condition has $W_{2,\rm TKE}$ about 15 times TurbDiff's (60 against 3.9) and $W_{2,\mathcal R}$ of $4.6\times10^8$ against 1.38, which the paper attributes to error accumulation over the longer roll-out.

5.5 How does white noise corrupt each Fourier mode?

Take the periodic box $[0,\mathcal L)^3$ of side $\mathcal L=2\pi$ with $N^3$ points, so wavevectors $\boldsymbol\kappa$ have integer components in $[-N/2,N/2)$, and use the course transform $\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}=N^{-3}\sum_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}$. Consider one velocity component $z(\mathbf x)$ of a data sample (the three components behave identically under white noise). Assume $z$ is real, zero-mean and statistically homogeneous with covariance $R(\mathbf r)=\mathbb E[z(\mathbf x)z(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)]$, and define the modal power $\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\mathbb E|\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2$. The noise $\boldsymbol\xi$ has independent standard normal entries, independent of $z$.

Proposition 5.3 — modes decouple, and white noise is flat. Under these assumptions $\mathbb E[\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\overline{\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}}]=\delta_{\boldsymbol\kappa\boldsymbol\kappa'}\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ with $\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)=N^{-3}\sum_{\mathbf r}R(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}$ and $\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)=R(\mathbf 0)$. For the noise, $\hat S_\xi=N^{-3}$ at every $\boldsymbol\kappa$. Consequently, along the course path $X_t=tz+(1-t)\boldsymbol\xi$,

$$\hat X_{t,\boldsymbol\kappa}=t\,\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}+(1-t)\,\hat\xi_{\boldsymbol\kappa},\qquad \mathbb E|\hat X_{t,\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2=t^2\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)+(1-t)^2\hat S_\xi,\qquad \mathrm{SNR}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)=\frac{t^2\,\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)}{(1-t)^2\,\hat S_\xi}.\tag{5.3}$$

Proof. Expand both transforms and substitute $\mathbf x=\mathbf x'+\mathbf r$, with indices taken modulo the periodic grid:

$$\mathbb E[\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\overline{\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}}]=N^{-6}\sum_{\mathbf r}R(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\sum_{\mathbf x'}e^{-i(\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa')\cdot\mathbf x'}.$$

The inner sum is $N^3\delta_{\boldsymbol\kappa\boldsymbol\kappa'}$ by orthogonality of the discrete exponentials, which gives the first two claims. Setting $\mathbf r=\mathbf 0$ in the inverse relation $R(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\hat S e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}$ gives Parseval. White noise has $R(\mathbf r)=\delta_{\mathbf r\mathbf 0}$, so $\hat S_\xi=N^{-3}$. The transform is linear, giving the first identity in (5.3); independence and zero means remove the cross term from $\mathbb E|\hat X|^2$, and the SNR is the ratio of the two remaining terms. ∎

Two remarks fix the meaning of (5.3). First, for unit-variance data Parseval gives $\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}N^3\hat S=N^3$, so the relative modal power $N^3\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ averages to one over the grid and $\mathrm{SNR}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)=r(t)\,N^3\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ with the scalar ratio $r(t)=t^2/(1-t)^2$. Modes with more than the average power are cleaner than the scalar SNR suggests and modes with less are noisier; for a decaying spectrum that means high wavenumbers. Second, the exponent of $\hat S$ is not the exponent of $E(\kappa)$. With shell summation, $E(\kappa)$ collects $\tfrac12\sum_i\hat S_{ii}$ over a shell containing about $4\pi\kappa^2$ modes, so for isotropic turbulence $\hat S(\kappa)\approx2E(\kappa)/(3\cdot4\pi\kappa^2)$ per component. An inertial range $E\propto\kappa^{-5/3}$ therefore has modal power $\hat S\propto\kappa^{-\lambda}$ with $\lambda=11/3$ in three dimensions ($\lambda=5/3+1$ in two dimensions, where the shell perimeter grows like $\kappa$). The value $\lambda=5/3$ describes the modal power of a one-dimensional signal with a $-5/3$ spectrum; applied to a 3D grid it is the error of reading the shell spectrum as the modal power. Lazy Diffusion's statement that the SNR "decays monotonically in wavenumber $|k|$ for spectra $S(k)\propto|k|^{-\lambda}$" concerns this modal power (its $k$ is the course's $\kappa$ and its $S$ the course's $\hat S$).

Proposition 5.4 — only the ratio of signal to noise coefficient matters. For any Gaussian path $X=a\,z+b\,\boldsymbol\xi$ with deterministic $a\ge0$, $b\gt0$, the conditional law of $z$ given $X$ equals its conditional law given $X/b=(a/b)z+\boldsymbol\xi$, so two paths carry the same information about $z$ whenever their ratios $a/b$ agree. The DDPM path $x_\tau=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_\tau}\,\boldsymbol\xi$ in the noising clock $\tau$ and the course path are matched by $t/(1-t)=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau/(1-\bar\alpha_\tau)}$, and the per-mode SNR is $\bar\alpha_\tau\hat S/((1-\bar\alpha_\tau)\hat S_\xi)$.

Proof. The map $X\mapsto X/b$ is a deterministic bijection, so it generates the same information; conditional laws given either variable coincide. $X/b$ depends on the path only through $a/b$. Equating $t/(1-t)$ with $\sqrt{\bar\alpha}/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}$ equates the ratios; substituting $a=\sqrt{\bar\alpha}$, $b=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}$ in the computation of (5.3) gives the last formula. ∎

Every statement below that depends only on the ratio $a/b$ therefore transfers to TurbDiff's DDPM and Lazy Diffusion's forward SDE after this change of clock, subject to the homogeneity limitation of Section 5.3. Lazy Diffusion's Eq. 8, $\mathrm{SNR}(k,\tau)=\bar\alpha_\tau A|k|^{-\lambda}/((1-\bar\alpha_\tau)\sigma^2)$, is the last formula of Proposition 5.4: the paper uses a unitary transform, which multiplies $\hat S$ and $\hat S_\xi$ by the same factor $N^3$, so its $A|k|^{-\lambda}$ is $N^3\hat S$ and its $\sigma^2$ is $N^3\hat S_\xi$. The scalar SNR of the course path at time $t$ is $r(t)=t^2/(1-t)^2$; TurbDiff's end points $10^3$ and $10^{-5}$ correspond to $t=\sqrt{10^3}/(1+\sqrt{10^3})\approx0.969$ and $t\approx0.00315$.

5.6 Where is the crossover wavenumber, and how does it move?

Call a mode signal-dominated at time $t$ if $\mathrm{SNR}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\ge1$, that is if the data contribute at least as much variance to $\hat X_{t,\boldsymbol\kappa}$ as the noise does. For a modal power that depends on $|\boldsymbol\kappa|=\kappa$ only, define $\zeta(\kappa)=\sqrt{\hat S_\xi/\hat S(\kappa)}$, the noise-to-signal amplitude ratio of the clean data. The crossover wavenumber below is written $\kappa_\times$; the symbol $\kappa_c$ is reserved for the filter cutoff $\pi/\Delta$ of chapter 4, a different quantity.

Proposition 5.5 — the crossover wavenumber. (a) If $\hat S$ is non-increasing in $\kappa$, then at every $t\in(0,1)$ the signal-dominated modes form a ball $\kappa\le\kappa_\times(t)$, and mode $\kappa$ is signal-dominated exactly for $t\ge t^*(\kappa)=\zeta(\kappa)/(1+\zeta(\kappa))$, a window of length $1-t^*=1/(1+\zeta(\kappa))$. (b) For $\hat S=A\kappa^{-\lambda}$ with $A,\lambda\gt0$,

$$\kappa_\times(t)=\Bigl(\frac{A}{\hat S_\xi}\Bigr)^{1/\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{t}{1-t}\Bigr)^{2/\lambda},\qquad \frac{d\log\kappa_\times}{dt}=\frac{2}{\lambda\,t(1-t)}\gt0,\tag{5.4}$$

so $\kappa_\times$ increases strictly from $0$ to $\infty$ as $t$ runs from $0$ to $1$, and doubling $\kappa_\times$ requires multiplying $t/(1-t)$ by $2^{\lambda/2}$.

Proof. (a) By (5.3), $\mathrm{SNR}\ge1$ is equivalent to $t/(1-t)\ge\zeta(\kappa)$, since all quantities are positive. The left side is strictly increasing in $t$, which gives the threshold $t^*=\zeta/(1+\zeta)$ and the window length. For fixed $t$, $\zeta$ is non-decreasing in $\kappa$ when $\hat S$ is non-increasing, so the set $\{\kappa:\zeta(\kappa)\le t/(1-t)\}$ is an interval starting at zero. (b) Solve $t^2A\kappa^{-\lambda}=(1-t)^2\hat S_\xi$ for $\kappa$. Differentiate $\log\kappa_\times=\text{const}+(2/\lambda)[\log t-\log(1-t)]$. Doubling $\kappa_\times$ adds $\log2$ to $\log\kappa_\times$, which requires adding $(\lambda/2)\log2$ to $\log(t/(1-t))$. ∎

Read in the generation clock, the large scales emerge first and the small scales last; read in the noising clock $\tau=1-t$, the small scales are destroyed first: mode $\kappa$ becomes noise-dominated at $\tau=1/(1+\zeta(\kappa))$. The exponent controls how fast the front moves. With $\lambda=11/3$ one must raise $t/(1-t)$ by $2^{11/6}\approx3.56$ to resolve one more octave, whereas the mistaken $\lambda=5/3$ would suggest a factor $2^{5/6}\approx1.78$.

Worked example 5.3 — the $64^3$ table. Normalize $\hat S=A\kappa^{-\lambda}$ to unit variance on the $64^3$ grid. The program of Section 5.12 gives $A=4.735\times10^{-2}$ for $\lambda=11/3$, so $A/\hat S_\xi=AN^3=1.241\times10^4$ and $\kappa_\times(1/2)=(1.241\times10^4)^{3/11}=13.08$. At $t=0.9$, $t/(1-t)=9$ and $\kappa_\times=13.08\times9^{6/11}=43.35$; at $t=0.99$, $\kappa_\times=160.34$, beyond the largest wavenumber magnitude on the grid, $32\sqrt3\approx55.4$. At $t=0.1$ only $\kappa\le3.94$ is signal-dominated. For TurbDiff's cleanest level, scalar SNR $10^3$, the same normalized power law gives $\kappa_\times=86.0$, so every resolved mode of a pure $\kappa^{-11/3}$ field would be signal-dominated there; with the dissipation-range model of the next section, the first noise-dominated shell at SNR $10^3$ is shell 30. Max-norm normalization lowers the data variance below one and lowers every per-mode SNR by the same factor.

5.7 Why is the dissipation range lost first?

A pure power law already orders modes from high to low wavenumber, but it gives a power-law window: by Proposition 5.5, $1-t^*\approx\zeta^{-1}\propto\kappa^{-\lambda/2}$ for weak modes. Real spectra end in a dissipation range where $E$ decays faster than any power. Essentials (14.9)–(14.10) model this by $E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}f_L(\kappa L)f_\eta(\kappa\eta)$ with

$$f_\eta(\kappa\eta)=\exp\Bigl\{-5.2\Bigl[\bigl((\kappa\eta)^4+c_\eta^4\bigr)^{1/4}-c_\eta\Bigr]\Bigr\},\qquad c_\eta=0.40,\tag{5.5}$$

where $5.2$ is the fitted exponent of Essentials chapter 14 (written as a number here because the symbol $\beta$ is taken by the noise schedule), $\eta$ is the Kolmogorov length, $L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon$ is a large-scale length, not the box side $\mathcal L$, and $f_L$ shapes the energy-containing range.

Proposition 5.6 — the generation window shrinks exponentially in the dissipation range. Let $\hat S(\kappa)=\hat S_0(\kappa)f_\eta(\kappa\eta)$ with $f_\eta$ as in (5.5) and any positive $\hat S_0$. Then the window of Proposition 5.5 satisfies

$$1-t^*(\kappa)\le\sqrt{\frac{\hat S(\kappa)}{\hat S_\xi}}\le e^{2.6\,c_\eta}\sqrt{\frac{\hat S_0(\kappa)}{\hat S_\xi}}\;e^{-2.6\,\kappa\eta}.\tag{5.6}$$

Each increase of $\kappa\eta$ by $1/2.6\approx0.385$ divides the bound by $e$, on top of whatever decay $\hat S_0$ supplies.

Proof. $1-t^*=1/(1+\zeta)\le1/\zeta=\sqrt{\hat S/\hat S_\xi}$. For $x\ge0$ and $c\gt0$, $(x^4+c^4)^{1/4}\ge x$, so the bracket in (5.5) is at least $x-c$ and $f_\eta(x)\le e^{-5.2(x-c)}$. Taking the square root halves the exponent. ∎

The proposition turns "lost first" into a statement about a share of the generation clock. When training draws $t$ uniformly, as flow matching does, $1-t^*(\kappa)$ is the fraction of training examples in which mode $\kappa$ carries more data than noise. In the dissipation range that fraction decays exponentially with $\kappa\eta$, in the inertial range only as a power. The program places the model on the $64^3$ grid with $L=1$ and $\eta=1/20$ ($\kappa\eta=1.6$ at $\kappa=32$) and compares with a $\kappa^{-11/3}$ law matched at $\kappa=4$: the windows are 0.342, 0.0826 and 0.0186 at $\kappa\eta=0.8$, 1.2 and 1.6, against 0.521, 0.341 and 0.234 for the matched power law. Lazy Diffusion's description, a degradation "with high-wavenumber modes collapsing exponentially earlier", is accurate in this sense for a dissipation range; for a pure power law, which is the assumption of the paper's own Eq. 8, the separation in time is algebraic.

The physical consequence is which statistic fails first. The dissipation spectrum $D(\kappa)=2\nu\kappa^2E(\kappa)$ of Essentials chapter 14 peaks near $\kappa\eta\approx0.26$, and in the model at $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=600$ 80 % of $\varepsilon$ lies between $\kappa\eta\approx0.10$ and $0.73$ (Table 14.1; the chapter's recomputed values are 0.097 and 0.723). In the model of the jet at $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453$ (Table 14.3) the energy-containing range holds 88.9 % of $k$ and 2.0 % of $\varepsilon$, and the dissipation range 3.6 % of $k$ and 89.0 % of $\varepsilon$. The modes that a white-noise path hides longest are therefore the modes that carry the dissipation. In the program's Wiener experiment below, a field that loses them shows a 5 % deficit in TKE and a 23 % deficit in gradient variance.

The same ordering appears for blurring in course chapter 4, where the heat semigroup damps mode $\kappa$ by $e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}$. The mechanisms differ: blur removes high-wavenumber signal, white noise buries it under a flat floor, but both make the dissipation range the last part of the field to be reconstructed.

5.8 What does an optimal denoiser do to the spectrum?

Proposition 5.5 is a statement about the forward process. An exact reverse sampler with the exact score reproduces the data law at every wavenumber regardless of SNR (MIT chapter 3, Section 3.8); low SNR alone does not imply a wrong spectrum. Spectral collapse arises when an estimator built from the noisy state replaces sampling, or when the learned score is inaccurate at weak modes. The first mechanism can be computed exactly for a Gaussian field. Assume now that $z$ is a Gaussian homogeneous field; then by Proposition 5.3 the Fourier coefficients of $z$ and $\boldsymbol\xi$ are uncorrelated across distinct pairs $\{\boldsymbol\kappa,-\boldsymbol\kappa\}$, hence independent, and $\hat X_{t,-\boldsymbol\kappa}=\overline{\hat X_{t,\boldsymbol\kappa}}$ adds no information.

Proposition 5.7 — posterior-mean attenuation. For a Gaussian homogeneous $z$ and $0\lt t\lt1$, the posterior mean of each mode is

$$\mathbb E[\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\mid X_t]=\frac{H}{t}\,\hat X_{t,\boldsymbol\kappa},\qquad H=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{1+\mathrm{SNR}},\qquad \mathbb E\bigl|\mathbb E[\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\mid X_t]\bigr|^2=H\,\hat S,\qquad \mathbb E\bigl|\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}-\mathbb E[\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\mid X_t]\bigr|^2=\frac{\hat S}{1+\mathrm{SNR}}.\tag{5.7}$$

The posterior-mean field, which minimizes $\mathbb E\|F(X_t)-z\|^2$ over all functions $F$, has modal power $H\hat S$: it keeps modes with $\kappa\ll\kappa_\times$, halves the power at $\kappa_\times$, and removes modes with $\mathrm{SNR}\ll1$ in proportion to their SNR.

Proof. Let $c=\mathbb E[\hat z\overline{\hat X}]/\mathbb E|\hat X|^2=t\hat S/(t^2\hat S+(1-t)^2\hat S_\xi)$, which equals $H/t$ after dividing numerator and denominator by $(1-t)^2\hat S_\xi$. By construction $\hat z-c\hat X$ is uncorrelated with $\hat X$; for modes with $\boldsymbol\kappa\ne-\boldsymbol\kappa$ it is also uncorrelated with $\overline{\hat X}=\hat X_{-\boldsymbol\kappa}$ by Proposition 5.3, and for the real self-conjugate modes the two coincide. Jointly Gaussian and uncorrelated variables are independent, so $\hat z-c\hat X$ is independent of $X_t$ (whose other modes are independent of this pair), and $\mathbb E[\hat z\mid X_t]=c\hat X$. Its power is $c^2(t^2\hat S+(1-t)^2\hat S_\xi)=H\hat S$, and the error power is $\hat S-H\hat S=\hat S/(1+\mathrm{SNR})$. The conditional mean minimizes mean-square error over all square-integrable functions of $X_t$ (the projection argument of MIT chapter 2, Proposition 2.2). ∎

Any network trained by mean-square regression of the clean field from a single noisy input approaches this estimator at its population optimum. For non-Gaussian turbulence the posterior mean is nonlinear and can retain more structure, but the Gaussian case sets the scale: wherever the data's own statistics do not let the small scales be inferred from the large ones, a mean predictor fills them in with their conditional mean, which is smaller than the field. The program's experiment at $t=1/2$ shows the measured shell ratios following $H$ shell by shell (0.6852 against 0.6593 at shell 12, 0.0416 against 0.0424 at shell 20), with the half-power point at shell 14, the first shell with $\mathrm{SNR}\lt1$.

The second mechanism concerns what training can learn about a weak mode. In noise-prediction form (MIT chapter 3, Section 3.4) the network predicts $\hat\xi$ from $\hat X_t$.

Proposition 5.8 — a weak mode supplies second-order supervision. In the Gaussian setting, the predictor $\hat X_t/(1-t)$, which treats the mode as pure noise, has risk $\hat S_\xi\,\mathrm{SNR}$ for the target $\hat\xi$; the optimal predictor has risk $\hat S_\xi\,\mathrm{SNR}/(1+\mathrm{SNR})$. The reduction available from learning the signal in that mode is

$$\hat S_\xi\,\frac{\mathrm{SNR}^2}{1+\mathrm{SNR}}\;\approx\;\hat S_\xi\,\mathrm{SNR}^2\quad(\mathrm{SNR}\ll1).\tag{5.8}$$

Proof. $\hat\xi-\hat X/(1-t)=-t\hat z/(1-t)$, whose power is $t^2\hat S/(1-t)^2=\hat S_\xi\mathrm{SNR}$. As in Proposition 5.7, the optimal predictor is $c'\hat X$ with $c'=(1-t)\hat S_\xi/\mathbb E|\hat X|^2$, and its risk is $\hat S_\xi-(1-t)^2\hat S_\xi^2/\mathbb E|\hat X|^2=\hat S_\xi(1-1/(1+\mathrm{SNR}))$. Subtract. ∎

A mode with SNR $0.1$ offers a loss reduction of about one percent of its noise variance, and one with SNR $0.01$ about $10^{-4}$. Averaged over a training clock in which a dissipation-range mode is signal-dominated only for the short window of Proposition 5.6, the gradient signal about that mode is small, which is one quantitative reading of Lazy Diffusion's statement that the network "receives virtually no usable supervision for high-$k$ modes" at moderate or large noise. It is a statement about learnability, not about the exact sampler.

5.9 The two mitigations of Lazy Diffusion, stated precisely

Lazy Diffusion addresses a different task from TurbDiff. Its target is the one-step conditional forecast $p(X(t_{\mathrm f}+\Delta t)\mid X(t_{\mathrm f}))$, sampled repeatedly to produce long autoregressive rollouts (read in the arXiv HTML; the paper writes $X(t)$ for physical time). Its systems are 2D forced Kolmogorov flow on a β-plane (Reynolds number $10{,}000$, β-plane parameter 20, forcing wavenumber 4, $256\times256$ pseudo-spectral) and GLORYS Gulf of Mexico surface velocity at 1/12° on a $192\times320$ grid. Its forward process is the DDPM path $x_\tau=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_\tau}\,\boldsymbol\xi$ in the noising clock, with $\bar\alpha_\tau=\prod_{\tau'\le\tau}(1-\beta_{\tau'})$, $\beta_{\tau'}=\beta(\tau')\,d\tau'$ in the continuous limit. Here $x_0$ is the prediction target and $\boldsymbol\xi$ replaces the paper's $\epsilon$. The paper's Section 3.1.2 sets $x_0=X(t+\Delta t)$, the future state; its Section 3.5 states that the ocean model predicts the increments $(\Delta U,\Delta V)$, and its released code noises the increment $X(t+\Delta t)-X(t)$ in both systems. Where the target is an increment, $\hat S$ below is the modal power of the increment, not of the state. The field of the first system is vorticity, not velocity, so the paper's $\lambda$ is not the velocity exponent of Section 5.5. By Proposition 5.4 the per-mode analysis of Sections 5.5–5.8 applies with the conditioning state added to every posterior, subject to homogeneity: the forcing $\sin4x+\sin4y$ of the first system and the coastline of the second make both fields inhomogeneous, so the decoupling of modes is an approximation there.

Mitigation 1: a power-law noise schedule. The paper's schedule is

$$\beta(\tau)=\beta_{\min}+(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\,\tau^\gamma,\qquad\tau\in[0,1],\tag{5.9}$$

and it states that raising $\gamma$ "concentrates the noise injection near the end" of the forward process. To make this exact, take logarithms of the product: $\log\bar\alpha_\tau=\sum\log(1-\beta_{\tau'})=-\sum\beta_{\tau'}+O(\sum\beta_{\tau'}^2)$, and the correction vanishes as $d\tau'\to0$, so $\bar\alpha_\tau=e^{-B(\tau)}$ with

$$B(\tau)=\int_0^\tau\beta(\tau')\,d\tau'=\beta_{\min}\tau+(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\frac{\tau^{\gamma+1}}{\gamma+1}.\tag{5.10}$$

The paper's example of this concentration is that raising $\gamma$ from 1 to 5 moves three-quarters of the total noise addition into the final 20 % or so of diffusion time. Equation (5.10) reproduces it when the power-law term dominates: $B(0.8)/B(1)\approx0.8^{\gamma+1}$, so the last 20 % of the clock holds $1-0.8^6=74$ % of $B$ at $\gamma=5$, against $1-0.8^2=36$ % at $\gamma=1$. For unit-variance data, mode $\boldsymbol\kappa$ is signal-dominated while $\bar\alpha_\tau N^3\hat S\ge1-\bar\alpha_\tau$, that is while $B(\tau)\le c(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\log(1+N^3\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa))$. Its survival time $\tau_s(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ is the solution of $B(\tau_s)=c$, or $1$ if $B(1)\le c$.

Proposition 5.9 — what the exponent can and cannot change. Let $\beta_{\max}\gt\beta_{\min}\gt0$. (a) At fixed $\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}$, $B(\tau)$ decreases in $\gamma$ for every $\tau\in(0,1]$; hence every survival time increases with $\gamma$, and so does the terminal $\bar\alpha_1=\exp[-\beta_{\min}-(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})/(\gamma+1)]$, which moves the terminal law away from $\mathcal N(0,I)$. (b) If instead $B(1)=B_1$ is held fixed by $\beta_{\max}=\beta_{\min}+(\gamma+1)(B_1-\beta_{\min})$, then $B(\tau)=\beta_{\min}\tau+(B_1-\beta_{\min})\tau^{\gamma+1}$ still decreases in $\gamma$ on $(0,1)$, so survival times increase while the terminal law is unchanged. (c) For every $\gamma$, $\tau_s(\boldsymbol\kappa)\le c(\boldsymbol\kappa)/\beta_{\min}$: a mode with $\log(1+N^3\hat S)\ll\beta_{\min}$ is lost within a noising interval that no exponent can lengthen. (d) With the exact score, the generated law does not depend on the schedule; the schedule changes how training samples (uniform in $\tau$) and sampler steps are distributed over SNR levels.

Proof. (a) For $\tau\in(0,1)$, $\partial_\gamma[\tau^{\gamma+1}/(\gamma+1)]=\tau^{\gamma+1}[\log\tau-1/(\gamma+1)]/(\gamma+1)\lt0$, and at $\tau=1$ the term is $1/(\gamma+1)$, also decreasing. $B$ is increasing in $\tau$, so a smaller $B$ at every $\tau$ reaches the level $c$ later. (b) Substitute the stated $\beta_{\max}$ into (5.10); for $\tau\in(0,1)$, $\tau^{\gamma+1}$ decreases in $\gamma$. (c) $B(\tau)\ge\beta_{\min}\tau$, so $B(\tau_s)=c$ forces $\tau_s\le c/\beta_{\min}$. (d) The reverse SDE and probability-flow ODE with the exact score reproduce the forward marginals for any admissible schedule (MIT chapter 3, Section 3.8); at $\tau=0$ that marginal is the data law. A uniform training draw of $\tau$ places mode $\boldsymbol\kappa$ in its signal-dominated regime with probability $\tau_s(\boldsymbol\kappa)$. ∎

The program evaluates this with illustrative values chosen here, $\beta_{\min}=0.1$ and $B_1=10.05$ (which is $\beta_{\max}=20$ at $\gamma=1$), on the dissipation-range model of Section 5.7. Raising $\gamma$ from 1 to 5 at fixed $B_1$ lengthens the survival time of shell 16 from 0.150 to 0.515 and of shell 4 from 0.722 to 0.897, but shell 32 survives only to $\tau\approx0.003$ to $0.004$ for every $\gamma$, as the bound $\log(1+3.6\times10^{-4})/0.1=3.6\times10^{-3}$ of part (c) requires; that bound moves only with $\beta_{\min}$, and at $\beta_{\min}=0.01$ it is ten times larger. At fixed $\beta_{\max}=20$ instead, the terminal $\bar\alpha_1$ rises from $4.3\times10^{-5}$ to $3.3\times10^{-2}$, which is the price of part (a). The paper does not state its $\beta_{\min}$ and $\beta_{\max}$, so these values are not its own. It sweeps $\gamma$ over $[1.0,7.0]$ (its Section 3.1.4; its Section 4.3 gives $[2.5,7.0]$) and reports on its first system a relative spectral error of 10.2 % at $\gamma=5.0$ against 96.3 % for the linear-schedule DDPM, then 1470 % and 1476 % at $\gamma=6.0$ and $7.0$, which it calls a training instability and attributes to the discrete DDPM formulation. The rate $\beta$ near $\tau=1$ in (5.10) grows like $\gamma+1$ when $B_1$ is held fixed (the program's $\beta_{\max}$ of 20.00, 39.90 and 59.80), which is consistent with that attribution and does not test it.

Mitigation 2: one-step "lazy" distillation. Fix one intermediate noise level $\tau^*$; the paper uses $\tau^*=0.5$. A new network $F_\phi$, initialized from the pretrained score model's parameters, is trained on pairs $(x_{\tau^*},x_0)$ built with the closed-form forward process, using the loss below (the paper's Section 3.5 names the root-mean-squared error as the training loss, which has the same minimizer when the root is taken of the population mean, and in the Gaussian model used below):

$$\mathcal L_{\rm lazy}(\phi)=\mathbb E\,\|F_\phi(x_{\tau^*},X(t_{\mathrm f}))-x_0\|^2,\tag{5.11}$$

and a forecast is one evaluation of $F_\phi$. The input of that evaluation is not determined by the paper. Its Section 3.2.4 describes forecasting in two steps: "we generate a corrupted sample at the chosen noise level", by the same display as in training, $x_{\tau^*}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{\tau^*}}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{\tau^*}}\epsilon$, and then $\hat X(t+\Delta t)=F_\phi(x_{\tau^*},X(t))$. That display needs $x_0$, the quantity being forecast, and the text names no replacement. The retraining panel of its Figure 1 labels the network input $\mathbf u_t$, the current state, and draws no noise field, whereas the base-model panel beside it draws one. The released code (commit c004897 of 2026-03-14) contains the score training and a 1000-step reverse-SDE rollout, and no lazy retraining or one-step sampler. Its only text on the matter is an unimplemented note in GulfOfMexico/losses.py, "Add cold/lazy diffusion" and "Just hard sample from the prior at diffusion T = 1, no other changes", with commented-out lines in both train.py files that fix the noise level at 1.0 for "cold diffusion". That note describes pure noise at $\tau=1$, not the paper's $\tau^*=0.5$. The three sources therefore give three different descriptions, none establishes what a forecast feeds $F_\phi$ in the reported runs, and this chapter does not supply a replacement.

Three consequences can still be stated, each under its own assumption. First, the population minimizer of (5.11) is the conditional mean $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_{\tau^*},X(t_{\mathrm f})]$, a point estimate, not a draw: its forecasts can vary only through randomness in its input. Second, in a Gaussian model where the conditioning state predicts part of each mode, let $\hat S_c(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ be the conditional modal variance of $x_0$ given $X(t_{\mathrm f})$ and $\mathrm{SNR}_c=\bar\alpha_{\tau^*}\hat S_c/((1-\bar\alpha_{\tau^*})\hat S_\xi)$. Applying Proposition 5.7 to the unpredictable part gives modal output power $\hat S-\hat S_c/(1+\mathrm{SNR}_c)$ on the training inputs (Exercise 5.5): the deficit is the unpredictable variance attenuated by the per-mode SNR at $\tau^*$. Those inputs contain $x_0$, so this formula describes the training distribution, not a forecast. Third, take the input $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{\tau^*}}\,\mathbb E[x_0\mid X(t_{\mathrm f})]+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{\tau^*}}\,\boldsymbol\xi$, which has the training noise level and carries no information about $x_0$ beyond $X(t_{\mathrm f})$. The same minimizer then has modal power $\hat S-\hat S_c+\hat S_c\mathrm{SNR}_c/(1+\mathrm{SNR}_c)^2$ (Exercise 5.5), so at most one quarter of the unpredictable variance returns, at $\mathrm{SNR}_c=1$. The bound belongs to this noise level: the output power grows with the square of the noise coefficient of the input. In the limit $\bar\alpha_{\tau^*}\to0$ the input is pure noise, as in the code note above, and the modal power is $\hat S-\hat S_c$. A trained network evaluated away from its training distribution need not equal this minimizer, so the third formula is a Gaussian calculation, not a prediction for the paper's network.

The fixed level interacts with the schedule. With this section's illustrative $\beta_{\min}=0.1$ and $\beta_{\max}=20$, (5.10) gives $B(0.5)=0.05+19.9\cdot0.25/2=2.54$ and $\bar\alpha_{0.5}=0.079$ at $\gamma=1$, and $B(0.5)=0.05+19.9\cdot0.0156/6=0.102$ and $\bar\alpha_{0.5}=0.90$, a scalar SNR of 9.3, at $\gamma=5$. In the second case a training input holds $x_0$ with little corruption, which is why the unstated forecast input decides what the training loss implies for forecasting. The paper reports for its $\gamma=5.0$ lazy variant a relative spectral error of 22.7 % on its first system, against 10.2 % for the corresponding score model and 96.3 % for the linear-schedule DDPM, at three orders of magnitude lower inference cost. It also states that lazy diffusion "cannot compensate for deficiencies in the learned geometry of the conditional distribution"; that sentence concerns errors inherited from the base score model, a different mechanism from the Gaussian attenuation above. Because the forecast input is not established, the 22.7 % does not test either formula.

5.10 A protocol for evaluating generated turbulence with physical statistics

The analysis above says where to look: low wavenumbers are the last to fail, gradients and dissipation the first, and every metric has a sampling floor. The protocol below turns those facts into a procedure. Each step names what its statistic can show and what it cannot.

  1. Fix the target and two disjoint reference sets. Use states after the transient, spaced by more than the integral time of the slowest statistic you will report (Proposition 5.1), from geometries or time windows held out from training. Split them into two disjoint sets $\mathcal D_1,\mathcal D_2$ of the same size as the generated set. Every metric below is reported as the pair (generated vs $\mathcal D_1$, $\mathcal D_2$ vs $\mathcal D_1$); a generated value inside the reference-to-reference spread is not evidence of an error.
  2. Generate with several seeds. Train at least three seeds (TurbDiff used three) and draw the same number of samples per condition as references. Report the spread across seeds separately from the spread across samples.
  3. One-point statistics. Compare the mean field, $k=\tfrac12\langle\mathbf v'\cdot\mathbf v'\rangle$, and the PDFs of each velocity component, including the tails. These test amplitude and intermittency but are dominated by the energy-containing scales; a 5 % TKE deficit can coexist with a missing dissipation range.
  4. Energy spectrum. Compute $E(\kappa)$ by shell summation with the course convention and check Parseval, $\sum_n E_n=k$ (chapter 3). For non-periodic cutouts, apply a window and state it. TurbDiff's three $48^3$ cubes serve a different purpose, equal resolution along each direction; they are not periodic, and its released code applies no window. Plot the compensated spectrum $\kappa^{5/3}E$ and the per-shell log ratio $\ell_n=\log(E_{{\rm gen},n}/E_{{\rm ref},n})$.
  5. Gradient statistics. Compare $2\nu\sum_n\kappa_n^2E_n$ (a dissipation proxy, or its LES analogue for resolved gradients) and the PDFs of vorticity or velocity-gradient components. These are the statistics that fail first under spectral collapse (Section 5.7).

5.11 Where does a generated spectrum leave the reference?

"Spectral collapse" needs an operational definition that separates a genuine departure from sampling scatter. The scatter of a shell estimate can be computed for a Gaussian field. Let shell $n$ contain $M_n$ grid wavevectors and let $\hat S$ be approximately constant across it.

Proposition 5.10 — scatter of a shell estimate. For a real Gaussian homogeneous field and a shell without self-conjugate modes, the estimate $\hat E_n=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa\in n}|\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2$ has relative standard deviation $\sqrt{2/M_n}$. The difference of the logarithms of two independent such estimates has standard deviation approximately $2/\sqrt{M_n}$ when $M_n$ is large.

Proof. The $M_n$ wavevectors form $M_n/2$ conjugate pairs; each pair contributes $2|\hat z_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2$, and different pairs are independent (Proposition 5.3 with Gaussianity). A circular complex Gaussian has $|\hat z|^2$ exponentially distributed, with standard deviation equal to its mean. The sum of $M_n/2$ independent identically distributed terms has relative standard deviation $(M_n/2)^{-1/2}$. For small relative fluctuations, $\log\hat E_n$ has standard deviation approximately equal to the relative one, and independent errors add in variance, giving $\sqrt2\sqrt{2/M_n}$. ∎

Define the tolerance $\delta_n=3\cdot2/\sqrt{M_n}$, three standard deviations of the log ratio, and the departure wavenumber as the smallest shell $n_d$ such that $|\ell_n|\gt\delta_n$ for every shell from $n_d$ up to the highest shell tested. The definition is two-sided on purpose. A posterior-mean failure produces a deficit, but a sampler that leaves residual noise produces an excess; the Lazy Diffusion paper reports excess high-wavenumber power for its linear-schedule DDPM in one system and energy deficits at intermediate and small scales in the other (read in the arXiv HTML). Requiring the departure to persist to the top shell separates collapse, which is monotone in $\kappa$ by Proposition 5.5, from an isolated outlier shell. On the $64^3$ grid shell 1 has $M_1=18$ and $\delta_1=1.41$ (a factor of four in power), while shell 31 has $M_{31}=12{,}146$ and $\delta_{31}=0.054$: the detector is weakest at the large scales, which is where collapse occurs last.

For a posterior-mean output, $\ell_n\approx\log H_n=-\log(1+1/\mathrm{SNR}_n)$, so the detector fires once $\log(1+1/\mathrm{SNR}_n)\gt\delta_n$, roughly once $\mathrm{SNR}_n\lt1/\delta_n=\sqrt{M_n}/6$. Because $\sqrt{M_n}/6$ exceeds one at mid shells, the departure wavenumber lies below $\kappa_\times$: the program finds $n_d=10$ while the first shell with $\mathrm{SNR}\lt1$ is 14, and the independent reference pair has no shell outside tolerance. In the same experiment the TKE ratio is 0.9506, the gradient-variance ratio 0.7716, and the log-spectrum distance over shells 1–31 is 19.9 against 0.692 for the reference pair. The three statistics rank the damage in the order Section 5.7 predicts.

Exercise 5.1 — A stationary flow that defeats the ergodic target. For the process of Worked example 5.1, compute the autocovariance, the limit of $\bar G_T$, and $\mathrm{Var}(\bar G_T)$ for large $T$. Explain which hypothesis of Proposition 5.1 fails and what a generative model trained on one run learns.

Solution

Independence of $Q$ and $w$ and $\mathbb E Q=0$, $\mathbb EQ^2=1$ give $C(h)=a^2+C_w(h)$. The time average is $aQ+\bar w_T$; by Proposition 5.1 applied to $w$, $\bar w_T\to0$ in mean square, so $\bar G_T\to aQ$. Hence $\mathrm{Var}(\bar G_T)\to\mathrm{Var}(aQ)=a^2$, which does not decay with $T$, whereas (5.2) would require decay like $1/T$. The failing hypothesis is integrability of $C$: it is $a^2\ne0$ plus an integrable function. A model trained on snapshots of one run learns the law of $X$ given the realized $Q$; the ensemble law (5.1) is the equal mixture of the two conditional laws and requires runs in both states or a record long enough to switch.

Exercise 5.2 — Translating TurbDiff's schedule to the course clock. Using Proposition 5.4, find the course times $t$ that match scalar SNR $10^3$ and $10^{-5}$. With $N_s=500$ steps and log-SNR linear in the step index, by how much does $\log_{10}$SNR change per step, and how many steps have scalar SNR above one?

Solution

The course path has scalar SNR $r=t^2/(1-t)^2$, so $t/(1-t)=\sqrt r$ and $t=\sqrt r/(1+\sqrt r)$. For $r=10^3$, $\sqrt r=31.62$ and $t=0.9693$; for $r=10^{-5}$, $\sqrt r=3.162\times10^{-3}$ and $t=3.152\times10^{-3}$. The decimal logarithm runs from $3$ to $-5$, a change of 8 over 499 intervals, or $0.01603$ per step. SNR exceeds one while $\log_{10}r\gt0$, i.e. for the first $3/0.01603\approx187$ intervals, about 188 of the 500 levels counted from the clean end. Per-mode SNRs are these values times $N^3\hat S(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ (times the data variance when it is not one), so a mode with relative power $10^{-2}$ is signal-dominated only on the $(3-2)/0.01603\approx62$ cleanest intervals.

Exercise 5.4 — A sharp collapse that TKE barely sees. Suppose $E(\kappa)=C\kappa^{-5/3}$ on $1\le\kappa\le32$ and a generated field has $E_{\rm gen}=E$ for $\kappa\le16$ and $E_{\rm gen}=0$ above. Compute the fractions of TKE and of the dissipation proxy $\int\kappa^2E\,d\kappa$ that are lost, and state what the log-spectrum distance of Eq. 7 does.

Solution

TKE is $\int\kappa^{-5/3}d\kappa=\tfrac32[\kappa^{-2/3}]$ between limits. Lost fraction $=(16^{-2/3}-32^{-2/3})/(1-32^{-2/3})=(0.1575-0.0992)/0.9008=0.0647$, about 6.5 %. The dissipation proxy is $\int\kappa^{1/3}d\kappa=\tfrac34[\kappa^{4/3}]$; lost fraction $=(32^{4/3}-16^{4/3})/(32^{4/3}-1)=(101.59-40.32)/100.59=0.609$, about 61 %. Exercise 5.5 — Lazy distillation with an informative conditioning state. In one Fourier mode let $x_0=m+u$, where $m=\mathbb E[x_0\mid X(t_{\mathrm f})]$ and the unpredictable part $u$ is Gaussian with variance $\hat S_c$, independent of $X(t_{\mathrm f})$ and of the noise. Show that the minimizer of (5.11) has modal power $\hat S-\hat S_c/(1+\mathrm{SNR}_c)$ with $\mathrm{SNR}_c$ as in Section 5.9, and interpret the two limits $\hat S_c\to0$ and $\hat S_c=\hat S$. Then evaluate the same minimizer on the input $\tilde x=\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,m+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\hat\xi$, which carries no information about $u$, and compute its modal power.

Solution

Given $X(t_{\mathrm f})$, $m$ is known, so $y=x_{\tau^*}-\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,m=\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,u+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\hat\xi$ carries all remaining information about $u$. Proposition 5.7 with signal variance $\hat S_c$ gives $\mathbb E[u\mid y]$ of power $H_c\hat S_c$, $H_c=\mathrm{SNR}_c/(1+\mathrm{SNR}_c)$, and the minimizer is $F^*=m+\mathbb E[u\mid y]$. Since $m$ and $\mathbb E[u\mid y]$ are uncorrelated (the second has zero mean given $X(t_{\mathrm f})$), its power is $(\hat S-\hat S_c)+H_c\hat S_c=\hat S-\hat S_c/(1+\mathrm{SNR}_c)$, using $\hat S=\mathbb E|m|^2+\hat S_c$. If $\hat S_c\to0$ the conditioning state determines the mode and nothing is lost: large scales over a short forecast step. If $\hat S_c=\hat S$ the conditioning state is uninformative and the deficit is that of Proposition 5.7 at $\tau^*$: small scales that decorrelate within one step. For the second input, $\mathbb E[u\mid y]=c\,y$ with $c=\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\hat S_c/(\bar\alpha\hat S_c+(1-\bar\alpha)\hat S_\xi)$, and on $\tilde x$ the argument is $y=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\hat\xi$. The output $m+c\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\hat\xi$ has power $(\hat S-\hat S_c)+c^2(1-\bar\alpha)\hat S_\xi$, and substituting $\mathrm{SNR}_c$ gives $c^2(1-\bar\alpha)\hat S_\xi=\hat S_c\mathrm{SNR}_c/(1+\mathrm{SNR}_c)^2$. The function $x/(1+x)^2$ has maximum $1/4$ at $x=1$, so at most one quarter of $\hat S_c$ returns. The training input gives $H_c\hat S_c$ in the same place, larger by the factor $1+\mathrm{SNR}_c$.

Exercise 5.6 — The schedule bound. With $\beta_{\min}=0.1$ and the model's relative modal power $N^3\hat S=3.602\times10^{-4}$ at shell 32, compute the bound of Proposition 5.9(c). For $\gamma=1$ and $B_1=10.05$, solve $B(\tau_s)=c$ exactly and compare with the program's 0.003 and 0.004.

Solution

$c=\log(1+3.602\times10^{-4})=3.601\times10^{-4}$, so $\tau_s\le c/\beta_{\min}=3.60\times10^{-3}$ for every $\gamma$. For $\gamma=1$, $B(\tau)=0.1\tau+9.95\tau^2$. A reader concludes from Table 1 that DilResNet-22 is the best generative model of the steady state because its $W_{2,\rm TKE}$ (2.15) and $W_{2,\mathcal R}$ (1.240) are lower than TurbDiff's (3.9 and 1.38). Identify two errors in this reading and say what additional number would make the comparison fair.

Solution

First, DilResNet-22 is not generating the steady state: it starts from a state the solver has already produced after its roughly 10-minute transition, a sample of the distribution it is asked to sample, and its 22 steps cover only 2.2 ms; the model that must generate turbulence from the initial condition, DilResNet-init, scores $60\pm47$ and $4.6\times10^8$. The two rows answer different questions. Second, $W_{2,\rm TKE}$ is a matching distance between two sets of 16 states with Eq. 7 as ground distance, and Eq. 7 compares log spectra of the cell TKE field, not the course's energy spectrum. Two independent finite sets from one distribution do not coincide, so that matching distance is positive for them, and the absolute value cannot be read without the same distance between two independent sets of solver samples. The missing number is that reference-to-reference value of each metric (step 1 of the protocol), together with the wall-clock cost to first sample, which the table supplies as 20.63 s for TurbDiff against $602+1.58$ s for DilResNet-22, of which 602 s is the solver.

What this chapter establishes. A marginal generative model of turbulence has a single target only if the flow is past its transient, its forcing is stationary and the long-time distribution does not depend on the initial state, which is assumed and not proved; Proposition 5.1 gives the variance of a time average of a statistic whose correlations decay. Such a model trades all multi-time information for freedom from error accumulation. For any Gaussian noising path, each Fourier mode has $\mathrm{SNR}=t^2\hat S/((1-t)^2\hat S_\xi)$ in the course clock (Propositions 5.3–5.4); the crossover wavenumber moves as $(t/(1-t))^{2/\lambda}$ with $\lambda=11/3$ for 3D inertial-range turbulence (Proposition 5.5); the dissipation range is signal-dominated for an exponentially short window (Proposition 5.6); a mean-square one-step estimator attenuates each mode by $\mathrm{SNR}/(1+\mathrm{SNR})$ (Proposition 5.7), and weak modes supply only second-order training signal (Proposition 5.8). The power-law schedule lengthens survival times but cannot rescue modes below the $\beta_{\min}$ bound and, at fixed $\beta_{\max}$, degrades the terminal law (Proposition 5.9).

What it does not establish. Nothing here proves that a trained network collapses: an exact sampler does not, and the size of a learned model's error at weak modes is an empirical question. The Gaussian calculations bound what a mean predictor loses; turbulent non-Gaussianity can let nonlinear posteriors keep more. TurbDiff's and Lazy Diffusion's numbers are quoted, not reproduced. The input of Lazy Diffusion's one-step forecast is not established by its paper or its released code (Section 5.9), so no formula here predicts that method's forecast spectrum. The mode-by-mode results assume a homogeneous field, which TurbDiff's channel and Lazy Diffusion's two systems are not.
