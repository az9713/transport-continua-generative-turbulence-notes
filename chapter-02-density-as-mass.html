2. Probability density as transported mass

A generative flow moves samples of noise along the trajectories of a velocity field $u_t$, and a likelihood-based model must then say what probability density the moved samples have. Continuum mechanics answers the same question for mass: a material element keeps its mass while its volume changes by the factor $J=\det\mathbf F$, so density times $J$ is constant along each particle. This chapter proves that probability obeys the same law in any dimension $d$. Along a trajectory $\psi_t(x_0)$ of a sufficiently regular field, $p_t(\psi_t(x_0))\,J_t(x_0)=p_0(x_0)$, and equivalently $\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(\psi_t(x_0))=-\operatorname{div}u_t(\psi_t(x_0))$. The second form turns density evaluation into a one-dimensional integral of the divergence, which is how continuous normalizing flows compute likelihoods. The chapter also identifies where the identities stop holding: at zero-density regions, at kinks and jumps, at non-Lipschitz fields, and at the singular endpoint of the straight conditional path that week 6 uses for flow matching.

From the mass identity $\rho J=\rho_0$ to a likelihood integral. Jacobi's formula in $d$ dimensions gives $\dot J_t=J_t\operatorname{div}u_t$. The pushforward of a density gives $p_tJ_t=p_0$, and the logarithm of that identity is the log-density ODE. The ODE is shown to be equivalent to the Eulerian continuity equation for smooth densities and to a weak form that survives jumps. Two exact examples follow, a linear field carrying a Gaussian and the straight conditional path, and then the likelihood integral, Hutchinson's trace estimator, the failure cases, and a program that checks the log-density ODE against the exact Gaussian.

2.1 Two traditions state one conservation law under different names

The continuum chapters of weeks 1–3 and the flow chapter of week 4 describe the same mathematical object from opposite ends. IITM chapter 10 follows a material region, proves $\dot J=J\operatorname{div}\mathbf v$ in three dimensions as its equation (10.4), and obtains the Lagrangian mass identity $\rho(\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf X,t),t)J(\mathbf X,t)=\rho_0(\mathbf X)$ as (10.8). Kanpur chapter 4 starts from a fixed control volume, derives $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)=0$ as (4.16), rewrites it in (4.17) as $D\rho/Dt+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0$, which it reads as $\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=-(1/\rho)\,D\rho/Dt$, and shows that the differential form fails where density jumps. MIT chapter 1 builds a flow map $\psi_t$ from a velocity field under Lipschitz hypotheses and pushes a law forward with it, while MIT chapter 2, read in week 6, derives the continuity equation $\partial_tp_t=-\operatorname{div}(p_tu_t)$ by a one-dimensional flux balance and states the $d$-dimensional form by analogy.

What none of these chapters supplies is the bridge between them. The determinant rate is proved only for $d=3$, and the Lagrangian identity for probability, $\frac{d}{dt}[p_t(\psi_t)D_t]=0$ in MIT's notation, is derived only for $d=1$. The equation $\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(\psi_t(x_0))=-\operatorname{div}u_t$, the form a likelihood computation uses, is never written. This chapter proves these results once, for every $d$, under hypotheses stated in advance.

One collision must be recorded: MIT chapter 2 §2.5 writes $J_t$ for probability flux, whereas this course reserves $J$ for $\det\mathbf F$ and writes the flux as $p_tu_t$.

2.2 Which velocity fields carry a density?

Let $d\ge1$ be the state dimension and let $\|\cdot\|$ be the Euclidean norm on $\mathbb R^d$ and the induced operator norm on $d\times d$ matrices. A velocity field is a map $u:[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$, written $u_t(x)$. Its spatial Jacobian $\nabla u_t(x)$ is the matrix with entries $(\nabla u_t)_{ij}=\partial u_{t,i}/\partial x_j$, the component-first convention of IITM's $\mathbf L$, and its divergence is the trace $\operatorname{div}u_t=\sum_{i=1}^d\partial u_{t,i}/\partial x_i=\operatorname{tr}\nabla u_t$. All results in this chapter use the following hypotheses.

Hypotheses (H). The field $u$ is continuous on $[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d$; the Jacobian $\nabla u_t(x)$ exists and is continuous on $[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d$; and $L=\sup_{t,x}\|\nabla u_t(x)\|$ is finite. Here $L$ is a number, the Lipschitz constant of MIT chapter 1, not IITM's tensor $\mathbf L$.

These hypotheses imply those of MIT chapter 1 Proposition 1.1. Integrating the Jacobian along the segment from $y$ to $x$ gives $u_t(x)-u_t(y)=\int_0^1\nabla u_t(y+s(x-y))(x-y)\,ds$, so $\|u_t(x)-u_t(y)\|\le L\|x-y\|$; and $\sup_t\|u_t(0)\|$ is finite because $u$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,1]\times\{0\}$. Proposition 1.1 then gives, for each initial state $x_0$, a unique solution of $\partial_t\psi_t(x_0)=u_t(\psi_t(x_0))$, $\psi_0(x_0)=x_0$, on all of $[0,1]$. The reversed-time argument of MIT chapter 1 §1.3 shows that each $\psi_t$ is a bijection of $\mathbb R^d$, with inverse given by solving the same ODE backward from time $t$.

Differentiability of $\psi_t$ in $x_0$ is a further property of ODEs, which uniqueness of solutions does not give. The change of variables in Section 2.4 needs it, so it is proved here.

Lemma 2.0 — differentiable dependence on initial data. Under (H), the map $(t,x_0)\mapsto\psi_t(x_0)$ is continuously differentiable. Its spatial Jacobian $\mathbf F_t(x_0)=\nabla\psi_t(x_0)$, with entries $\partial\psi_{t,i}/\partial x_{0,j}$, is continuously differentiable in $t$ and solves the variational equation

$$\dot{\mathbf F}_t(x_0)=\nabla u_t(\psi_t(x_0))\,\mathbf F_t(x_0),\qquad \mathbf F_0(x_0)=I_d.$$

Proof. Fix $x_0$, write $y_t=\psi_t(x_0)$ and $\mathbf M_t=\nabla u_t(y_t)$, and let $\mathbf F_t$ denote the solution of the displayed linear equation, which exists on $[0,1]$ because $\mathbf M_t$ is continuous in $t$ and bounded by $L$. Grönwall's inequality gives $\|\mathbf F_t\|\le e^{L}$. For $h\in\mathbb R^d$ with $\|h\|\le1$, set $\delta_t=\psi_t(x_0+h)-y_t$ and $w_t=\delta_t-\mathbf F_th$. MIT Proposition 1.1 gives $\|\delta_t\|\le e^{L}\|h\|$. Integrating the Jacobian along the segment from $y_t$ to $y_t+\delta_t$, as above, gives

$$\dot\delta_t=(\mathbf M_t+R_t(h))\,\delta_t,\qquad R_t(h)=\int_0^1\big[\nabla u_t(y_t+s\delta_t)-\mathbf M_t\big]\,ds,$$

and therefore $\dot w_t=\mathbf M_tw_t+R_t(h)\delta_t$ with $w_0=0$. The points $(t,y_t+s\delta_t)$ stay in the compact set of $(t,y)$ with $t\in[0,1]$ and $\|y-y_t\|\le e^{L}$, where $\nabla u$ is uniformly continuous, so $\omega(h)=\sup_t\|R_t(h)\|$ tends to zero as $h\to0$. Grönwall's inequality gives $\|w_t\|\le e^{2L}\omega(h)\|h\|$, so $\psi_t$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with derivative $\mathbf F_t$. For continuity in $x_0$, let $x_0'$ be a second initial state, with $\mathbf M_t'=\nabla u_t(\psi_t(x_0'))$ and $\mathbf F_t'$ the corresponding solution. Then $\mathbf G_t=\mathbf F_t'-\mathbf F_t$ solves $\dot{\mathbf G}_t=\mathbf M_t'\mathbf G_t+(\mathbf M_t'-\mathbf M_t)\mathbf F_t$ with $\mathbf G_0=0$, and $\mathbf M_t'\to\mathbf M_t$ uniformly in $t$ as $x_0'\to x_0$, by the same uniform continuity, so $\sup_t\|\mathbf G_t\|\to0$. Hence $\mathbf F_t(x_0)$ is continuous in $(t,x_0)$. The time derivative $\partial_t\psi_t(x_0)=u_t(\psi_t(x_0))$ is continuous in $(t,x_0)$ because $\psi$ is. All first partial derivatives are continuous, which proves the claim. ∎

The argument is the case $k=1$ in the proof of Theorem 2.10 of Teschl (PDF pp. 57–58). The statement of that theorem assumes $f\in C^k$ jointly in time and state, so it does not cover (H) as stated, and Lemma 2.0 is proved here for that reason. The steps above differentiate only in the state and use the time variable only through the continuity of $\nabla u$ in $(t,x)$.

The variational equation is what one obtains by differentiating $\partial_t\psi_t=u_t(\psi_t)$ with respect to $x_0$ and applying the chain rule; the proof shows that the result is the true Jacobian of $\psi_t$. It is the $d$-dimensional analog of IITM (9.10), $\dot{\mathbf F}=\mathbf L\mathbf F$, derived in IITM chapter 9 for a smooth motion. Applied to the reversed field, which also satisfies (H), Lemma 2.0 makes $\psi_t^{-1}$ continuously differentiable as well. Each $\psi_t$ is therefore a $C^1$ diffeomorphism of $\mathbb R^d$, and the multivariable change-of-variables formula applies to it.

2.3 Jacobi's formula holds in every dimension

For a $d\times d$ matrix $A$, let $C(A)$ be its cofactor matrix: $C_{kj}=(-1)^{k+j}\det A^{(kj)}$, where $A^{(kj)}$ is $A$ with row $k$ and column $j$ deleted. The adjugate is $\operatorname{adj}A=C(A)^{\mathsf T}$, and Laplace expansion along row $k$ reads $\det A=\sum_jA_{kj}C_{kj}$. The identity $A\operatorname{adj}A=(\det A)I_d$ follows from the same expansion, because expanding along row $k$ with the entries of a different row $i$ computes the determinant of a matrix with two equal rows, which is zero.

Proposition 2.1 — Jacobi's formula. Let $t\mapsto A(t)$ be a continuously differentiable path of $d\times d$ matrices. Then

$$\frac{d}{dt}\det A(t)=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\operatorname{adj}A(t)\,\dot A(t)\big),$$

and if $A(t)$ is invertible, $\frac{d}{dt}\det A=\det A\,\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}\dot A)$.

Proof. The Leibniz expansion $\det A=\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^dA_{i\sigma(i)}$, summed over permutations $\sigma$ of $\{1,\dots,d\}$, is a polynomial in the entries. The product rule applied to each term differentiates one factor at a time. Collecting the terms in which row $k$ is differentiated gives the determinant of $A$ with row $k$ replaced by $\dot A$'s row $k$, so

$$\frac{d}{dt}\det A=\sum_{k=1}^d\det A_{[k]},\qquad \det A_{[k]}=\sum_{j=1}^d\dot A_{kj}C_{kj}(A).$$

The second equality is Laplace expansion along the replaced row; the cofactors of that row do not involve row $k$, so they are those of $A$. Summing over $k$ gives $\sum_{k,j}\dot A_{kj}C_{kj}=\sum_{k,j}(C^{\mathsf T})_{jk}\dot A_{kj}=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}A\,\dot A)$. If $A$ is invertible, $\operatorname{adj}A=(\det A)A^{-1}$ by the identity above. ∎

The adjugate form does not require invertibility. This matters when a flow degenerates, as the conditional path of Section 2.9 does at $t=1$, and Exercise 2.1 evaluates the formula at a singular matrix. The invertible form is the one IITM uses in three dimensions, where it proves (10.4) by differentiating one column at a time.

Corollary 2.2 — the volume factor. Under (H), $J_t(x_0)=\det\mathbf F_t(x_0)$ satisfies

$$\dot J_t(x_0)=J_t(x_0)\,\operatorname{div}u_t(\psi_t(x_0)),\qquad J_t(x_0)=\exp\!\Big(\int_0^t\operatorname{div}u_s(\psi_s(x_0))\,ds\Big)\gt0.$$

Proof. Fix $x_0$ and abbreviate $\mathbf F_t=\mathbf F_t(x_0)$ and $\mathbf M_t=\nabla u_t(\psi_t(x_0))$. Proposition 2.1 with the variational equation gives $\dot J_t=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}\mathbf F_t\,\mathbf M_t\mathbf F_t)=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf F_t\operatorname{adj}\mathbf F_t\,\mathbf M_t)=J_t\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M_t$, using the cyclic property of the trace and $\mathbf F\operatorname{adj}\mathbf F=(\det\mathbf F)I_d$. This holds without assuming invertibility. The function $s\mapsto\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M_s$ is continuous, so the scalar linear equation $\dot J=J\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M$ with $J_0=\det I_d=1$ has the unique solution displayed; it is positive because it is an exponential. ∎

Positivity is now a theorem rather than an assumption. IITM assumes $J\gt0$ for an orientation-preserving motion; here it follows from $J_0=1$ and the continuity of $\operatorname{div}u$. The formula also gives two-sided bounds. Since $|\operatorname{tr}\mathbf M|\le\sum_i|e_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf Me_i|\le d\|\mathbf M\|$ for the standard basis vectors $e_i$, hypothesis (H) gives $e^{-dLt}\le J_t(x_0)\le e^{dLt}$ for every $x_0$. Section 2.11 uses this bound.

2.4 Probability per unit volume obeys the mass identity

Let $p_0$ be a probability density on $\mathbb R^d$, a nonnegative integrable function with $\int p_0\,dx=1$, and let $X_0$ be a random vector with density $p_0$. Define $X_t=\psi_t(X_0)$. MIT chapter 1 §1.5 calls the law of $X_t$ the pushforward of the law of $X_0$ and computes its density for a scalar affine map. The general statement is the probabilistic form of IITM (10.8).

Proposition 2.3 — change of variables along the flow. Under (H), $X_t$ has the density

$$p_t(x)=\frac{p_0(\psi_t^{-1}(x))}{J_t(\psi_t^{-1}(x))},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad p_t(\psi_t(x_0))\,J_t(x_0)=p_0(x_0)\quad\text{for all }x_0.$$

In particular $\int p_t\,dx=1$ for every $t$.

Proof. For a Borel set $\mathcal B\subset\mathbb R^d$, the event $X_t\in\mathcal B$ equals the event $X_0\in\psi_t^{-1}(\mathcal B)$, so $\Pr(X_t\in\mathcal B)=\int_{\psi_t^{-1}(\mathcal B)}p_0(x_0)\,dx_0$. Because $\psi_t$ is a $C^1$ diffeomorphism with positive Jacobian determinant (Section 2.2 and Corollary 2.2), the substitution $x=\psi_t(x_0)$ has $dx=J_t(x_0)\,dx_0$ and maps $\psi_t^{-1}(\mathcal B)$ onto $\mathcal B$. Hence

$$\Pr(X_t\in\mathcal B)=\int_{\mathcal B}\frac{p_0(\psi_t^{-1}(x))}{J_t(\psi_t^{-1}(x))}\,dx.$$

The integrand is therefore a density of $X_t$. Taking $\mathcal B=\mathbb R^d$ gives total probability one. The second form is the first evaluated at $x=\psi_t(x_0)$. ∎

A density is determined only up to sets of zero volume. From here on $p_t$ denotes the particular version defined by the formula, which is defined at every point where $p_0$ is defined; every pointwise statement below refers to this version. The interpretation is the one IITM gives for mass. The probability in a small region around $x_0$ is $p_0(x_0)\,dV_0$; the flow carries that region to a region of volume $J_t\,dV_0$ around $\psi_t(x_0)$ and keeps its probability, so the density there is $p_0/J_t$. The one difference from (10.8) is normalization: $\rho_0$ may have any total mass, while $p_0$ integrates to one, and Proposition 2.3 shows that the flow keeps it so.

2.5 The logarithm of density changes at minus the divergence

Proposition 2.4 — the log-density ODE. Assume (H), let $p_t$ be given by Proposition 2.3, and fix $x_0$ with $p_0(x_0)\gt0$. Then $\ell_t=\log p_t(\psi_t(x_0))$ is continuously differentiable in $t$, and

$$\frac{d\ell_t}{dt}=-\operatorname{div}u_t(\psi_t(x_0)),\qquad \log p_t(\psi_t(x_0))=\log p_0(x_0)-\int_0^t\operatorname{div}u_s(\psi_s(x_0))\,ds.$$

Proof. Proposition 2.3 gives $p_t(\psi_t(x_0))=p_0(x_0)/J_t(x_0)$, which is positive, so $\ell_t=\log p_0(x_0)-\log J_t(x_0)$. Corollary 2.2 gives $\log J_t(x_0)=\int_0^t\operatorname{div}u_s(\psi_s(x_0))\,ds$, whose integrand is continuous in $s$. Both displayed statements follow. ∎

The proof uses no derivative of $p_0$. The identity holds along every trajectory that starts where $p_0$ is positive, even when $p_0$ is discontinuous, because it compares the density at the two ends of one trajectory and never at neighbouring points. The left side is a total derivative along the trajectory. It is not the partial derivative at a fixed state: if $p$ is differentiable, the chain rule gives $\frac{d\ell_t}{dt}=\partial_t\log p_t+u_t\cdot\nabla\log p_t$ evaluated at $\psi_t(x_0)$, the material derivative of $\log p$. Chen et al. write the same total derivative as $\partial\log p(z(t))/\partial t$ in their Theorem 1, where $z(t)$ is the moving state, the course's $\psi_t(x_0)$, and not a data point.

The mass analog is Kanpur's (4.17) rearranged, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=-(1/\rho)\,D\rho/Dt$, read as $D\log\rho/Dt=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf V$. A diverging flow spreads its contents over more volume, so the density of each element falls at the rate $\operatorname{div}u$, and a converging flow concentrates it. A divergence-free field therefore keeps $p_t(\psi_t(x_0))=p_0(x_0)$ exactly, which is Kanpur's incompressible flow: the density of each element is constant while the density at a fixed point may change. For generation this has a specific consequence. A divergence-free field can rearrange where the density values occur but cannot change the set of values, so it cannot map $\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ to $\mathcal N(0,4I_d)$; Exercise 2.2 proves this.

2.6 The Lagrangian identity and the continuity equation are equivalent for smooth densities

The Eulerian description asks how the density changes at a fixed state $x$. Kanpur derives the continuity equation from an integral balance over fixed regions; MIT chapter 2 derives it from flux through the ends of an interval. With the flow map available, it follows from the Lagrangian identity by one application of the product rule, and conversely.

Proposition 2.5 — Lagrangian and Eulerian forms. Assume (H). (a) If $p_t(x)$ from Proposition 2.3 is continuously differentiable in $(t,x)$, then $\partial_tp_t+\operatorname{div}(p_tu_t)=0$ on $[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d$. (b) Conversely, if $q$ is continuously differentiable in $(t,x)$, solves $\partial_tq_t+\operatorname{div}(q_tu_t)=0$, and has $q_0=p_0$, then $q_t=p_t$ for all $t$.

Proof. Let $q$ be any function continuously differentiable in $(t,x)$. Fix $x_0$ and write $y_t=\psi_t(x_0)$. The chain rule, $\dot y_t=u_t(y_t)$, and Corollary 2.2 give

$$\frac{d}{dt}\big[q_t(y_t)J_t(x_0)\big]=\big[\partial_tq_t+u_t\cdot\nabla q_t+q_t\operatorname{div}u_t\big](y_t)\,J_t(x_0)=\big[\partial_tq_t+\operatorname{div}(q_tu_t)\big](y_t)\,J_t(x_0),$$

where the last step is the product rule $\operatorname{div}(qu)=u\cdot\nabla q+q\operatorname{div}u$, valid because $u_t$ is differentiable. For (a), take $q=p$: by Proposition 2.3 the bracket on the left is the constant $p_0(x_0)$, so the right side is zero; since $J_t\gt0$, the continuity residual vanishes at $y_t$, and since $\psi_t$ is onto, it vanishes at every state. For (b), the right side is zero by assumption, so $q_t(\psi_t(x_0))J_t(x_0)=q_0(x_0)=p_0(x_0)$, which is the defining identity of $p_t$ in Proposition 2.3. ∎

The hypothesis of (a) is not automatic. The formula for $p_t$ contains $J_t\circ\psi_t^{-1}$, and $J_t$ involves first derivatives of $\psi_t$, so differentiating $p_t$ in $x$ requires second derivatives of $\psi_t$. Lemma 2.0 does not supply them. A sufficient condition, quoted from Teschl, is that $p_0$ is continuously differentiable and that $u$ is twice continuously differentiable in $(t,x)$ on an open set containing $[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d$: Theorem 2.10 and Theorem 6.1, applied with $t$ added as a state variable as in the proof of Theorem 2.10, then make $\psi_t$ and $\psi_t^{-1}$ twice continuously differentiable in $(t,x)$. The Lagrangian form of Proposition 2.4 needs neither. Dividing the continuity equation by a positive $p_t$ gives the material form $\partial_t\log p_t+u_t\cdot\nabla\log p_t=-\operatorname{div}u_t$, which is the first of IITM's three forms in (10.8) divided by $\rho$ and is the Eulerian expression of Proposition 2.4. The calculation in the proof is the $d$-dimensional version of MIT chapter 2's one-dimensional identity $\frac{d}{dt}[p_t(\psi_t)D_t]=0$.

2.7 The weak form survives density jumps

Kanpur chapter 4 §"The continuity equation from the integral form" observes that at an oil–water front the derivatives in (4.16) do not exist while the integral balance still holds. Probability densities have the same issue: a uniform density on a box has a jump at the boundary of the box. The weak form replaces derivatives of $p$ by derivatives of a smooth test function. Let $C^1_c$ denote the continuously differentiable functions $\varphi:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R$ that vanish outside a bounded set.

Proposition 2.6 — weak continuity equation. Assume (H) and let $X_0$ have any probability law, with or without a density. For every $\varphi\in C^1_c$, the function $t\mapsto\mathbb E[\varphi(X_t)]$ is continuously differentiable and

$$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\varphi(X_t)]=\mathbb E\big[\nabla\varphi(X_t)\cdot u_t(X_t)\big].$$

If $X_0$ has density $p_0$, this reads $\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi\,p_t\,dx=\int\nabla\varphi\cdot u_t\,p_t\,dx$; and if moreover $p$ is continuously differentiable, it is equivalent to $\partial_tp_t+\operatorname{div}(p_tu_t)=0$.

Proof. For fixed $x_0$, the chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dt}\varphi(\psi_t(x_0))=\nabla\varphi(\psi_t(x_0))\cdot u_t(\psi_t(x_0))$. Let $K$ be the bounded set outside which $\varphi$ vanishes, with closure $\bar K$. The gradient $\nabla\varphi$ vanishes outside $\bar K$, so this derivative is bounded in absolute value by $B=\sup_y\|\nabla\varphi(y)\|\cdot\sup_{s\in[0,1],\,y\in\bar K}\|u_s(y)\|$, which is finite because $u$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,1]\times\bar K$. By the mean value theorem, the difference quotients of $t\mapsto\varphi(X_t)$ are bounded by $B$, and dominated convergence allows differentiation under the expectation. The derivative is continuous in $t$ by the same domination. With a density, Proposition 2.3 converts each expectation into an integral against $p_t$. If $p$ is continuously differentiable, integration by parts moves the divergence onto $p_tu_t$ with no boundary term, because $\varphi$ has bounded support, giving $\int\varphi\,[\partial_tp_t+\operatorname{div}(p_tu_t)]\,dx=0$ for every $\varphi\in C^1_c$. A continuous function whose integral against every such $\varphi$ vanishes is zero, by the localization argument Kanpur uses for (4.16). ∎

This is the deterministic-field counterpart of MIT chapter 2's Proposition 2.1, which proves the same weak identity for the conditional-expectation field of a flow-matching bridge. The present version needs no density at all, so it covers a density with jumps, an empirical law made of finitely many atoms, and a Dirac initial law. It does not cover the endpoint of Section 2.9, because that field fails (H) at $t=1$. The converse question, whether the weak equation determines the law, has a positive answer for fields satisfying (H). If $\mu_t$, $t\in[0,1]$, are probability measures with $\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi\,d\mu_t=\int\nabla\varphi\cdot u_t\,d\mu_t$ for every $\varphi\in C^1_c$, then $\mu_t$ is the law of $\psi_t(X_0)$ with $X_0\sim\mu_0$. This uniqueness theorem is quoted here and not proved. Ambrosio and Crippa, Theorem 9 (PDF p. 9), states that uniqueness of ODE solutions from every point is equivalent to uniqueness of nonnegative measure-valued solutions of the continuity equation. Its proof uses their superposition principle, Theorem 12 (PDF p. 11), whose hypothesis $\int_0^T\!\int\|b_t\|/(1+\|x\|)\,d\mu_t\,dt\lt\infty$ holds with $b=u$ and $T=1$, because (H) gives $\|u_t(x)\|\le\sup_t\|u_t(0)\|+L\|x\|$. The linear growth matters: the field of Section 2.8 is unbounded, and their Remark 7 (PDF p. 8) records that the Lipschitz theory of their Section 2 still works under a local Lipschitz bound and this growth bound. The theorem is what licenses using the weak form as the defining equation when densities are not smooth.

2.8 A linear field carries a Gaussian exactly

Let $A:[0,1]\to\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ be continuous and set $u_t(x)=A(t)x$. Then $\nabla u_t=A(t)$ and $\operatorname{div}u_t=\operatorname{tr}A(t)$, independent of $x$, and (H) holds with $L=\max_t\|A(t)\|$. Let $\Phi(t)$ solve $\dot\Phi=A\Phi$, $\Phi(0)=I_d$; then $\psi_t(x_0)=\Phi(t)x_0$ and $\mathbf F_t=\Phi(t)$ for every $x_0$.

Proposition 2.7 — Gaussian transport by a linear field. If $p_0=\mathcal N(0,I_d)$, then $p_t=\mathcal N(0,\Sigma_t)$ with $\Sigma_t=\Phi(t)\Phi(t)^{\mathsf T}$, which solves $\dot\Sigma_t=A\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tA^{\mathsf T}$. Along every trajectory,

$$\log p_t(\Phi(t)x_0)=\log p_0(x_0)-\int_0^t\operatorname{tr}A(s)\,ds,\qquad J_t=\det\Phi(t)=\exp\!\Big(\int_0^t\operatorname{tr}A(s)\,ds\Big).$$

Proof. Corollary 2.2 gives $J_t$, which is positive, so $\Phi(t)$ is invertible. Proposition 2.3 gives $p_t(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}J_t^{-1}\exp(-\tfrac12\|\Phi^{-1}x\|^2)$. Since $\|\Phi^{-1}x\|^2=x^{\mathsf T}(\Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T})^{-1}x=x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_t^{-1}x$ and $\det\Sigma_t=J_t^2$, this is the density of $\mathcal N(0,\Sigma_t)$. Differentiating $\Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T}$ gives the covariance equation. The log-density identity is Proposition 2.4 with $\operatorname{div}u_s=\operatorname{tr}A(s)$. ∎

An independent check confirms Proposition 2.5 in this case. With $\log p_t(x)=c-\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma_t-\tfrac12x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_t^{-1}x$, Jacobi's formula and $\frac{d}{dt}\Sigma^{-1}=-\Sigma^{-1}\dot\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}$ give

$$\partial_t\log p_t+\operatorname{tr}A+u_t\cdot\nabla\log p_t=-\operatorname{tr}A+\tfrac12x^{\mathsf T}(\Sigma^{-1}A+A^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1})x+\operatorname{tr}A-x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}Ax=0,$$

because $x^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}x=x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}Ax$ for the symmetric $\Sigma^{-1}$. This computation differentiates the closed-form density at a fixed state and does not use the flow map.

For the program of Section 2.12, take $d=2$, $A(t)=g(t)A_0$ with $g(t)=1+0.8\sin(3\pi t)$ and

$$A_0=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&1.5\\0&-1.0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \operatorname{tr}A_0=-0.4.$$

Because the matrices $A(t)$ at different times are multiples of one matrix, they commute, and $\Phi(t)=\exp(G(t)A_0)$ with $G(t)=\int_0^tg=t+0.8(1-\cos3\pi t)/(3\pi)$. For an upper-triangular matrix with diagonal entries $a\ne b$ and corner entry $c$, $\exp(sA_0)$ has diagonal $e^{as},e^{bs}$ and corner $c(e^{as}-e^{bs})/(a-b)$, which one verifies by differentiating in $s$. At $t=1$, $G(1)=1+1.6/(3\pi)=1.169765$, $J_1=e^{-0.4\cdot1.169765}=0.626312$, and

$$\Phi(1)=\begin{pmatrix}2.017500&1.600369\\0&0.310440\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}6.631488&0.496818\\0.496818&0.096373\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det\Sigma_1=0.392267=J_1^2.$$

The field contracts volume on average, since $\int_0^1\operatorname{div}u_t\,dt=-0.467906$, so the density rises by the factor $e^{0.467906}$ along every trajectory, while the non-normal corner entry shears the Gaussian into an elongated ellipse. A field with $x$-independent divergence makes the log-density increment the same on every trajectory; the program therefore also checks the trajectory itself, because the exact density must be evaluated at the numerically computed endpoint.

2.9 The straight conditional path concentrates density without bound

Week 6 introduces flow matching through conditional paths, and the simplest one is already an exact example of the identities above. Fix a data point $z\in\mathbb R^d$ and Gaussian noise $\boldsymbol\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$, and set $X_t=tz+(1-t)\boldsymbol\xi$. The conditional flow is $\psi_t(\xi\mid z)=tz+(1-t)\xi$. Its time derivative at fixed $\xi$ is $z-\xi$, and expressing $\xi=(x-tz)/(1-t)$ through the current state gives the conditional velocity

$$u_t(x\mid z)=\frac{z-x}{1-t},\qquad \nabla u_t=-\frac{1}{1-t}I_d,\qquad \operatorname{div}u_t(x\mid z)=-\frac{d}{1-t},\qquad t\lt1.$$

For every $t_\star\lt1$, hypotheses (H) hold on $[0,t_\star]$ with $L=1/(1-t_\star)$, and all results above apply on that interval. Corollary 2.2 gives $J_t=\exp(-\int_0^td/(1-s)\,ds)=(1-t)^d$, which agrees with the determinant of the map $\xi\mapsto(1-t)\xi$. Proposition 2.4 then gives

$$\log p_t(\psi_t(\xi\mid z)\mid z)=\log p_0(\xi)-d\log(1-t),\qquad p_t(x\mid z)=\mathcal N\big(x;\,tz,\,(1-t)^2I_d\big).$$

The density along every trajectory rises without bound as $t\to1$, and the field's Lipschitz constant $1/(1-t)$ does too. At $t=1$ every trajectory reaches $z$, $J_1=0$, $\psi_1$ is not invertible, and the conditional law is the Dirac measure $\delta_z$, which has no density. Proposition 2.10 below shows that this singularity is forced: no field satisfying (H) on all of $[0,1]$ can turn a density into an atom. Proposition 2.6 does not apply on $[0,1]$, because $u_1(x\mid z)$ is undefined and reads $0/0$ at $x=z$. Along a sampled path the singular terms cancel: $u_t(X_t\mid z)=z-\boldsymbol\xi$ for $t\lt1$, so $\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\varphi(X_t)]=\mathbb E[\nabla\varphi(X_t)\cdot(z-\boldsymbol\xi)]$, and dominated convergence gives the limit $\nabla\varphi(z)\cdot z$ as $t\to1$. The endpoint is therefore a weak limit and not a case of Proposition 2.6. MIT chapter 2 §2.5 proves its weak identity on a horizon $t\le\tau$ with $\tau\lt1$ (its $\tau$ is not the course's noising clock) and states: "Singular endpoint limits require separate care". Integrating the divergence numerically with a uniform step also fails near the endpoint. With 64 RK4 steps on $[0,t]$, the program of Section 2.12 reproduces the exact rise $-2\log(1-t)$ for $d=2$ to $1.6\times10^{-6}$ at $t=0.9$, with error $0.0118$ at $t=0.99$, and with error $2.26$ (16.078 against 13.816) at $t=0.999$, where the integrand changes by a factor of about 17 within the last step.

2.10 Likelihood is a divergence integral along one trajectory

Course time runs from noise at $t=0$ to data at $t=1$. To evaluate the model density $p_1$ at a data point $x$, Proposition 2.4 is applied to the trajectory that ends at $x$.

Proposition 2.8 — likelihood by integrating the divergence. Assume (H) and $p_0\gt0$ everywhere. For every $x\in\mathbb R^d$, let $x(t)$ solve $\dot x(t)=u_t(x(t))$ on $[0,1]$ with terminal value $x(1)=x$, and let $r(t)$ solve $\dot r(t)=-\operatorname{div}u_t(x(t))$ with $r(1)=0$. Then

$$\log p_1(x)=\log p_0(x(0))-r(0),\qquad r(0)=\int_0^1\operatorname{div}u_s(x(s))\,ds.$$

Proof. The terminal-value problem has the unique solution $x(t)=\psi_t(x_0)$ with $x_0=\psi_1^{-1}(x)$, by the bijectivity in Section 2.2. Integrating the $r$ equation from $t=0$ to $t=1$ gives $r(0)=\int_0^1\operatorname{div}u_s(x(s))\,ds$. Proposition 2.4 at $t=1$, which applies since $p_0(x_0)\gt0$, completes the proof. ∎

In practice the pair $(x,r)$ is integrated backward in time from $t=1$ to $t=0$ by one ODE solve, and $\log p_0$ is evaluated in closed form at the end. This is the procedure Chen et al. introduce for continuous normalizing flows. Their Theorem 1 (Instantaneous Change of Variables), Section 4, equation (8) states $\partial\log p(z(t))/\partial t=-\operatorname{tr}(df/dz(t))$ under the hypothesis that the dynamics $f$ is "uniformly Lipschitz continuous in z and continuous in t", with the proof in their Appendix A. Grathwohl et al. write the backward solve as their equation (4), with the same time direction as the course ($t_0$ noise, $t_1$ data). Their equation (3), $\log p(z(t_1))=\log p(z(t_0))-\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\operatorname{Tr}(\partial f/\partial z(t))\,dt$, agrees with Proposition 2.8. Their equation (4) as printed has the opposite sign: it labels $\int_{t_1}^{t_0}-\operatorname{Tr}(\partial f/\partial z(t))\,dt=+\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\operatorname{Tr}(\partial f/\partial z(t))\,dt$ as $\log p(x)-\log p_{z_0}(z_0)$ and says to add $\log p_{z_0}(z_0)$. The integrated state is $r(0)$ of Proposition 2.8, and it must be subtracted; Exercise 2.7 gives the size of the error on the program's field. One hypothesis differs. A Lipschitz field is differentiable only almost everywhere, and Section 2.11 gives a Lipschitz field whose trace does not exist on one trajectory and whose transported density has a jump there. Propositions 2.4 and 2.8 therefore assume a continuous Jacobian, which is stronger than the hypothesis quoted from Theorem 1. Grathwohl et al. state that existence and uniqueness of their (4) "require that f and its first derivatives be Lipschitz continuous", which also gives a Jacobian continuous in the state.

The cost lies in the divergence. Chen et al. note that the determinant in a discrete change of variables has "a cubic cost in either the dimension of z, or the number of hidden units", and the trace replaces it. Grathwohl et al. state in Section 3.1 that "computing Tr(∂f/∂z(t)) exactly costs O(D²), or approximately the same cost as D evaluations of f". For the turbulence samples of this course, $X\in\mathbb R^{3\times N^3}$, so $d=3N^3$; at $N=64$ that is $d=786{,}432$ Jacobian-vector products for each evaluation of the divergence. Their remedy is Hutchinson's trace estimator, their equation (7), $\operatorname{Tr}(A)=\mathbb E_{p(\epsilon)}[\epsilon^{\mathsf T}A\epsilon]$ for any probe distribution with $\mathbb E[\epsilon]=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon)=I$, with "a standard Gaussian or Rademacher distribution" as typical choices. The course reserves $\varepsilon$ for dissipation and writes the probe as $\boldsymbol\eta$. Each probe costs one vector–Jacobian product, about one evaluation of the field. The variances below are derived here; they are not quoted from the paper.

Proposition 2.9 — Hutchinson's estimator. Let $A$ be a real $d\times d$ matrix with symmetric part $S=\tfrac12(A+A^{\mathsf T})$, and let $\boldsymbol\eta$ be a random vector with $\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\eta\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}]=I_d$. Then $\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol\eta]=\operatorname{tr}A$. If $\boldsymbol\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$, then $\operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol\eta)=2\|S\|_F^2$. If the entries of $\boldsymbol\eta$ are independent and equal to $\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each (Rademacher), then $\operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol\eta)=2\big(\|S\|_F^2-\sum_iS_{ii}^2\big)$. Here $\|S\|_F^2=\sum_{i,j}S_{ij}^2$.

Proof. The scalar $\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol\eta$ equals its trace, so $\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol\eta]=\mathbb E\operatorname{tr}(A\boldsymbol\eta\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T})=\operatorname{tr}(A\,\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\eta\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}])=\operatorname{tr}A$. Also $\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol\eta=\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}S\boldsymbol\eta$, because the skew part contributes zero. Gaussian case: write $S=Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}$ with $Q$ orthogonal and eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. Then $\boldsymbol\zeta=Q^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ and $\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}S\boldsymbol\eta=\sum_i\lambda_i\zeta_i^2$, a sum of independent terms with $\operatorname{Var}(\zeta_i^2)=\mathbb E\zeta_i^4-1=2$, so the variance is $2\sum_i\lambda_i^2=2\|S\|_F^2$. Rademacher case: $\eta_i^2=1$, so $\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}S\boldsymbol\eta=\sum_iS_{ii}+2\sum_{i\lt j}S_{ij}\eta_i\eta_j$. The products $\eta_i\eta_j$ with $i\lt j$ have mean zero and variance one, and two different products are uncorrelated because their product contains some $\eta_k$ to the first power. Hence the variance is $4\sum_{i\lt j}S_{ij}^2=2(\|S\|_F^2-\sum_iS_{ii}^2)$. ∎

The Rademacher probe never has larger variance, and it is exact for diagonal matrices. Grathwohl et al. use one probe for a whole ODE solve, their equation (8), so that the dynamics stay deterministic within the solve. The estimate $\log p_0(x(0))-\int_0^1\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}\nabla u_s(x(s))\boldsymbol\eta\,ds$ is then unbiased for $\log p_1(x)$, because the trajectory $x(s)$ does not depend on $\boldsymbol\eta$ and $|\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}\nabla u_s\boldsymbol\eta|\le L\|\boldsymbol\eta\|^2$ has finite mean $Ld$, so Fubini's theorem exchanges the expectation and the time integral. It is not unbiased for $p_1(x)$ itself: the exponential is convex, so Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb E[\exp(\widehat{\log p})]\ge\exp(\mathbb E\widehat{\log p})=p_1(x)$, with equality only when the estimate has zero variance.

2.11 Where the identities fail

Every result above rests on (H). This section shows what each hypothesis protects by removing it, and it ends with a positive statement of what (H) guarantees.

Proposition 2.10 — flows under (H) preserve positivity and bound density ratios. Assume (H) with constant $L$. Then for every $t$ and $x$, $e^{-dLt}p_0(\psi_t^{-1}(x))\le p_t(x)\le e^{dLt}p_0(\psi_t^{-1}(x))$. In particular, if $p_0\gt0$ everywhere then $p_t\gt0$ everywhere, $\sup p_t\le e^{dLt}\sup p_0$, and $X_t$ has no atoms.

Proof. Proposition 2.3 gives $p_t(x)=p_0(\psi_t^{-1}(x))/J_t(\psi_t^{-1}(x))$, and Section 2.3 gives $e^{-dLt}\le J_t\le e^{dLt}$. A law with a density assigns probability zero to every single point. ∎

Zero-density regions. If $p_0$ vanishes exactly on a set $\mathcal Z$, then $p_t$ vanishes exactly on $\psi_t(\mathcal Z)$, so the support is carried by the flow. On trajectories that start in $\mathcal Z$ the logarithm is undefined; the multiplicative identity $p_tJ_t=p_0$ still holds there with both sides zero. With Gaussian noise, Proposition 2.10 has a consequence for modelling: a flow satisfying (H) on $[0,1]$ produces a density that is positive everywhere, so it can only approximate a target with empty regions, such as two separated uniform blobs or data on a lower-dimensional set, and its likelihood at a point far from the data is never $-\infty$. Approximating such a target closely requires a large $L$. Proposition 2.10 bounds the ratio $p_1(x)/p_0(\psi_1^{-1}(x))$ along one trajectory between $e^{-dL}$ and $e^{dL}$, so a ratio of $M\gt1$ forces $dL\ge\log M$. In $d=1$ the map $\psi_1$ is increasing, because $J_1\gt0$. If the model places probability $\tfrac12$ on each side of a point $m$ where its density is $w$, then $\psi_1^{-1}(m)=0$, and $w\ge e^{-L}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ gives $L\ge\log\big(1/(w\sqrt{2\pi})\big)$. At $w=10^{-3}$, in the gap between two equal blobs, this is $L\ge5.99$.

Lipschitz but not differentiable. In $d=1$ take $u(x)=-|x|$, which is Lipschitz with constant one and has a kink at zero. Trajectories are $\psi_t(x_0)=e^{-t}x_0$ for $x_0\ge0$ and $e^{t}x_0$ for $x_0\lt0$, and the point $0$ is fixed. For $x_0\ne0$ Proposition 2.4 holds with $\operatorname{div}u=-\operatorname{sign}(x_0)$. On the trajectory at $0$ the derivative $\nabla u$ does not exist, and $\psi_t$ is not differentiable at $0$. Proposition 2.3 applied on each half-line gives $p_t(x)=e^{t}p_0(e^{t}x)$ for $x\gt0$ and $p_t(x)=e^{-t}p_0(e^{-t}x)$ for $x\lt0$. A smooth $p_0$ with $p_0(0)\gt0$ thus becomes a density with a jump at zero, of ratio $e^{2t}$ between the right and left limits. The classical continuity equation fails at the jump. The weak form of Proposition 2.6 still holds, because its proof uses only the chain rule along each trajectory, the continuity of $u$, and the unique flow that a Lipschitz field has. Networks with corner activations such as ReLU produce fields of this kind; MIT chapter 1 §1.3 notes that such a network is Lipschitz but need not be differentiable everywhere.

Non-Lipschitz fields. MIT chapter 1 §1.3 shows that $dx/dt=2\sqrt{x}$ has many solutions from $x_0=0$, so no flow map and no density formula exist. A non-Lipschitz field can also create an atom. Take $u(x)=-2\operatorname{sign}(x)\sqrt{|x|}$ in $d=1$. For $x_0\gt0$, $\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{x_t}=-1$, so $x_t=(\sqrt{x_0}-t)^2$ until $t=\sqrt{x_0}$, after which the trajectory stays at zero; negative $x_0$ behave symmetrically. At time $t$ every initial state with $|x_0|\le t^2$ sits at zero. For $X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ the atom at zero has mass $\Pr(|X_0|\le t^2)$, which is $0.197413$ at $t=0.5$. Before collapse, $J_t(x_0)=(\sqrt{x_0}-t)/\sqrt{x_0}$ and $\operatorname{div}u=-1/\sqrt{|x|}$, so $\log p_t(\psi_t(x_0))=\log p_0(x_0)+\log\big(\sqrt{x_0}/(\sqrt{x_0}-t)\big)$ rises to $+\infty$ in finite time.

The singular endpoint. The straight conditional path of Section 2.9 satisfies (H) on every $[0,t_\star]$ with $t_\star\lt1$ but not on $[0,1]$. By Proposition 2.10 this is unavoidable for any path whose endpoint is an atom. MIT chapter 2 works on finite horizons $t\lt1$ and treats an atomic endpoint as a weak limit. Its §2.3 also records the positive terminal noise of the original flow-matching paper, which leaves a small Gaussian around each data point; "that avoids this particular singularity but changes the terminal distribution to a smoothed target".

"""Log-density ODE along trajectories of a 2D linear field (chapter 2).

The field is u_t(x) = g(t) A0 x with g(t) = 1 + 0.8 sin(3 pi t) and an
upper-triangular A0, so the flow matrix and the Gaussian law are exact.
"""
import math
import numpy as np

A0 = np.array([[0.6, 1.5], [0.0, -1.0]])
a, c, b = A0[0, 0], A0[0, 1], A0[1, 1]
TRACE = a + b


def g(t):
    return 1.0 + 0.8 * math.sin(3.0 * math.pi * t)


def big_g(t):
    return t + 0.8 * (1.0 - math.cos(3.0 * math.pi * t)) / (3.0 * math.pi)


def flow_matrix(t):
    """Exact Phi(t) = expm(G(t) A0) for the triangular A0."""
    s = big_g(t)
    off = c * (math.exp(a * s) - math.exp(b * s)) / (a - b)
    return np.array([[math.exp(a * s), off], [0.0, math.exp(b * s)]])


def gauss_logpdf(x, cov):
    """Log density of N(0, cov) in 2D; x has shape (n, 2)."""
    prec = np.linalg.inv(cov)
    quad = np.einsum("ni,ij,nj->n", x, prec, x)
    return -math.log(2.0 * math.pi) - 0.5 * math.log(np.linalg.det(cov)) \
        - 0.5 * quad


def rhs(t, x):
    """Joint right side: dx/dt = u_t(x), d(log p)/dt = -div u_t."""
    return x @ (g(t) * A0).T, -g(t) * TRACE


def integrate(x0, ell0, steps, method):
    h = 1.0 / steps
    x, ell = x0.copy(), ell0.copy()
    for k in range(steps):
        t = k * h
        k1x, k1l = rhs(t, x)
        if method == "euler":
            x, ell = x + h * k1x, ell + h * k1l
            continue
        k2x, k2l = rhs(t + h / 2, x + h / 2 * k1x)
        k3x, k3l = rhs(t + h / 2, x + h / 2 * k2x)
        k4x, k4l = rhs(t + h, x + h * k3x)
        x = x + h / 6 * (k1x + 2 * k2x + 2 * k3x + k4x)
        ell = ell + h / 6 * (k1l + 2 * k2l + 2 * k3l + k4l)
    return x, ell

2.13 Exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 2.1 — Jacobi's formula at a singular matrix (derivation)

Let $A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1+t&2\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Compute $\frac{d}{dt}\det A$ at $t=0$ directly and by Proposition 2.1. Explain why the form $\det A\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}\dot A)$ cannot be used, and what the result says about the volume factor of a flow whose Jacobian matrix becomes singular.

Solution

Directly, $\det A(t)=2(1+t)-2=2t$, with derivative $2$. At $t=0$, $\operatorname{adj}A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-2\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\dot A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $\operatorname{adj}A\,\dot A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ with trace $2$, in agreement. The matrix $A(0)$ has determinant zero and no inverse, so the second form has an undefined factor $A^{-1}$ multiplied by a zero factor $\det A$. For a flow, Corollary 2.2 shows that $J_t$ cannot reach zero at a finite time while (H) holds, because $J_t$ is an exponential. A singular Jacobian, as in the conditional path at $t=1$, therefore signals that (H) has failed; the adjugate form of Jacobi's formula remains valid at that point, but the exponential solution does not.

Exercise 2.2 — divergence-free fields cannot rescale a Gaussian (conceptual)

Prove that no field satisfying (H) with $\operatorname{div}u_t=0$ everywhere transports $\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ at $t=0$ to $\mathcal N(0,4I_d)$ at $t=1$. Give a divergence-free field that transports $\mathcal N(0,I_2)$ to itself with nontrivial motion, and compare with an incompressible fluid of nonuniform density.

Solution

By Proposition 2.4, $\operatorname{div}u=0$ gives $p_1(\psi_1(x_0))=p_0(x_0)$ for every $x_0$. Since $\psi_1$ is a bijection, $p_1$ takes exactly the values of $p_0$, so $\sup p_1=\sup p_0=(2\pi)^{-d/2}$. The target $\mathcal N(0,4I_d)$ has supremum $(2\pi)^{-d/2}2^{-d}$, which is smaller, so no such field exists. The rotation field $u(x)=\Omega x$ with $\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has $\operatorname{tr}\Omega=0$, moves every nonzero point along a circle, and maps $\mathcal N(0,I_2)$ to itself because $\Phi(t)\Phi(t)^{\mathsf T}=I_2$ in Proposition 2.7; MIT chapter 2 §2.5 uses the same example. In an incompressible fluid, IITM chapter 10 and Kanpur chapter 4 show that the density of each element is constant while the density at a fixed point may change, for instance $\rho(x,t)=\rho_0(x-Ut)$. A divergence-free probability flow behaves the same way: it rearranges the density values among locations but cannot change them.

Exercise 2.3 — the log-density of MIT's running bridge (computation)

MIT chapter 2 §2.4 finds, for the scalar bridge with $Z\sim\mathcal N(2,4)$ and $\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, the marginal field $u_t(x)=2+(c_t/v_t)(x-m_t)$ with $m_t=2t$, $v_t=5t^2-2t+1$ and $c_t=5t-1$. Compute $\int_0^1\operatorname{div}u_t\,dt$ and use Proposition 2.4 to find $p_1(\psi_1(\xi))/p_0(\xi)$. Check the answer against the endpoint map $\psi_1(\xi)=2+2\xi$. On which time interval does the field concentrate density?

Solution

The divergence is $c_t/v_t$, independent of $x$. Since $\dot v_t=10t-2=2c_t$, it equals $\tfrac12\dot v_t/v_t$, so $\int_0^1c_t/v_t\,dt=\tfrac12\log(v_1/v_0)=\tfrac12\log4=\log2=0.693147$. Proposition 2.4 gives $\log p_1(\psi_1(\xi))=\log p_0(\xi)-\log2$, so the ratio is $1/2$. Directly, the density of $\mathcal N(2,4)$ at $2+2\xi$ is $(2\sqrt{2\pi})^{-1}e^{-\xi^2/2}=p_0(\xi)/2$, in agreement; the derivative of the map is $J_1=2$. The divergence is negative for $t\lt1/5$, where $c_t\lt0$, so density along each trajectory rises during that interval, and it falls afterward. This matches MIT's observation that the variance $v_t$ first contracts to its minimum $4/5$ at $t=1/5$ and then expands.

Exercise 2.4 — Hutchinson variances for the program's matrix (derivation and computation)

For $A_0=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&1.5\\0&-1.0\end{pmatrix}$, compute the variances of the Gaussian and Rademacher estimators from Proposition 2.9 and compare with the program's output. Why does the Rademacher estimator take only two values here? Which matrices make the Rademacher estimator exact?

Solution

The symmetric part is $S=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.75\\0.75&-1.0\end{pmatrix}$, so $\|S\|_F^2=0.36+2(0.5625)+1=2.485$ and $\sum_iS_{ii}^2=1.36$. The Gaussian variance is $2(2.485)=4.97$; the program reports $4.8771$. The fourth central moment of this quadratic form, computed from the eigenvalues $0.8966$ and $-1.2966$ of $S$, gives a standard error of about $0.10$ for a variance estimated from 20000 draws, so the difference $0.093$ is about $0.9$ standard errors. The Rademacher variance is $2(2.485-1.36)=2.25$; the program reports $2.2499$. With $\eta_i^2=1$, the estimate is $S_{11}+S_{22}+2S_{12}\eta_1\eta_2=-0.4\pm1.5$, since $\eta_1\eta_2=\pm1$ with equal probability; its variance is exactly $1.5^2=2.25$. The Rademacher variance $4\sum_{i\lt j}S_{ij}^2$ vanishes exactly when the symmetric part is diagonal, that is, when $A$ is a diagonal matrix plus a skew-symmetric matrix.

Exercise 2.5 — a kink makes a jump (derivation)

For $u(x)=-|x|$ in $d=1$ and $p_0=\mathcal N(0,1)$, derive $p_t$ on each half-line, verify Proposition 2.4 on trajectories with $x_0\ne0$, and verify that the probabilities of the two half-lines stay equal to $1/2$. Which of Propositions 2.4–2.6 hold at $x=0$?

Solution

For $x_0\gt0$, $\dot x=-x$ gives $\psi_t(x_0)=e^{-t}x_0$ and $J_t=e^{-t}$; for $x_0\lt0$, $\dot x=x$ gives $e^{t}x_0$ and $J_t=e^{t}$. Since each half-line maps onto itself, $p_t(x)=e^{t}p_0(e^{t}x)$ for $x\gt0$ and $p_t(x)=e^{-t}p_0(e^{-t}x)$ for $x\lt0$. On a positive trajectory, $\log p_t(e^{-t}x_0)=t+\log p_0(x_0)$, and $\operatorname{div}u=-1$ there, so $-\int_0^t\operatorname{div}u=t$, in agreement; the negative side gives $-t$ with $\operatorname{div}u=1$. Substituting $y=e^{t}x$ gives $\int_0^\infty e^{t}p_0(e^{t}x)\,dx=\int_0^\infty p_0(y)\,dy=1/2$, and likewise on the negative side. The right and left limits at zero are $e^{t}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ and $e^{-t}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. At $x=0$, Proposition 2.4 is undefined because the divergence does not exist, and the classical equation of Proposition 2.5 is undefined because $p_t$ is not differentiable there. The argument of Proposition 2.6 still applies for every $t$, because it uses only the chain rule along each trajectory, the continuity of $u$, and the unique flow that a Lipschitz field has.

Exercise 2.6 — collapse into an atom (computation)

For $u(x)=-2\operatorname{sign}(x)\sqrt{|x|}$ and $X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, find the trajectories, the time at which $x_0=1$ reaches zero, the atom mass at $t=0.5$, and the density on $x\gt0$ at time $t$. Which hypothesis of (H) fails, and why does this not contradict Proposition 2.10?

Solution

For $x_0\gt0$, $\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{x_t}=\dot x_t/(2\sqrt{x_t})=-1$, so $x_t=(\sqrt{x_0}-t)^2$ for $t\le\sqrt{x_0}$ and $x_t=0$ afterward; $x_0=1$ reaches zero at $t=1$. Trajectories cannot leave zero, since a positive state has negative velocity. At $t=0.5$ the states with $|x_0|\le0.25$ are at zero, with probability $\Pr(|X_0|\le0.25)=\operatorname{erf}(0.25/\sqrt2)=0.197413$, where $\operatorname{erf}(y)=(2/\sqrt\pi)\int_0^ye^{-s^2}ds$ is the error function. For $x\gt0$ the preimage is $x_0=(\sqrt x+t)^2$, and $J_t=(\sqrt{x_0}-t)/\sqrt{x_0}=\sqrt x/(\sqrt x+t)$, so $p_t(x)=p_0((\sqrt x+t)^2)(\sqrt x+t)/\sqrt x$, which tends to infinity as $x\to0^+$ for $t\gt0$. The Jacobian $\nabla u=-1/\sqrt{|x|}$ is unbounded near zero, so the finite constant $L$ in (H) does not exist; the field is also not differentiable at zero. Proposition 2.10 assumes (H) and makes no claim about this field. The forward trajectory from each point is unique here, so uniqueness alone does not prevent an atom; the bound on $\nabla u$ does.

Exercise 2.7 — the sign of the likelihood correction (computation)

For the field of Section 2.8 and the data point $x=(1,0)$, compute $x_0=\Phi(1)^{-1}x$ and $\log p_1(x)$ by Proposition 2.8, and check the value against $\mathcal N(0,\Sigma_1)$. What value would a program obtain if it added the divergence integral instead of subtracting it?

Solution

Since $\Phi(1)$ is upper triangular, $x_0=(1/2.017500,\,0)=(0.495663,\,0)$. Then $\log p_0(x_0)=-\log2\pi-\tfrac12(0.495663)^2=-1.837877-0.122841=-1.960718$. The divergence integral is $r(0)=G(1)\operatorname{tr}A_0=-0.467906$, so $\log p_1(x)=-1.960718+0.467906=-1.492812$. The Gaussian $\mathcal N(0,\Sigma_1)$ gives the same value to six decimals, since Proposition 2.7 makes the two computations identical. Adding the integral gives $-2.428624$, an error of $2\times0.467906=0.935812$ in log-likelihood, a factor $e^{0.935812}=2.55$ in density. The sign check is that a field which contracts volume on average, $\int\operatorname{div}u\lt0$, must raise the density, so $\log p_1(x)$ must exceed $\log p_0(x_0)$.

What this chapter establishes. Under hypotheses (H), probability density obeys the same Lagrangian identity as mass density in every dimension: $p_t(\psi_t)J_t=p_0$, with $J_t=\exp\int\operatorname{div}u$ by Jacobi's formula. Its logarithm is the log-density ODE that continuous normalizing flows integrate to compute likelihoods, it is equivalent to the continuity equation for smooth densities, and its weak form holds for any initial law. A linear field and the straight conditional path give exact cases, and Hutchinson's estimator replaces the $d$ products needed for an exact divergence by one product per probe.

What it does not establish. It does not prove uniqueness of weak solutions or the second-order regularity of the flow used in Section 2.6, which it quotes from the sources named there, and it says nothing about which velocity field to choose, since the identities hold for every admissible field. Proposition 2.10 shows that a flow satisfying (H) on $[0,1]$ from Gaussian noise produces only densities that are positive everywhere, so it cannot reach an atom or a region of zero density. The singular endpoint of the straight path follows from its atomic target. Positive terminal noise avoids this singularity by changing the target, and reaching the atom itself requires a field outside (H).
