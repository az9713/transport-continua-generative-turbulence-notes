6. Training data and study design

A generative model of turbulence is trained on snapshots that a direct numerical simulation (DNS) produced at great cost, and those snapshots are strongly correlated in time and in space. The course's own $256^3$ DNS stores 100 snapshots one time unit apart, and its measured velocity correlation leaves about 22 independent samples among them. The public JHTDB forced isotropic dataset stores 5028 frames of a $1024^3$ field, about $8.6\times10^{13}$ bytes if held in single precision, over about five large-eddy turnover times; if its integral time has the same ratio to its turnover time as the course DNS and its correlation is exponential, two assumptions, those frames carry about 2.7 independent samples. This chapter turns the DNS cost model of the essentials edition into a training-set cost, derives the effective number of independent samples in a correlated record exactly and evaluates it from the measured correlation, shows why a train/validation split must be made by time with a guard band, and assembles these results into a study-design template, one fully worked study for the course laboratory, and the rubric by which the capstone is graded.

From the price of a snapshot to a defensible claim. Section 6.1 prices a DNS training set in grid points, time steps and bytes, and decomposes the gap between the essentials estimate and the grid actually used into four named factors, for the course DNS and for JHTDB. Section 6.2 proves the exact effective sample count for correlated snapshots, evaluates it from the measured correlation, and gives its limits in time and in space. Section 6.3 builds the data and GPU budget for Lab 2 from measured numbers. Section 6.4 proves what an interleaved split hides. Sections 6.1–6.3 serve outcome 16, and Sections 6.5–6.7 give the capstone template, a worked study and the rubric (outcome 18); outcome 17 is assessed in week 14 through the modeling edition's chapter 22.

6.1 What does a DNS training set cost?

Throughout this chapter $t_{\mathrm f}$ denotes physical flow time, which must be kept distinct from the generation time $t\in[0,1]$ of the probability path. A training sample is one velocity field $X\in\mathbb R^{3\times n_c^3}$ on a cube of $n_c^3$ grid points, either a full DNS frame or a cutout from one; $n_c$ is the $N$ of the course notation table, renamed here because $N$ denotes the DNS grid. A DNS on $N^3$ points advanced by $M$ time steps of size $\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$ costs, in the model of the essentials edition, a number of floating-point operations proportional to the number of mode-steps $N^3M$; storing $w$ words of $n_b$ bytes per point costs $wn_bN^3$ bytes per frame, which is equation (17.19). The training-set question adds one factor to that model: a generative model needs many frames, so the cost of a dataset is the cost of the run that produced the frames plus the bytes of the frames kept.

The essentials chapter fixes the grid by two criteria, a periodic box of side $\mathcal L=8L_{11}$ and a resolution $\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5$, where $L_{11}$ is the longitudinal integral length, $\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$ the Kolmogorov length, $\nu$ the kinematic viscosity, and $\kappa_{\max}$ the largest retained wavenumber. With $L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon$ the large-scale length built from the turbulent kinetic energy $k$ and the dissipation rate $\varepsilon$, and $\mathrm{Re}_L=k^2/(\varepsilon\nu)$, it obtains equation (17.14), $N^3\approx4.4\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{9/4}$, by taking the model-spectrum asymptote $L_{11}/L=0.43$. With a Courant criterion $k^{1/2}\Delta t_{\mathrm f}/\Delta x=1/20$ and a run of $4k/\varepsilon$ it obtains equation (17.16), $M=(120/\pi)L/\eta\approx9.2\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{3/2}$, and from both the time at one gigaflop, equation (17.18), which assumes 1000 operations per mode per step. That chapter found the estimate to be 3.0 and 3.1 times the grids of two production simulations and stated that its comparison could not locate the factor. The course DNS logs $L_{11}$ and the JHTDB README reports it, so for these two flows the factor can be located.

Proposition 6.1 — the grid identity. Let a periodic cube of side $\mathcal L$ retain wavenumbers up to $\kappa_{\max}=fN\kappa_0$, where $\kappa_0=2\pi/\mathcal L$ and $f$ is the retained fraction ($f=1/2$ for every mode to the Nyquist wavenumber, $f=\sqrt2/3$ for the spherical truncation, $f=85/256$ for the cube rule of the course DNS). Then

$$N=\frac{\kappa_{\max}\eta}{2\pi f}\,\frac{\mathcal L}{L_{11}}\,\frac{L_{11}}{L}\,\frac{L}{\eta},\qquad \frac{L}{\eta}=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}.$$

Consequently the ratio of two grid counts for the same flow is the product of four ratios: resolution, truncation fraction (inversely), box size in integral lengths, and $L_{11}/L$.

Proof. From $\kappa_{\max}=fN\kappa_0=2\pi fN/\mathcal L$, $N=\kappa_{\max}\mathcal L/(2\pi f)$. Multiply and divide by $\eta$, $L_{11}$ and $L$ to separate the factors. For the second identity, $L/\eta=k^{3/2}\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}\nu^{-3/4}=(k^2/(\varepsilon\nu))^{3/4}$. With $f=1/2$, $\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5$ and $\mathcal L/L_{11}=8$, the prefactor is $1.5\cdot8/\pi=12/\pi$, which is the essentials (17.13). ∎

Worked example 6.1 — two grids against the estimate. The course DNS logs, as means over $12\le t_{\mathrm f}\le111$: $\nu=0.002$, $k=0.6223$, $\varepsilon=0.09815$, rms velocity $u'=0.644$, $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=115$, integral scale $L_{11}=1.218$, on a $256^3$ grid in a box of side $2\pi$ that keeps $|\kappa_i|\le85$. Hence $\mathrm{Re}_L=1973$, $L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon=5.002$, $\eta=0.01690$ and $L/\eta=296$. Equation (17.14) gives $N=485$ against the 256 used, a ratio of 1.895. Proposition 6.1 decomposes it into resolution $1.5/1.436=1.044$, truncation $(85/256)/(1/2)=0.664$, box $8/(2\pi/1.218)=8/5.159=1.551$ and length $0.43/(1.218/5.002)=1.766$, whose product is 1.899; the remaining 0.2 % is the rounding of 4.431 to 4.4 in (17.14).

The README of the JHTDB dataset isotropic1024coarse gives, time-averaged over $0\le t_{\mathrm f}\le10.056$: $\nu=0.000185$, $k=0.705$, $\varepsilon=0.103$, $u'=0.686$, $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=418$, $\eta=0.00280$, $L_{11}=1.364$ and large-eddy turnover time $T_L=L_{11}/u'=1.99$, on a $1024^3$ grid in a box of side $2\pi$ with phase shifting and a $2\sqrt2/3$ truncation. (The dataset web page quotes $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx433$; the README gives 433 for the shorter average over $0\le t_{\mathrm f}\le2.048$.) Hence $\mathrm{Re}_L=26\,084$, $L=5.747$ and $L/\eta=2052$, and (17.14) gives $N=3363$ against 1024, a ratio of 3.28. The factors are resolution $1.5/1.352=1.110$, truncation $(\sqrt2/3)/(1/2)=0.943$, box $8/(2\pi/1.364)=8/4.606=1.737$ and length $0.43/(1.364/5.747)=1.812$, with product 3.29.

The two largest factors above unity are the same in both flows: each box holds about five integral lengths, not 8, and $L_{11}/L$ is about 0.24, not the model's 0.43. The essentials comparison scales out resolution and truncation, and on that footing the JHTDB ratio is $1.737\times1.812=3.15$, beside that chapter's 3.0 and 3.1. Its two grids belong to other simulations (Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope 2018), whose $L_{11}$ the pages checked in its comparison section do not report, so their factor stays unlocated.

The time steps follow the same audit. JHTDB uses $\Delta t_{\mathrm f}=0.0002$ with $\Delta x=2\pi/1024$, so $k^{1/2}\Delta t_{\mathrm f}/\Delta x=0.0274=1/36.5$, a step 0.55 times the (17.15) criterion. Its stored segment, $0\le t_{\mathrm f}\le10.056$, is 50 280 DNS steps, or $N^3M=5.399\times10^{13}$ mode-steps, which the (17.18) normalization prices at 625 days on a gigaflop computer. That figure is the essentials cost model applied to the JHTDB grid and step, not the computing time JHTDB spent, for which this chapter has no source. The course DNS uses $\Delta t_{\mathrm f}=0.004$ with $\Delta x=2\pi/256$, so $k^{1/2}\Delta t_{\mathrm f}/\Delta x=0.1286=1/7.8$, a step 2.6 times that criterion; the third-order Runge-Kutta scheme stays stable with a largest logged Courant number, on the peak velocity, of $0.65$ over the whole run and $0.58$ over the stored window. Its 27 750 steps to $t_{\mathrm f}=111$, spin-up included, are $N^3M=4.656\times10^{11}$ mode-steps, priced at 5.4 gigaflop days. The run measured 276.5 ms per step on one laptop GPU, 2.13 hours in total, or $6.07\times10^7$ mode-steps per second.

Storage is the second cost. By (17.19) with $w=4$ (three velocity components and pressure) and $n_b=4$, one frame is $17\,179\,869\,184$ bytes and 5028 frames are $8.638\times10^{13}$ bytes. The precision in which JHTDB stores its fields is not stated in the README pages read here, so this is the single-precision value, not a measured database size. The modeling edition's program counts four double-precision fields, $32n^3$ bytes for $n^3$ points (section 18.4), twice this per point. The unit a laboratory actually moves is far smaller: one $64^3$ float32 velocity block is $3\times4\times64^3=3\,145\,728$ bytes (3 MiB), and one JHTDB frame contains 4096 non-overlapping such blocks. For the course DNS, one $256^3$ velocity field is $201\,326\,592$ bytes and holds 64 such blocks; the 100 filtered fields are $314\,572\,800$ bytes and the 800 crops $2\,516\,582\,400$ bytes.

6.2 How many independent samples does a correlated record hold?

A frame stored every $\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$ is not a new draw from the stationary distribution; it is the previous frame advanced by a short time. The question "how many samples" is therefore only meaningful for a stated scalar statistic $g(X)$, such as the kinetic energy of a cutout or its spectrum in one shell. Let $g_1,\dots,g_n$ be its values on $n$ snapshots, each with variance $\sigma^2$, and let $\rho_{ij}=\operatorname{Cov}(g_i,g_j)/\sigma^2$. Here $\rho$ is a correlation coefficient, as in the essentials (8.4); no mass density appears in this chapter. Define the effective sample count by $\operatorname{Var}(\bar g)=\sigma^2/n_{\mathrm{eff}}$, where $\bar g=n^{-1}\sum_ig_i$: it is the number of independent values whose mean would have the same variance.

Proposition 6.2 — exact effective count. If $\operatorname{Var}(\bar g)\gt0$,

$$n_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{n^2}{\sum_{i,j=1}^n\rho_{ij}},\qquad 1\le n_{\mathrm{eff}},$$

and $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\le n$ whenever every off-diagonal $\rho_{ij}\ge0$, with equality exactly when $\sum_{i\ne j}\rho_{ij}=0$. If the values are equally spaced samples of a stationary process with lag correlation $\rho_m$, the denominator is $n[1+2\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}(1-m/n)\rho_m]$, which is equation (18.4) of the modeling edition.

Proof. By bilinearity, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar g)=n^{-2}\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Cov}(g_i,g_j)=\sigma^2n^{-2}\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}$; equating with $\sigma^2/n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ gives the formula. Since $|\rho_{ij}|\le1$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}\le n^2$, and the sum is positive because it equals $n^2\operatorname{Var}(\bar g)/\sigma^2$; hence $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\ge1$. The diagonal contributes exactly $n$, so nonnegative off-diagonal terms give $\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}\ge n$ and $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\le n$, with equality iff their sum vanishes. Under stationarity $\rho_{ij}=\rho_{|i-j|}$, and the lag $m\ge1$ occurs for $2(n-m)$ ordered pairs, which gives the second form. ∎

The proposition settles the cross-check with the modeling edition: for $n=1000$ and $\rho_m=0.99^m$ the program reproduces its value $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=5.58033052$. It also permits $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\gt n$ for anticorrelated values ($n=2$, $\rho_{12}=-1/2$ gives 4), which is why the upper bound needs the sign condition. To obtain numbers for a record, the correlation must be measured or modelled. The essentials edition's worked case is the exponential, $\rho(h)=e^{-|h|/\bar\tau}$ for lag $h$, with integral time scale $\bar\tau=\int_0^\infty\rho\,dh$, its (8.6). The bar separates this scale from the bare $\tau$, the noising clock of a diffusion process.

Proposition 6.3 — exponential correlation in closed form. Let $n$ snapshots be spaced by $\delta$, with $\rho_m=r^m$, $r=e^{-\delta/\bar\tau}\in(0,1)$, and record length $T=n\delta$. Then

$$\sum_{i,j=1}^nr^{|i-j|}=n\frac{1+r}{1-r}-\frac{2r(1-r^n)}{(1-r)^2},\qquad \lim_{\substack{n\to\infty\\ n\delta=T}}n_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{T}{2\bar\tau}\Big/\Big[1-\frac{\bar\tau}{T}\big(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau}\big)\Big].$$

The limit reproduces the exact variance (8.18) of a continuous time average, and as $T/\bar\tau\to\infty$ it tends to the essentials statement (8.17) that a record of length $T$ is worth $T/(2\bar\tau)$ independent samples. At fixed spacing, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}/n\to(1-r)/(1+r)$ as $n\to\infty$.

Proof. Index the snapshots $0,\dots,n-1$. The diagonal gives $n$. For fixed $i$, $\sum_{j\gt i}r^{j-i}=(r-r^{n-i})/(1-r)$. Summing over $i$, $\sum_i r^{n-i}=r+\dots+r^n=r(1-r^n)/(1-r)$, so the strict upper triangle sums to $[nr-r(1-r^n)/(1-r)]/(1-r)$. Doubling it and adding $n$ gives $n+2nr/(1-r)-2r(1-r^n)/(1-r)^2$, which is the stated form because $1+2r/(1-r)=(1+r)/(1-r)$.

For the limit, put $\delta=T/n$. Then $r^n=e^{-T/\bar\tau}$ exactly, $(1+r)/(1-r)=\coth(\delta/(2\bar\tau))=2\bar\tau/\delta+O(\delta)$ and $r/(1-r)^2=1/[4\sinh^2(\delta/(2\bar\tau))]=\bar\tau^2/\delta^2+O(1)$. Dividing the double sum by $n$,

$$\frac1n\sum_{i,j}r^{|i-j|}=\frac{2\bar\tau}{\delta}-\frac{2\bar\tau^2(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau})}{T\delta}+O\!\left(\frac1n\right)+O(\delta) =\frac{2\bar\tau}{\delta}\Big[1-\frac{\bar\tau}{T}\big(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau}\big)\Big]+o\!\left(\frac1\delta\right).$$

Hence $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n^2/\sum r^{|i-j|}=(T/\delta)\big/\{(2\bar\tau/\delta)[\cdots]+o(1/\delta)\}$, which converges to the stated limit. Writing $\operatorname{Var}=\sigma^2/n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ gives $(2\bar\tau\sigma^2/T)[1-(\bar\tau/T)(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau})]$, equation (8.18). The bracket tends to one as $T/\bar\tau\to\infty$, giving (8.17). At fixed $r$, the first term of the double sum dominates, so $n_{\mathrm{eff}}/n\to(1-r)/(1+r)$. ∎

The essentials edition proved (8.17) as a long-time approximation and noted that it overstates the variance by at most the fraction $\bar\tau/T$. The proposition shows that the discrete count converges to the exact form, which matters when $T$ is only a few integral times long.

The course DNS measures the correlation instead of assuming it. For the velocity field, $\rho(h)$ is the mean over snapshot pairs at lag $h$ of $\langle\mathbf v(t_{\mathrm f})\cdot\mathbf v(t_{\mathrm f}+h)\rangle$ divided by the two root-mean-square norms, computed by labs/dns_correlation.py from the 100 filtered fields and written to rho_lags.csv. By homogeneity and isotropy every velocity component at every grid point has the same autocorrelation, so this $\rho$ is the correlation of one scalar statistic in the sense of Proposition 6.2: $g$ equal to one velocity component at one point. The solver wrote an independent estimate at lags up to 1.0 from the full fields to corr.csv; at $h=1$ the two give 0.602 and 0.603. The measured values at $h=0.2,1,2,\dots,8$ are 0.896, 0.603, 0.427, 0.303, 0.208, 0.136, 0.084, 0.047 and 0.020, with the first zero at $h=9.08$. Beyond that zero the values stay between $-0.019$ and $0.050$ up to lag 59 and reach $0.091$ among the lags from 60 on, which have 40 snapshot pairs or fewer; they are treated as sampling noise. The integral to the first zero, by the trapezoid rule on the solver's lags up to 1 and the whole lags after them, is $\bar\tau=2.30$; on whole lags alone it is 2.33, the form comparable with the shell values below.

Three time scales must be kept apart. The measured integral is $\bar\tau=2.30$. The turnover time $L_{11}/u'=1.89$ is the column TL of stats.csv, the analog of the JHTDB $T_L$, and it is the ratio of a length to a speed, not an integral of $\rho$. The eddy time is $k/\varepsilon=6.34$. Thus $\bar\tau/(L_{11}/u')=1.21$ and $c=\bar\tau/(k/\varepsilon)=0.362$. The correlation is also not an exponential: the successive ratios $\rho_{m+1}/\rho_m$ are 0.708, 0.710, 0.686, 0.655, 0.616 and 0.554 for $m=1,\dots,6$, and the exponential through $\rho(1)$ has the scale 1.98, not 2.30. Proposition 6.2 needs no model, so the counts below use the measured $\rho_m$ directly.

The count is a property of the statistic, and $\rho$ above belongs to the field. The energy of one shell has its own correlation. From spectrum.csv, the integral to the first zero of the sample autocorrelation is 1.65, 1.57, 1.17 and 1.33 at shells 1, 2, 3 and 5, and 2.32 to 2.53 at shells 10 to 31. The total energy $k(t_{\mathrm f})$ differs again: its correlation is 0.88, 0.60, 0.29 and 0.03 at lags 1 to 4, then falls to $-0.39$ at lag 8, a negative lobe that an exponential cannot represent. The forcing holds the energy of the lowest modes fixed, which is a plausible cause that was not tested. A sample autocorrelation from 100 correlated values is itself biased low at long lags, so these numbers carry a limited precision.

Worked example 6.2 — the course record and the JHTDB record. Proposition 6.2 with the measured $\rho_m$, set to zero from the first zero onward, gives $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=21.91$ for all 100 snapshots. Keeping every lag gives 18.83, because the sum then includes the noise beyond the zero; the chapter uses the first value and reports the second. The long-time count (8.17), with $T$ the span 99 of the record, is $T/(2\bar\tau)=99/4.594=21.55$ ($T=n\delta=100$ gives 21.77), and an exponential with $\bar\tau=2.297$ gives 21.92, so the exponential recovers the count although it does not fit the shape. Thinning changes little at first: every 2nd, 3rd and 5th snapshot give 20.55, 18.88 and 15.88, and every 10th gives 10.00. Half the snapshots keep 94 % of the count, and half the bytes.

For JHTDB no integral time is reported. If the ratio $\bar\tau/T_L=1.213$ of the course DNS held there, an assumption, then $\bar\tau=2.41$, and the record of length $T=10.056$ has a long-time count of 2.08 and, if its correlation is also exponential, a second assumption, an exact count of 2.73 by Proposition 6.3. The corrections matter there because $T/\bar\tau$ is only 4.2, against 43 for the course DNS.

The frames JHTDB stores at intervals of 0.002 are not wasted; they serve time derivatives, particle tracking and spectra in frequency. For the purpose of estimating a stationary statistic, however, storage grows linearly with the number of frames while the effective count saturates near the limit of Proposition 6.3. The same arithmetic applies in space, where the periodic box of the course DNS holds $2\pi/1.218=5.16$ integral lengths per axis.

Proposition 6.4 — separable space–time counts multiply. Let samples be indexed by pairs $(a,b)$ of a time index $a$ from a set of size $n_t$ and a position index $b$ from a set of size $n_s$, and suppose $\rho_{(a,b),(a',b')}=\rho^{\mathrm t}_{aa'}\rho^{\mathrm s}_{bb'}$. Then $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n^{\mathrm t}_{\mathrm{eff}}\,n^{\mathrm s}_{\mathrm{eff}}$, where each factor is computed by Proposition 6.2 from its own correlation matrix. If the spatial correlation is itself a product over the three axes, $n^{\mathrm s}_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the product of three one-axis counts.

Proof. The total count is $n=n_tn_s$, and the double sum factorizes: $\sum_{a,b,a',b'}\rho^{\mathrm t}_{aa'}\rho^{\mathrm s}_{bb'}=\big(\sum_{a,a'}\rho^{\mathrm t}_{aa'}\big)\big(\sum_{b,b'}\rho^{\mathrm s}_{bb'}\big)$. By Proposition 6.2, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n_t^2n_s^2/[(\sum\rho^{\mathrm t})(\sum\rho^{\mathrm s})]=n^{\mathrm t}_{\mathrm{eff}}n^{\mathrm s}_{\mathrm{eff}}$. The same factorization applied to the three axes gives the last statement. ∎

Separability is an assumption, not a property of isotropic turbulence: the two-point correlation of an isotropic field depends on the separation through its magnitude and direction (essentials section on the two-point correlation), not on a product of axis functions. It is used here because it makes the count computable from one length per axis, and because the resulting numbers are meant as an order of magnitude to be replaced by a measured correlation. On a periodic axis of length $B$ the program uses the periodized exponential $\rho(d)=[e^{-d/\ell}+e^{-(B-d)/\ell}]/(1+e^{-B/\ell})$ of the modeling edition's program, which is the correlation of an exponential process wrapped onto the circle, with $\ell=L_{11}=1.218$. Four equally spaced positions per axis, which tile a $256^3$ field with its 64 contiguous $64^3$ crops, give $n^{\mathrm s}_{\mathrm{eff}}=2.299$ per axis and $12.15$ in total. Two per axis give $1.738$ and $5.25$ of 8, the size of the set of eight crops that the DNS saves per snapshot, although those crops are at random positions and may overlap. A filtered field covers the whole box, so it counts as one spatial sample.

The essentials cost model can now be written per independent sample rather than per run.

Proposition 6.5 — cost per independent sample. Let a stationary DNS on $N^3$ points with step $\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$ supply a statistic of integral time $\bar\tau=c\,k/\varepsilon$, and count independent samples by the long-time limit of Proposition 6.3. Then each independent sample costs $2\bar\tau N^3/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$ mode-steps. Under the criteria of (17.15)–(17.16), this is $(c/2)N^3M\approx84.5\,c\,\mathrm{Re}_L^3$ mode-steps, excluding spin-up.

Proof. A record of length $T$ costs $N^3T/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$ mode-steps and holds $T/(2\bar\tau)$ independent samples; the quotient is $2\bar\tau N^3/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$. In (17.16), $M=4(k/\varepsilon)/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$, so $2\bar\tau/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}=2c(k/\varepsilon)M/(4k/\varepsilon)=(c/2)M$. The essentials chapter gives the unrounded $N^3M=169\,\mathrm{Re}_L^3$, and half of 169 is 84.5. ∎

The training-data cost of an independent large-scale sample therefore grows as $\mathrm{Re}_L^3$, like the cost of the simulation itself: doubling $\mathrm{Re}_L$ multiplies it by 8. For the course DNS the measured $\bar\tau$ gives $2\bar\tau/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}=1149$ steps per independent sample of the field, which took 318 s on the GPU and $1.927\times10^{10}$ mode-steps. The model of the proposition, with $c=0.362$ and $\mathrm{Re}_L=1973$, gives $2.351\times10^{11}$ mode-steps, 12.2 times as many. That ratio is the product of two measured departures from the criteria and one rounding: the grid holds 6.80 times fewer modes than (17.14); the step is 1.78 times the step $4(k/\varepsilon)/M$ of (17.16), which rests on the model's grid spacing and so differs from the 2.6 of (17.15) on the course grid; and the 169 behind 84.5 uses 4.431 where (17.14) uses 4.4, a factor 1.007. The finite-$T$ correction of Proposition 6.3 lowers these costs slightly; it does not change their scaling.

6.4 Why the split must be made by time, with a guard band

A validation set measures generalization only if its samples are not near-copies of training samples. For snapshots of a flow, nearness has a precise meaning. Let $X(t_{\mathrm f})\in\mathbb R^D$ be the field of one cutout location, stationary in time, with component autocovariances $R_j(h)=\operatorname{Cov}(X_j(t_{\mathrm f}),X_j(t_{\mathrm f}+h))$ for $j=1,\dots,D$, and define the energy-weighted correlation $\bar\rho(h)=\sum_jR_j(h)/\sum_jR_j(0)$.

Proposition 6.6 — a memorized frame is close in proportion to $1-\bar\rho$. For every lag $h$, $\mathbb E\|X(t_{\mathrm f}+h)-X(t_{\mathrm f})\|^2=2[1-\bar\rho(h)]\sum_jR_j(0)$, whereas an independent copy $X'$ of the same stationary law gives $\mathbb E\|X'-X(t_{\mathrm f})\|^2=2\sum_jR_j(0)$. A generator that outputs the training frame at lag $h$ from a given validation frame therefore has, for that pair, the fraction $1-\bar\rho(h)$ of the expected squared distance of a genuinely new sample to the same frame. For training frames at lags $h_1,\dots,h_m$ from it, the expected squared distance to the nearest of them is at most $2[1-\max_i\bar\rho(h_i)]\sum_jR_j(0)$; these identities give no lower bound for it.

Proof. The means cancel in each difference. For each component, $\operatorname{Var}(X_j(t_{\mathrm f}+h)-X_j(t_{\mathrm f}))=2R_j(0)-2R_j(h)$ by stationarity, and summing over $j$ gives the first identity. For an independent copy the covariance term is zero, giving $2\sum_jR_j(0)$. Their ratio is $1-\bar\rho(h)$. The minimum of $m$ squared distances is at most each of them, so its expectation is at most the smallest of the $m$ expectations. A lower bound would need the joint law of the $m$ distances, which second moments do not fix. ∎

The weighting matters: by Parseval's identity $\sum_jR_j(h)$ has the same value in the Fourier basis, where the modes that carry the most variance, the energy-containing scales, dominate it. For a whole field, $\bar\rho$ is estimated by the measured $\rho$ of Section 6.2; that estimator averages the ratio of each pair, and the ratio of the two sums differs from it by less than 0.001 at lags 1 to 9. A random 80/20 split of the 100 snapshots leaves most validation fields one time unit from a training field, where $1-\bar\rho=0.397$: a copy of that training field sits at 39.7 % of the expected squared distance that a genuinely new sample has to the same validation field. The same split of fields saved every 0.2 would give $1-\bar\rho=0.104$. A smooth autocorrelation, which a viscous velocity has at short lags (essentials section on the spectrum and the variance), makes $1-\bar\rho$ quadratic rather than linear in a small enough lag. The solver's values at lags 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 are 0.104, 0.213, 0.290, 0.349 and 0.398, so its finest logged lag lies above that regime and does not resolve it.

A score that matches each validation field to its nearest sample takes a minimum, and the last statement of the proposition then governs. Measured on the filtered fields, with squared distances normalized by twice the mean squared norm of the 74 training fields of Worked example 6.3 as the estimate of $2\sum_jR_j(0)$, and called distances from here on, the distance of a pair has mean 0.396 at lag 1 and 0.949 at lag 7, as the proposition predicts, with standard deviations 0.036 and 0.082 across pairs. Pairs at lag 26 or more have mean 0.978, standard deviation 0.079 and range 0.747 to 1.228, so the minimum over a set lies below that mean. As a proxy for a new sample, compare each of the 100 fields with the fields at least 26 lags from it, 49 to 74 of them: the nearest lies at a median distance of 0.842, with range 0.747 to 0.925 and standard deviation 0.039. The cut at 26 gives each field at either end of the record 74 comparison fields, the size of the training set, and cuts from 9 to 28 lags give medians of 0.841 to 0.842. Under such a score a lag-1 copy has about $0.396/0.842=0.47$ of the new sample's distance, not 0.397. A distance-based validation score therefore rewards copying under a random split, by a factor of about 2 when it takes the nearest match.

A split by time puts every validation frame after every training frame, and a guard band of length $G$ between them guarantees $h_{\min}\ge G$. Requiring $\bar\rho(G)\le0.05$ under an exponential model gives $G\ge\bar\tau\ln20=2.9957\,\bar\tau$, the threshold of the modeling edition's section 18.7, a design choice there, which is 6.88 for $\bar\tau=2.297$. The measured correlation gives the same answer directly: $\bar\rho(6)=0.084$ and $\bar\rho(7)=0.047$, so $G=7$. That is 7 % of the 99-unit record. At that lag $1-\bar\rho=0.953$, so a copy of the boundary training field has in expectation 95.3 % of the squared distance that a new sample has to the first validation field. Nearest-match distances agree: the 20 test fields of Worked example 6.3 lie at a median distance of 0.823 from the nearest of the 74 training fields, with range 0.747 to 0.860, against the 0.842 of the new-sample proxy. Only the first test field has its nearest training field at the boundary; the other 19 find it at lags 51 to 85, so time proximity no longer selects the match. The difference 0.019 is about half the proxy's standard deviation. A guard band does not detect copying by itself; that needs the nearest-training-neighbor test of Table 6.3, which compares a sample with the training set.

Lab 2 sorts its files, whose names begin with the snapshot number, takes the last fifth as the test set, and drops the --guard files before it. With the default of no guard the boundary pair is 1.0 apart, where $1-\bar\rho=0.397$. For the 100 filtered fields, --guard 6 leaves 74 training fields, drops six, and puts the boundary pair at lag 7. Crops have eight files per snapshot, so the same band is --guard 48. Even a correct time split tests only interpolation within one stationary state of one flow: it says nothing about another Reynolds number, another forcing or another geometry, and it rests on the ergodicity assumption that chapter 5 examined for Lienen et al.

6.5 The capstone: a study-design template

The capstone (outcome 18) is a written study design, not a trained model. Its purpose is to state, before any training, what claim the study can support, what evidence would refute it, and what the evidence costs. Each item below must be answered with numbers and with the source of each number: a derivation, a quoted paper equation, a measurement made by the student, or an assumption marked as such.

  1. Claim. One sentence of the form "model $\mathcal M$ trained on data $\mathcal D$ reproduces statistics $S_1,\dots,S_q$ of target distribution $P$ within tolerances $\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_q$".
  2. Target distribution. The law of exactly which object: dataset, field, cutout size, contiguous or strided sampling, time window in $t_{\mathrm f}$, positions, normalization, and the stationarity or ergodicity assumption that makes a time window a sample of $P$.
  3. Model and probability path. The path (here $X_t=tz+(1-t)\boldsymbol\xi$), the conditional velocity and loss, the network, its parameter count, the sampler and its step count. State the conditional loss floor (MIT chapter 2): a training loss that stays above zero is not by itself a failure.
  4. Data and split. Frames, positions, bytes, the time split and its guard band, and $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ for each test statistic with the source of $\bar\tau$ and $\ell$.
  5. Physical tests. For each statistic, what agreement shows and what it cannot show (Table 6.3).
  6. Uncertainty and thresholds. Pass/fail tolerances fixed before training and justified against the reference set's own sampling scatter.
  7. Compute budget. Storage, download route, GPU-hours from measured ms/step, and generation time, each with provenance.
  8. Limits. What a passing result would still not establish.

6.6 A worked example study

Claim. A width-32 two-level 3D U-Net (1.42 M parameters) trained by conditional flow matching on the 74 filtered fields of the course DNS with $t_{\mathrm f}\le85$ reproduces, on the 20 fields with $t_{\mathrm f}\ge92$, (i) the mean shell spectrum within a factor 1.25 for all shells $1\le\kappa\le31$, (ii) the flatness averaged over the three components within 0.2 and the velocity-gradient skewness within 0.1; and it shows (iii) no memorization by the nearest-neighbor test.

Target distribution. The law of the velocity field after the sharp cube cutoff $|\kappa_i|\lt32$, on the $64^3$ grid over the whole box, at a time in the stationary window. The acceptance check finds no drift in the gradient flatness over $12\le t_{\mathrm f}\le111$, and the study treats that window as a sample of the stationary state by the ergodicity assumption. Fields are divided by the training-set standard deviation, as Lab 2 does.

Model and path. $X_t=tz+(1-t)\boldsymbol\xi$, target $z-\boldsymbol\xi$, mean-squared loss, AdamW at learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, fp16 autocast, circular padding (the fields are periodic), 30 000 steps at batch 2, sampling by 50 Euler steps. The conditional loss has a positive floor (MIT chapter 2), so the training loss is not used as evidence of quality. The command is python labs/lab2_generative_turbulence_3d.py --data filtered --batch 2 --iters 30000 --guard 6.

Correlation. The study needs no pilot, because the correlation of the field and of every shell energy is measured from the published files (Section 6.2), and that of the one-point and gradient statistics from the filtered fields. The slowest statistic among the tests sets the guard band. On whole lags the field integral is 2.33. It is larger than the shell-energy integrals below shell 10 (1.17 to 2.29) and within 9 % of those at shells 10 to 31 (2.32 to 2.53), and every shell correlation is below 0.05 at lag 7. The per-field flatness gives, by component, integrals of 5.94, 1.83 and 1.99 and correlations at lag 7 of 0.19, $-0.22$ and 0.01; the gradient skewness gives 1.25, 0.73 and 0.70, and 0.06, $-0.14$ and $-0.01$. Isotropy makes the three components equal in law, so these spreads show how poorly 100 snapshots fix such a correlation. The correlations averaged over the components have integrals 2.24 and 0.78 and values $-0.01$ and $-0.03$ at lag 7, and the band $G=7$ rests on those averages, not on any single component. The series of flatness averaged over the components, the statistic of the claim, has correlation 0.08 at lag 7, inside its sampling standard error of about 0.16, from Bartlett's formula $\sqrt{(1+2\sum_m\rho_m^2)/100}$ for a lag beyond the correlated range, with the sum over the series' correlations at lags 1 to 6.

Data and split. Training $t_{\mathrm f}=12,\dots,85$, 74 fields; guard band, six fields dropped; test $t_{\mathrm f}=92,\dots,111$, 20 fields; $295\,698\,432$ bytes in total. Effective counts as in Worked example 6.3: 16.34 for training and 4.79 for test.

Physical tests. Test (i) is the first row of Table 6.3: agreement shows the second-order distribution of energy over shells 1 to 31, and it cannot show phases or any statistic above second order. Test (ii) is the fourth and fifth rows. The averaged flatness tests the one-point shape and the gradient skewness the small-scale asymmetry; neither reaches below the grid. A Gaussian field with the same spectrum misses the skewness tolerance by about 0.32. It misses the flatness tolerance by about 0.07 on the moment that Lab 2 pools over the fields (3 against 2.73), and by about 0.02 on the per-field mean (2.94 against 2.72), about 0.6 of the 0.038 standard error of that reference mean given below. The 2.94 comes from 800 simulated Gaussian solenoidal fields with the mean shell spectrum of the filtered fields, a check outside the chapter program; one such field has a flatness below 3 because shells 1 and 2, with 80 wavevectors, hold 60 % of its energy, and without those shells the same simulation gives 2.99. The skewness is therefore the larger margin against that alternative. Test (iii) is the last row: it can show the absence of copying and cannot show that the distribution is correct.

Thresholds against reference scatter. The factor 1.25 is a log-ratio tolerance of $\ln1.25=0.223$. For it to test the model rather than the reference, the reference's own uncertainty must be well inside it. The program computes, for each shell $1\le\kappa\le31$, the coefficient of variation $c_v$ of the shell energy across the 100 snapshots and its effective count among 20 consecutive snapshots from the shell's own correlation; the largest relative standard error $c_v/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ of a test-set mean is $0.068$, at shell 31. The tolerance is therefore 3.3 of those standard errors there. Thirty-two independent generated fields add at most about $0.15/\sqrt{32}=0.03$ in quadrature, which leaves about 3.1 standard errors there. The flatness and skewness tolerances are checked the same way from the filtered fields, each statistic with its own correlation. Over the 20 test fields the component flatness has a mean between 2.64 and 2.82 per field (Lab 2, which pools the fields before it forms the moment, gets 2.65 to 2.86). A single component has a standard deviation of 0.13 to 0.17 across the 100 snapshots and a count of 2.68 to 5.80 among 20, so a standard error of 0.054 to 0.106, and the reference components already differ by about the tolerance. The claim therefore averages over the components: that average has a standard deviation of 0.080 across the snapshots and a count of 4.44 among 20, so a standard error of 0.038, and the tolerance is 5.3 of those. The one-sided-difference gradient skewness has a mean between $-0.42$ and $-0.41$, with a count of 8.5 to 14.4 among 20 and a standard error of at most 0.016, so the tolerance is at least 6 of those.

Memorization test. For each of 32 generated fields and each of the 20 test fields, compute the squared distance to the nearest of the 74 training fields, normalized by twice the mean squared norm of the training fields, the estimate of $2\sum_jR_j(0)$. A copy of a training field gives 0. Proposition 6.6 bounds the expected test distance only from above, because the nearest field is a minimum over the set, so the reference level is measured: the median test distance is 0.823 (Section 6.4). The study fails the test if the median generated distance is below the median test distance. For a generator of new samples the proxy of Section 6.4 gives a median of 0.842 and a standard deviation of 0.039, so the median of 32 independent draws has a standard error of about $1.25\times0.039/\sqrt{32}=0.009$, with 1.25 the factor for the median of a Gaussian, and the margin 0.019 is about two of those. The proxy median is itself an estimate with a similar scatter, so the margin is between one and two standard errors, and the rule can fail a correct generator with a probability that this chapter does not compute. Lab 2 does not compute this distance, so the study must add it.

Budget. Generating the data: 2.13 GPU-hours once (measured), with no download; the 100 filtered fields occupy 300 MiB. Training: about 2.5 GPU-hours at the 298 ms per step of the full run, against 1.19 from the probe (Worked example 6.3). Generation: at most 114 s (bound). Peak training memory in the probe: 1281 MiB of 4096 MiB.

Limits. A pass would show that the model reproduces second-order spectral content and low-order one-point and gradient statistics on a later window of the same stationary flow at $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=115$, with tolerances limited by 4.79 effective reference samples. It would not show generalization to other Reynolds numbers or forcings, any inertial-range property (chapter 3 finds no inertial band in these data), the statistics above $\kappa=31$ (those shells hold 15.5 % of the dissipation, the cube cutoff keeps only their corners, and the filtered field has 9.5 % less dissipation than the logged mean), correct dynamics in $t_{\mathrm f}$ (the model is unconditional and has no time), or the absence of errors that the tolerances cannot detect. This chapter reports no result of the study. The numerical laboratory reports one run of Lab 2 with these settings, which covers tests (i) and (ii) and not test (iii).

def ar1_factor(n, r):
    """1 + 2 sum_{m=1}^{n-1} (1 - m/n) r^m in closed form (Prop. 6.3)."""
    s = r / (1 - r) - r * (1 - r**n) / (n * (1 - r) ** 2)

def n_eff(n, rho, stride=1):
    """n^2 / sum(rho_ij) for n equally spaced values (Prop. 6.2)."""
    lags = np.arange(1, n)
    return n / (1 + 2 * ((1 - lags / n) * rho[lags * stride]).sum())


def to_first_zero(rho):
    """Set rho to zero from its first non-positive value onward."""
    out = rho.copy()
    out[np.argmax(rho <= 0):] = 0
    return out


def ring_corr(d, box, ell):
    """Periodized exponential correlation on a ring of length box."""
    num = np.exp(-d / ell) + np.exp(-(box - d) / ell)
    return num / (1 + math.exp(-box / ell))


def n_eff_ring(p, box, ell):
    """Exact n^2 / sum(rho) for p equally spaced points on a ring."""
    d = np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(p), np.arange(p))) * box / p
    return p * p / ring_corr(d, box, ell).sum()

6.9 Exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 6.1 — bounds on the effective count

Prove that $1\le n_{\mathrm{eff}}\le n$ when all off-diagonal correlations are nonnegative. Give an example with $n=2$ where $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\gt n$, and explain what it would mean for a split into two half-records.

Solution

By Proposition 6.2, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n^2/\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}$. Each $|\rho_{ij}|\le1$, so the sum is at most $n^2$, and it is positive because it equals $n^2\operatorname{Var}(\bar g)/\sigma^2$; hence $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\ge1$. The diagonal gives $n$, and nonnegative off-diagonal terms make the sum at least $n$, so $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\le n$. With $n=2$ and $\rho_{12}=-1/2$, the sum is $2-1=1$ and $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=4$. Two anticorrelated half-records average to a mean with half the variance of two independent values; their errors cancel partly. For a stationary turbulent record this requires a negative lobe in the autocorrelation at the half-record lag, and the long-time count of Proposition 6.3 does not apply.

Exercise 6.2 — exact and long-time counts

Suppose a statistic of the JHTDB record has measured $\bar\tau=1.0$. Compute the long-time count $T/(2\bar\tau)$ and the exact continuous count of Proposition 6.3 for $T=10.056$. Which one should a study report, and by what fraction do they differ?

Solution

The long-time count is $10.056/2=5.028$. The bracket is $1-(1/10.056)(1-e^{-10.056})=1-0.099443\times0.999957=0.900561$, so the exact count is $5.028/0.900561=5.583$. The exact count is 11.0 % larger: the bracket differs from one by $(\bar\tau/T)(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau})=0.0994$, essentially $\bar\tau/T$ because $e^{-T/\bar\tau}$ is negligible. A study should report the exact count, or the discrete double sum of Proposition 6.2 for its actual frames, and state that both rest on the exponential model; the long-time form is 9.9 % below the exact value here and is a conservative approximation.

Exercise 6.3 — the Reynolds-number price of one independent sample

Derive Proposition 6.5 from (17.15) and (17.16). A study needs 50 independent large-scale samples ($c=0.362$, the value measured on the course DNS) at $\mathrm{Re}_L$ twice that of the course DNS. By what factor does the data cost exceed that of the same study at the course Reynolds number, and why is the factor independent of 50?

Solution

A record of length $T$ costs $N^3T/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$ mode-steps and holds $T/(2\bar\tau)$ independent samples, so one sample costs $2\bar\tau N^3/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$. From (17.16), $\Delta t_{\mathrm f}=4(k/\varepsilon)/M$, so this equals $2c(k/\varepsilon)N^3M/(4k/\varepsilon)=(c/2)N^3M=84.5c\,\mathrm{Re}_L^3$. Here $c=2.297/6.341=0.362$. Fifty samples cost $50\times84.5c\,\mathrm{Re}_L^3$; doubling $\mathrm{Re}_L$ multiplies this by $2^3=8$. The number of samples and $c$ cancel in the ratio, so the factor 8 holds for any required count, provided $c$ does not change with Reynolds number, which is an assumption. Spin-up is excluded and adds a further cost of the same scaling.

Exercise 6.4 — what a random split hides

A student assigns each of the 100 filtered fields independently to validation with probability 0.2. Find the probability that a given interior validation field has a training field at lag 1, and the measured value of $1-\bar\rho$ at that lag. Repeat the distance fraction for fields saved every 0.2. Explain why a validation CFM loss equal to the training loss is then no evidence of generalization.

Solution

An interior validation field lacks a training neighbor at lag 1 only if both neighbors are validation fields, with probability $0.2^2=0.04$, so the probability is 0.96. At that lag $1-\bar\rho=1-0.603=0.397$, and at lag 0.2 it is $1-0.896=0.104$. By Proposition 6.6, a copy of the neighboring training field is in expectation at 39.7 % or 10.4 % of the squared distance a new sample has to the same validation field, so a score that matches each validation field to its nearest sample rewards a model that reproduces its training fields; when that score takes a minimum over many samples, the new sample's side is a minimum too, and Section 6.4 measures the advantage at lag 1 as a factor of about 2. The validation fields are not new data for the CFM loss either. They have correlation 0.603 or 0.896 with a training field, so a validation loss equal to the training loss shows only that the model does as well on near-copies of its training fields as on those fields, and it says nothing about a field with no near-copy in training. The CFM loss has a further defect as evidence: it is bounded below by the conditional variance of the target (MIT chapter 2), so its value measures regression error against noisy labels, not the physical statistics of samples.

Exercise 6.5 — staying inside the measured range

A student proposes width 48 at batch 4 for 30 000 steps and estimates its memory as $2\times1924=3848$ MiB and its time as $2\times329$ ms per step. Using Table 6.2, give one reason each estimate is unreliable, and state what must be done instead. What is the largest measured configuration that fits, and its time?

Solution

At width 32 the measured memory is close to linear in batch: 656, 1281 and 2536 MiB at batch 1, 2 and 4, so each added sample costs 625 to 628 MiB above a small fixed part. At width 48 there is only one batch point, 1924 MiB at batch 2, so its fixed part and per-sample slope cannot be separated from the data. Doubling to 3848 MiB assumes a zero fixed part, and that estimate is within 6 % of the 4096 MiB device limit, so an error of that size decides between running and failing. Time does not scale with batch either: width 32 goes from 143 to 380 ms, a factor 2.66, not 2. The configuration must be measured with the same probe before it is budgeted. The largest measured configuration is $64^3$, batch 4, width 32: 2536 MiB, 380 ms per step, 3.17 GPU-hours for 30 000 steps.

Exercise 6.6 — two positions per axis

Prove the factorization of Proposition 6.4 for two time indices and two positions. Then compute the separable spatial count for 2 positions per axis, spaced $\pi$ apart on the periodic axis of length $2\pi$ with $\ell=1.218$, and compare it with the 12.15 of 4 positions per axis.

Solution

With $\rho^{\mathrm t}=\begin{pmatrix}1&p\\p&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\rho^{\mathrm s}=\begin{pmatrix}1&q\\q&1\end{pmatrix}$, the sum over the four indices of $\rho^{\mathrm t}_{aa'}\rho^{\mathrm s}_{bb'}$ is $(2+2p)(2+2q)$, the product of the two matrix sums, so $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=16/[(2+2p)(2+2q)]=n^{\mathrm t}_{\mathrm{eff}}n^{\mathrm s}_{\mathrm{eff}}$. For the axis, $d=\pi$ gives $\rho=2e^{-2.579}/(1+e^{-5.159})=0.1516/1.0057=0.1507$, so $n^{\mathrm s}_{\mathrm{eff}}=4/(2+0.3014)=1.738$ per axis and $1.738^3=5.25$ of 8 positions. Four positions per axis give 12.15 of 64. Eight times the crops buy 2.3 times the effective samples, because the box holds only $5.16$ integral lengths per axis.

Exercise 6.7 — choose a guard band from a measured correlation

A study tests only the energy of shell 1, whose measured integral time is $\bar\tau=1.65$ and whose measured correlation is 0.77, 0.35 and 0.04 at lags 1, 2 and 3. Find the guard band for $\bar\rho\le0.05$ from the exponential rule, and state which measured property of $\rho$ the rule ignores. Keep the test set of 20 snapshots, spanning a window of length 19 at the end of the record of length 99, and compute the long-time and exact effective counts of the training window under the exponential model. Why must the study of Section 6.6 still use $G=7$?

Solution

$G=1.65\ln20=4.94$, so a guard of 5 at the snapshot spacing. The rule ignores that this correlation is not exponential: its measured value is 0.04 at lag 3, so a band of 3 already meets the criterion for this statistic. With $G=5$ the training window is $99-19-5=75$ long. The long-time count is $75/3.3=22.73$. The bracket is $1-(1.65/75)(1-e^{-45.5})=0.978$, so the exact count is $22.73/0.978=23.24$. The study of Section 6.6 tests more than one statistic, and memorization is judged on the whole field, whose correlation first falls below 0.05 at lag 7. The guard must serve the slowest statistic in the study.

Exercise 6.8 — referee a claim

A report states: "We trained on 640 crops of the course DNS and tested on 160 held-out crops drawn at random from all 100 snapshots; the generated spectrum matches the test spectrum within 5 % at every wavenumber, so the model generalizes." Identify three defects using this chapter's results and the boundaries in Table 6.3.

Solution

First, crops drawn at random times lie in the same or the adjacent snapshot as training crops, at lag 0 or 1, and crops of one snapshot may overlap in space. Proposition 6.6 compares a pair at one position, and for the whole field its fraction at lag 1 is 0.397, so the test set is not held out from the training set and a nearest-match distance score rewards copying. Second, the 100 snapshots hold about 22 independent samples of the field (Section 6.2), so 5 % agreement is a resolved statement only if two standard errors, $2c_v/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}$, are below 0.05, that is, if $c_v\lt0.117$ for $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=22$; the measured $c_v$ of shells 1 to 31 is between $0.064$ and about $0.15$ for whole-box shell energies, and the report must measure it for crops. Third, a spectral match is a second-order test: a Gaussian field with the same spectrum would pass it, so the claim needs a higher-order test such as gradient skewness or flatness, and a memorization test. The word "generalizes" also needs a scope: at best, interpolation within one stationary state at one Reynolds number.

What this chapter establishes. The cost of a DNS training set is the essentials cost model applied per independent sample: $2\bar\tau N^3/\Delta t_{\mathrm f}$ mode-steps, $(c/2)N^3M\approx84.5c\,\mathrm{Re}_L^3$ under that model's criteria. The effective count of correlated snapshots is $n^2/\sum\rho_{ij}$ exactly; for exponential correlation it has a closed form whose continuum limit is the exact (8.18), and it saturates as frames are added within a fixed record. For the course DNS the measured correlation gives an integral time of 2.30 and about 22 independent samples among 100 snapshots, 16.3 in a training set and 4.8 in a test set of 20, a data budget of 300 MiB and about 2.5 GPU-hours for the worked study, and a guard band of 7 time units, at which a copy of the boundary training field has in expectation 95.3 % of the squared distance that a new sample has to the first test field. The template and rubric turn these into a gradeable study design.

What it does not establish. The integral time of any JHTDB statistic, for which labelled assumptions are used; the validity of the separable exponential model in space; the JHTDB storage precision and download times; and anything about a trained model's quality, which belongs to the laboratory. All GPU numbers are single runs on one laptop GPU, and all DNS numbers come from one run at $\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=115$.
