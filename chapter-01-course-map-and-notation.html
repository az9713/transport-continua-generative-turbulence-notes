1. Course map and notation

This course follows the continuity equation through two subjects and applies it to a third. In a continuum, the mass of a moving material volume is conserved; in flow matching, the probability carried by a velocity field from noise to data is conserved, and both obey that equation. In turbulence, kinetic energy is not conserved: it is moved across wavenumbers and removed by viscosity. A generative model of a turbulent field transports probability, and it is judged by whether its samples reproduce that distribution of energy. The subjects are taught in five existing study editions, and those editions use the same letters for different objects. $\mathbf X$ is a particle label in one edition and a current position in another; $\varepsilon$ is an infinitesimal strain, a strain rate, a Gaussian noise variable or a dissipation rate, depending on the edition; and the two turbulence editions place the factor $2\pi$ of the Fourier transform on opposite sides of the transform pair. A reader who moves between the editions without a translation can carry a wrong factor of $(2\pi)^3\approx248.05$ or a reversed time variable into a later calculation. This chapter supplies the translation. It lays out the four-part, fourteen-week route, states the prerequisites with a self-test, fixes one meaning for each symbol, records each conflict found with the edition and place where it occurs, proves that energy and filter transfer functions take the same values in both Fourier conventions, shows the same for the energy spectrum $E(\kappa)$, and checks the $2\pi$ factors and Parseval's identity numerically.

Map, test, translate, convert. Read the route and see which weeks send you to an existing chapter and which to a new one. Take the prerequisite self-test. Learn the binding notation and the translation of each conflicting symbol. Derive the relation between the two Fourier conventions, verify it on a filter kernel, a Gaussian and a periodic box, and finish with the eighteen outcomes and the week that assesses each.

1.1 Four parts carry the continuity equation from matter to probability and apply it to turbulent fields

Part I, Continua (weeks 1–3), builds the kinematics of a deforming body. A motion maps particle labels to current positions; its derivative with respect to the label, the deformation gradient $\mathbf F$, maps neighbouring line elements; its determinant $J=\det\mathbf F$ is the local ratio of current to reference volume. The part ends with the transport theorem and the continuity equation $\partial_{t_{\mathrm f}}\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho\mathbf v)=0$ for the mass density $\rho$, the fluid velocity $\mathbf v$ and the physical time $t_{\mathrm f}$, derived once along particle paths and once for a fixed control volume. Chapter 2 repeats the first derivation for probability and relates it to the second.

Part II, Probability transport (weeks 4–7), replaces mass by probability. A velocity field $u_t(x)$ integrated from $t=0$ to $t=1$ moves samples of a simple noise law to samples of a data law, and the density $p_t$ of the moving samples obeys the same continuity equation as $\rho$. Week 5 is a new bridge chapter that makes the identification exact: Jacobi's formula $\dot J=J\operatorname{div}\mathbf v$ from Part I becomes the log-density equation $d\log p_t(\psi_t)/dt=-\operatorname{div}u_t(\psi_t)$, where $\psi_t$ is the flow map of $u_t$, the position at time $t$ of the sample that started at a given point. The remaining weeks of the part construct flow matching, scores and the stochastic samplers that share the same one-time laws.

Part III, Turbulence as a random field (weeks 8–10), supplies the statistics a generated field must reproduce: probability densities and correlations of a random velocity, the integral time scale, Kolmogorov's hypotheses, the energy spectrum $E(\kappa)$ and its three ranges, and large-eddy-simulation filters as convolutions. A new chapter in week 9 computes $E(\kappa)$ from a periodic field with the discrete convention fixed in Section 1.5.

Part IV, Generative models of turbulent fields (weeks 11–14), joins the threads. A Gaussian filter is the heat equation run for a known time, so blurring is a probability path of its own; a diffusion model trained on turbulence can lose its high-wavenumber content first; training data come from direct numerical simulation at a cost that grows with the Reynolds number; and the course ends with a complete study design. The partition into four parts is this chapter's construction from the fourteen-week route.

Eleven of the fourteen weeks send the reader to existing chapters of five study editions, cited below by short names: IITM (NPTEL continuum mechanics, IIT Madras), Kanpur (NPTEL fluid mechanics, IIT Kanpur), MIT (MIT 6.S184 flow matching and diffusion models), essentials (NPTEL essentials of turbulence) and modeling (NPTEL turbulence modeling). Weeks 5, 11 and 12 consist of new chapters only; weeks 9, 13 and 14 combine both kinds. In the table, “links out” marks a reading in an existing edition and “new” marks a chapter of this course.

1.2 What the course assumes, and a self-test that locates the gaps

The course assumes the mathematical physics of a first graduate year and an introduction to machine learning. From vector calculus it uses the divergence theorem, the chain rule for maps between Euclidean spaces and the Jacobian determinant of a change of variables. From partial differential equations it uses separation of variables for the heat equation on a periodic interval and the idea that each Fourier mode of a linear constant-coefficient equation evolves independently. From Fourier analysis it uses series on a periodic interval, the transform on the real line, orthogonality of complex exponentials and Parseval's identity. From probability it uses densities, expectation, variance, independence, the Gaussian law and the density of an affine image of a random variable. From machine learning it uses a loss function, gradient descent and the fact that the minimizer of an expected squared error is a conditional mean. The seven questions below sample these items; they do not test the chain rule for maps, the transform on the real line or independence. A reader who cannot answer two or more should repair those items before week 1, because weeks 3, 5, 6, 9 and 11 use them without review.

  1. Divergence and flux. Let $\mathbf v(\mathbf x)=\mathbf A\mathbf x$ with a constant real $3\times3$ matrix $\mathbf A$. Compute $\operatorname{div}\mathbf v$ and the outward flux of $\mathbf v$ through the surface of the unit cube $[0,1]^3$.

    Solution

    Component $i$ is $v_i=\sum_jA_{ij}x_j$, so $\partial v_i/\partial x_i=A_{ii}$ and $\operatorname{div}\mathbf v=\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A$, a constant. The divergence theorem gives an outward flux equal to $\int_{[0,1]^3}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A\,d\mathbf x=\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A$. A linear field therefore expands every volume at the same relative rate $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A$; week 5 uses exactly this fact for a linear probability flow.

  2. Heat equation on a mode. Show that $u(x,s)=e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}\cos(\kappa x)$ solves $\partial_su=\nu\,\partial_x^2u$. After the time $s=1/\nu$, by what factor has the amplitude of the mode $\kappa=2$ fallen relative to that of the mode $\kappa=1$?

    Solution

    $\partial_su=-\nu\kappa^2u$ and $\partial_x^2u=-\kappa^2u$, so both sides equal $-\nu\kappa^2u$. At $s=1/\nu$ the amplitudes are $e^{-4}$ and $e^{-1}$; their ratio is $e^{-3}\approx0.0498$. The mode $\kappa$ is multiplied by $e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}$, so a higher wavenumber decays faster. This ordering is the basis of chapter 4.

  3. Fourier series and Parseval. On $[0,2\pi)$ write $f(x)=3+2\cos(4x)$ as $\sum_nc_ne^{inx}$. Compute $(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}f^2\,dx$ and $\sum_n|c_n|^2$.

    Solution

    $2\cos(4x)=e^{4ix}+e^{-4ix}$, so $c_0=3$, $c_{\pm4}=1$ and every other coefficient is zero. Expanding $f^2=9+12\cos4x+4\cos^24x$ and averaging gives $9+0+2=11$. The coefficient sum is $9+1+1=11$. The two agree, as Parseval's identity for the normalized series requires.

  4. Density of an affine image. Let $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $Y=aX+b$ with real $a\ne0$. Give the density of $Y$ in terms of the density $p_X$ of $X$, and name the law of $Y$.

    Solution

    For $a\gt0$, $P(Y\le y)=P(X\le(y-b)/a)$; differentiating gives $p_X((y-b)/a)/a$. For $a\lt0$ the inequality reverses and the derivative carries $-1/a$. Both cases give $p_Y(y)=p_X((y-b)/a)/|a|$, which is the density of $\mathcal N(b,a^2)$. The factor $1/|a|$ is a one-dimensional Jacobian; week 5 generalizes it to $p_t(\psi_t(x_0))\,J_t(x_0)=p_0(x_0)$.

  5. The best constant predictor. For a random variable $Y$ with finite variance, find the constant $c$ that minimizes $\mathbb E[(Y-c)^2]$ and the minimum value.

    Solution

    $\mathbb E[(Y-c)^2]=\operatorname{Var}Y+(\mathbb EY-c)^2$, obtained by adding and subtracting $\mathbb EY$; the cross term has zero expectation. The minimum is at $c=\mathbb EY$ and equals $\operatorname{Var}Y$. Applied conditionally on an input, the same argument shows that squared-error regression learns a conditional mean, the fact on which conditional flow matching rests.

  6. Gradient descent on a quadratic. Apply gradient descent with step $\eta\gt0$ to $L(w)=\tfrac12(w-3)^2$. Find the update, the factor by which the error $w_n-3$ changes per step and the range of $\eta$ for which the iteration converges from every start.

    Solution

    $L'(w)=w-3$, so $w_{n+1}=w_n-\eta(w_n-3)$ and $w_{n+1}-3=(1-\eta)(w_n-3)$. The error is multiplied by $1-\eta$ each step; it tends to zero from every start exactly when $|1-\eta|\lt1$, that is $0\lt\eta\lt2$. At $\eta=1$ the iteration reaches the minimizer in one step.

  7. The finest resolvable wave on a grid. A periodic interval of length $\mathcal L$ carries $N$ equally spaced samples. Which angular wavenumbers $\kappa=2\pi n/\mathcal L$ can be distinguished from each other on that grid, and what is the largest $|\kappa|$ for $N=64$ and $\mathcal L=2\pi$?

    Solution

    At the sample points $x_j=j\mathcal L/N$, $e^{i2\pi(n+N)x_j/\mathcal L}=e^{i2\pi nx_j/\mathcal L}e^{i2\pi j}=e^{i2\pi nx_j/\mathcal L}$, so integers $n$ that differ by $N$ give the same samples. One representative per class can be chosen with $-N/2\le n\lt N/2$, which gives $|\kappa|\le\pi N/\mathcal L$, the Nyquist wavenumber. For $N=64$, $\mathcal L=2\pi$ this is $\kappa_{\max}=32$. Chapter 3 uses this bound.

Four distinctions in the table need explanation. First, the course has four clocks. Generation time $t$ runs from noise to data; a diffusion model's forward clock $\tau$ runs from data to noise; the heat-equation time $s$ measures how long a field has been blurred; and physical flow time $t_{\mathrm f}$ is the time of the Navier–Stokes equations. Chapter 4 relates a filter width to a blur time, $\Delta^2=24\nu s$, while chapter 5 uses $t$ for its probability path; with one letter for both, the two relations could not appear in the same equation. In Parts I and III, where only physical time occurs, the editions' $t$ is kept and means $t_{\mathrm f}$.

Second, the course has two velocity fields. The fluid velocity $\mathbf v(\mathbf x,t_{\mathrm f})$ moves matter in physical space; the probability velocity $u_t(x)$ moves samples in the state space of a generative model. When the state is a turbulent velocity field, $x$ is itself a field $X$ with $3N^3$ components, and $u_t$ acts on that whole field. The two velocities then live in different spaces: $\mathbf v$ has three components at each grid point, while $u_t(X)$ has $3N^3$ components. Bold type marks vectors in physical space; the state-space objects $x$, $z$ and $u_t$ are set in italic, as in MIT. The noise $\boldsymbol\xi$ is the one exception: it lives in state space but is set in bold.

Third, the course has two densities: $\rho$, mass per unit volume, and $p_t$, probability per unit volume of state space. Week 5 shows that they obey the same equation, $D\log\rho/Dt_{\mathrm f}=-\operatorname{div}\mathbf v$ and $d\log p_t(\psi_t)/dt=-\operatorname{div}u_t(\psi_t)$ where the densities are positive, which is why distinct letters are needed: the analogy is between two equations, not an identity between two quantities.

Fourth, the letters $\kappa$ and $k$ are not interchangeable. In the course, $\kappa$ is always a wavenumber and $k$ is always the turbulent kinetic energy. The two generative-turbulence papers read for chapter 5 write $k$ for wavenumber; when a chapter quotes them it translates $k$ to $\kappa$.

One pair in Table 1.2 shares a letter: $s$ without a subscript is heat time, and $s_t$ is the score. The subscript $t$ separates them.

Rows 1 and 2 belong together, because the letters $\mathbf X$ and $\mathbf V$ appear in both continuum editions with the roles of description exchanged. In IITM, $\mathbf X$ is a label and $\mathbf V$ is a function of that label; in Kanpur, $\mathbf X$ is a position that moves and $\mathbf V$ is a function of a fixed point in space. The translation follows from IITM's definitions. Take the configuration at $t_0$ as the reference configuration, so that the label of a particle is its position $\mathbf x_0$ at $t_0$. Then Kanpur's path is IITM's motion, $\mathbf X^{\mathrm{Kanpur}}(\mathbf x_0,t_0;t)=\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf x_0,t)$, Kanpur's velocity field is IITM's spatial velocity, $\mathbf V^{\mathrm{Kanpur}}(\mathbf x,t)=\mathbf v(\mathbf x,t)$, and IITM's material velocity is the composition $\mathbf V^{\mathrm{IITM}}(\mathbf X,t)=\mathbf v(\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf X,t),t)$. The two Kanpur objects therefore correspond to two different IITM objects that happen to have the other letter.

Row 3 shows that $\varepsilon$ has four meanings with three different units: dimensionless (IITM strain), $\mathrm s^{-1}$ (Kanpur strain rate), dimensionless and random (MIT noise) and $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-3}}$ (dissipation). A units check detects every confusion except the first and third. The course removes $\varepsilon$ from noise for a separate reason: chapter 5 uses both objects, the noise of its probability paths and the dissipation rate in the model spectrum $E(\kappa)\propto\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}$. The Kanpur strain rate is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient, so in course notation it is $D_{xy}$; IITM's $\mathbf D$ is the same tensor.

Row 4 records a conflict that becomes visible in week 5. MIT chapter 2 uses $D_t=\partial_\xi\psi_t$, a one-dimensional Jacobian, to derive the density change of variables $p_t(\psi_t(\xi))D_t=p_0(\xi)$, in which $\xi$ is the starting point that the course writes $x_0$ (row 14). Row 10 was found during the preparation of this chapter: MIT chapter 3 writes $\rho_\tau$ for the density of the noising process, which collides with mass density in every chapter that contains both.

1.5 The course places $(2\pi)^{-3}$ on the forward transform; energy, transfer functions and $E(\kappa)$ do not depend on that choice

Two placements of $2\pi$ occur on the route. The essentials edition defines the one-dimensional transform (19.7) and the three-dimensional spectrum tensor (8.29) with the prefactor on the forward transform. The modeling edition, §20.1, uses $\widehat f(\kappa)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)e^{-i\kappa x}dx$ with the prefactor $(2\pi)^{-1}$ on the inverse. The course adopts the essentials placement. For a function $f$ on $\mathbb R^d$, $d=1$ or $3$, write

$$\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\frac1{(2\pi)^d}\int_{\mathbb R^d}f(\mathbf x)\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\,d\mathbf x,\qquad f(\mathbf x)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)\,e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa,\tag{1.1}$$
$$\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}f(\mathbf x)\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\,d\mathbf x,\qquad f(\mathbf x)=\frac1{(2\pi)^d}\int_{\mathbb R^d}\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\,e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa.\tag{1.2}$$

Equation (1.1) is the course convention and (1.2) the modeling convention, marked by the superscript $\mathrm M$. Comparing the two forward integrals gives the conversion $\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}=(2\pi)^d\widehat f$: a factor $2\pi$ in one dimension and $(2\pi)^3\approx248.05$ in three. Each inverse undoes its own forward transform, so a formula that uses one convention for both directions is correct; an error arises when formulas of the two conventions are combined, for example when a forward transform taken from one edition is inverted with the formula of the other. Assume in this section that $f$ is integrable and square-integrable and that its transform is integrable, so that every integral below exists and the order of integration may be exchanged; Gaussian functions satisfy all three conditions.

Proposition 1.1 — Parseval's identity in each convention. Under the assumptions above,

$$\int_{\mathbb R^d}|f(\mathbf x)|^2\,d\mathbf x=(2\pi)^d\int_{\mathbb R^d}|\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)|^2\,d\boldsymbol\kappa=\frac1{(2\pi)^d}\int_{\mathbb R^d}|\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}(\boldsymbol\kappa)|^2\,d\boldsymbol\kappa.\tag{1.3}$$

Proof. Write $|f|^2=\overline f f$ and replace $f$ by its inverse transform (1.1). Exchanging the order of integration,

$$\int|f|^2\,d\mathbf x=\int\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)\left[\int\overline{f(\mathbf x)}\,e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\,d\mathbf x\right]d\boldsymbol\kappa=\int\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)\,\overline{(2\pi)^d\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa,$$

because the bracket is the complex conjugate of the forward integral in (1.1), which equals $(2\pi)^d\widehat f$. This is the first equality. The second follows by substituting $\widehat f=(2\pi)^{-d}\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}$. ∎

The identity says that the energy of a signal is the same number in either convention, but the weight with which the transform is squared and integrated differs by $(2\pi)^{2d}$ between the two. A program that computes $\int|\widehat f|^2d\boldsymbol\kappa$ with one convention and compares it with a formula written in the other is wrong by that factor, which is $(2\pi)^6\approx6.15\times10^4$ in three dimensions.

The filter transfer function needs a separate statement, because the essentials edition uses a second integral besides (19.7). Its (19.8) defines the transfer function of a kernel $G$ as $\hat G(\kappa)=\int G(r)e^{-i\kappa r}dr=2\pi\,\mathscr F\{G\}$, where $\mathscr F$ is the transform (19.7), with the explicit factor $2\pi$ placed so that the transfer function equals one at $\kappa=0$. That integral is the modeling forward transform (1.2), and modeling (20.1) writes it with the same hat. The course keeps this usage, as chapter 4 does: a hat on a kernel $G$ means the transfer function, and the course transform (1.1) of a kernel is written $\mathscr F\{G\}$. The next proposition states the consequence in $d$ dimensions.

Proposition 1.2 — the transfer function is the same function in both editions. Let $G$ be an integrable kernel with $\int G\,d\mathbf r=1$, let $\overline f(\mathbf x)=\int G(\mathbf r)f(\mathbf x-\mathbf r)\,d\mathbf r$, and define the transfer function $\hat G(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\int G(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}d\mathbf r$. Then

$$\widehat{\overline f}=\hat G\,\widehat f,\qquad \widehat{\overline f}{}^{\,\mathrm M}=\hat G\,\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M},\qquad \hat G=(2\pi)^d\,\mathscr F\{G\},\qquad \hat G(\mathbf 0)=1.\tag{1.4}$$

Proof. Insert the definition of $\overline f$ into (1.1), exchange the integrals, and substitute $\mathbf y=\mathbf x-\mathbf r$ at fixed $\mathbf r$, so that $e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}=e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf y}$:

$$\widehat{\overline f}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\int G(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\,d\mathbf r\cdot\frac1{(2\pi)^d}\int f(\mathbf y)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf y}\,d\mathbf y=\hat G(\boldsymbol\kappa)\,\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa).$$

The same steps without the prefactor give the modeling form. The identity $\hat G=(2\pi)^d\mathscr F\{G\}$ is the definition of $\hat G$ compared with (1.1), the modeling forward integral (1.2) of $G$ is $\hat G$ itself, and $\hat G(\mathbf 0)=\int G=1$. ∎

The proposition explains why essentials Table 19.1 and modeling §20.2–20.4 agree on the three transfer functions they share (box, Gaussian, sharp spectral; Table 19.1 also lists the Cauchy and Pao filters, which modeling chapter 20 does not treat) although their transforms differ: both tabulate $\hat G$, and $\hat G$ does not depend on the convention. It also identifies the error to avoid. In the course convention the transform of a kernel is not its transfer function; $\mathscr F\{G\}=\hat G/(2\pi)^d$, and at $\boldsymbol\kappa=\mathbf 0$ it equals $(2\pi)^{-d}$, not one. The proposition has two limits. The sharp spectral kernel $\sin(\pi r/\Delta)/(\pi r)$ is not integrable, so the proposition does not cover it; essentials defines that filter by its transfer function and obtains the kernel from the inverse of (19.8). A periodic or statistically homogeneous field is not integrable either, and for it the identity holds mode by mode: $\int G(\mathbf r)e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf r)}d\mathbf r=\hat G(\boldsymbol\kappa)e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}$, so each coefficient of a filtered periodic field is multiplied by $\hat G(\boldsymbol\kappa)$.

Worked conversion: the Gaussian filter. Take the one-dimensional Gaussian kernel of essentials Table 19.1 and modeling §20.4, $G(r)=(6/(\pi\Delta^2))^{1/2}\exp(-6r^2/\Delta^2)$, whose second moment is $\Delta^2/12$. With $a=6/\Delta^2$, completing the square in the exponent gives the standard integral $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-ar^2}e^{-i\kappa r}dr=(\pi/a)^{1/2}e^{-\kappa^2/(4a)}$. Hence

$$\hat G(\kappa)=\left(\frac6{\pi\Delta^2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\pi\Delta^2}6\right)^{1/2}e^{-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24}=e^{-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24},\qquad \mathscr F\{G\}(\kappa)=\frac1{2\pi}\,e^{-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24}.\tag{1.5}$$

At $\kappa=0$ the course transform is $1/(2\pi)\approx0.159155$, and at every $\kappa$ the ratio of the two transforms is $2\pi$. In three dimensions the product kernel $G(r_1)G(r_2)G(r_3)$ has transfer function $e^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Delta^2/24}$ and course transform $(2\pi)^{-3}e^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Delta^2/24}$. Chapter 4 equates this transfer function with the heat semigroup $e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}$; that comparison is between two transfer functions and is therefore free of $2\pi$ factors.

Spectra. The essentials edition defines the velocity-spectrum tensor by (8.29), $\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=(2\pi)^{-3}\int e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}R_{ij}(\mathbf r)\,d\mathbf r$, where $R_{ij}$ is the two-point correlation of the fluctuation $u_i$, the course's $v'_i$ (Table 1.3, row 17), with inverse (8.30) and no prefactor. Hence $\langle u_iu_j\rangle=R_{ij}(\mathbf 0)=\int\Phi_{ij}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa$ by (8.31). The energy spectrum (8.32) integrates $\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}$ over the sphere $|\boldsymbol\kappa|=\kappa$, and (8.33) gives $\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa=k$. Had the spectrum tensor been defined with the modeling convention, it would be $\Phi^{\mathrm M}_{ij}=(2\pi)^3\Phi_{ij}$. Then $E$ would need the factor $(2\pi)^{-3}$ in front of the integral (8.32) of $\tfrac12\Phi^{\mathrm M}_{ii}$, and the same factor would stand in front of the integral for $k$. With that factor $E(\kappa)$ is the same function as (8.32); only the intermediate tensor is rescaled.

The discrete periodic convention. A simulated field lives on a periodic box $[0,\mathcal L)^3$ sampled at $N^3$ points $\mathbf x=(j_1,j_2,j_3)\mathcal L/N$, with integers $0\le j_i\lt N$. The admissible wavenumbers are $\boldsymbol\kappa=2\pi\mathbf n/\mathcal L$ with integer components $-N/2\le n_i\lt N/2$. The course transform pair is

$$\widehat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}=\frac1{N^3}\sum_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x},\qquad f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\widehat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\,e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}.\tag{1.6}$$

In NumPy the forward transform is numpy.fft.fftn(f)/N**3 and the inverse is numpy.fft.ifftn(f_hat)*N**3, because NumPy's default places $N^{-3}$ on the inverse. The aliasing test product() in the program of essentials chapter 17 uses the same normalization, dividing fftn by Ng**3. The largest wavenumber per direction is the Nyquist value $\pi N/\mathcal L$.

Proposition 1.3 — what a discrete coefficient means. (i) For every real or complex grid function, $N^{-3}\sum_{\mathbf x}|f(\mathbf x)|^2=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|\widehat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2$. (ii) If $f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{\mathbf n}a_{\mathbf n}e^{i2\pi\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x/\mathcal L}$ is a finite Fourier series with $-N/2\le n_i\lt N/2$, then $\widehat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}=a_{\mathbf n}$ exactly at $\boldsymbol\kappa=2\pi\mathbf n/\mathcal L$. (iii) For the same periodic $f$, the course transform (1.1) is $\widehat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\sum_{\mathbf n}a_{\mathbf n}\,\delta(\boldsymbol\kappa-2\pi\mathbf n/\mathcal L)$, a sum of Dirac masses with weights $a_{\mathbf n}$, whereas the modeling forward integral over one period, $\int_{[0,\mathcal L)^3}f\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}d\mathbf x$, equals $\mathcal L^3a_{\mathbf n}$.

Proof. The discrete exponentials are orthogonal on the grid: for admissible $\boldsymbol\kappa,\boldsymbol\kappa'$, $\sum_{\mathbf x}e^{i(\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa')\cdot\mathbf x}=N^3$ if $\boldsymbol\kappa=\boldsymbol\kappa'$ and zero otherwise, since each of the three factors is a geometric sum of $N$-th roots of unity, which vanishes unless the integer $n_i-n_i'$ is zero (it lies strictly between $-N$ and $N$). Inserting the inverse in (1.6) into $\sum_{\mathbf x}|f|^2$ and using orthogonality gives (i). Inserting the finite series into the forward sum and using orthogonality gives (ii). For (iii), the inverse in (1.1) applied to $\sum a_{\mathbf n}\delta(\boldsymbol\kappa-2\pi\mathbf n/\mathcal L)$ returns $\sum a_{\mathbf n}e^{i2\pi\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x/\mathcal L}=f$; this is the distributional sense in which a periodic function has a transform. The period integral is $\mathcal L^3a_{\mathbf n}$ by orthogonality of $e^{i2\pi\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x/\mathcal L}$ over one period. ∎

Part (iii) is a practical consequence of the course choice. With $(2\pi)^{-3}$ on the forward transform, the continuous spectrum of a periodic field is a set of Dirac masses whose weights are exactly the coefficients that fftn/N**3 returns, with no factor of $\mathcal L^3$ or $(2\pi)^3$. A velocity field in a box with zero grid mean then satisfies $k=\tfrac12\langle\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v\rangle=\tfrac12\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\langle|\widehat{\mathbf v}_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2\rangle$, by part (i) and an ensemble average; a nonzero grid mean appears in the coefficient at $\boldsymbol\kappa=\mathbf 0$, and chapter 3 subtracts it first. The sum of $\tfrac12|\widehat{\mathbf v}_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2$ over a shell of wavenumbers of width $\kappa_0=2\pi/\mathcal L$ is the energy of that shell. The discrete energy spectrum is that sum divided by $\kappa_0$, so that the spectrum summed over shells and multiplied by $\kappa_0$ equals $k$, the discrete form of $\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa=k$. Chapter 3 builds its spectrum estimator on that quotient, its (3.3). Where a chapter uses a formula from the modeling edition, it multiplies the edition's forward transform by $(2\pi)^{-d}$ before combining it with a course formula, and leaves transfer functions unchanged.

1.6 A numerical check: the factor is $2\pi$ in one dimension, and Parseval holds in the course convention

The modeling-convention integral over the box, summed directly on the $48^3$ grid, is $\mathcal L^3=(2\pi)^3=248.0502134424$ times the coefficient $a_{(3,0,0)}=1$. Here $(2\pi)^3$ is the box volume at $\mathcal L=2\pi$, not the convention factor of Section 1.5. The Dirac statement of (iii) has no numerical check here; it rests on the proof. The grid mean of $f^2$ and the coefficient sum are both $2.5$: the mean of $4\cos^2(3x)$ is $2$, the mean of $\sin^2(5y)$ is $1/2$, and the cross term averages to zero.

Part (d) confirms Proposition 1.3(i) on a field with no special structure. The printed $k$ is $\tfrac12\langle\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v\rangle$, which here includes the small nonzero grid mean of the random field, because the identity tested holds with or without it. The three values agree to twelve decimal places, with relative difference $2.967\times10^{-16}$, which is rounding error of double precision. Grouping the modes into integer shells $|\mathbf n|$ rounded to the nearest integer does not change the sum; any assignment of modes to shells preserves it. The program's wavenumbers are the integers $\mathbf n$, which means a box side $\mathcal L=2\pi$ and $\kappa_0=1$, so its shell energies and its discrete spectrum have the same values. The final line shows that raw fftn coefficients, which carry no prefactor, give a squared sum $N^6=1.073742\times10^9$ times larger than the course coefficients for $N=32$, a ratio that holds by construction because the course coefficients are the raw ones divided by $N^3$. This field is neither solenoidal nor turbulent; it tests the normalization only. Chapter 3 builds a solenoidal field with a prescribed spectrum and recovers that spectrum.

1.8 Exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 1.1 — translate a Kanpur statement into IITM notation

Kanpur chapter 3 writes $\mathbf X(\mathbf x_0,t_0;t)$ for the path of an element and $\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t)$ for the velocity field, and states that the element's velocity is $\partial\mathbf X/\partial t$. Rewrite this statement in IITM notation with the configuration at $t_0$ as reference, and say which IITM object is $\mathbf V^{\mathrm{IITM}}$.

Solution

With the configuration at $t_0$ as reference, the label of the element is $\mathbf X^{\mathrm{IITM}}=\mathbf x_0$ and its current position is $\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf x_0,t)$, so $\mathbf X^{\mathrm{Kanpur}}(\mathbf x_0,t_0;t)=\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf x_0,t)$. The element's velocity is $\partial\boldsymbol\chi/\partial t$ at fixed label, which IITM calls $\mathbf V^{\mathrm{IITM}}(\mathbf x_0,t)$. Kanpur's statement that this equals the field at the element's position reads $\mathbf V^{\mathrm{IITM}}(\mathbf x_0,t)=\mathbf v(\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf x_0,t),t)$, where $\mathbf v=\mathbf V^{\mathrm{Kanpur}}$. Thus $\mathbf V^{\mathrm{IITM}}$ is the material description of the same velocity whose spatial description Kanpur calls $\mathbf V$. The two letters coincide, but one is a function of a label and the other of a place; the course writes $\mathbf v$ for the latter.

Exercise 1.2 — rewrite the conditional flow-matching target in course notation

MIT chapter 2 writes $X_t=tZ+(1-t)\varepsilon$, $u_t(x\mid z)=(z-x)/(1-t)$ and the training label $V_t=Z-\varepsilon$. Rewrite these in course notation, verify that $u_t(X_t\mid Z)=V_t$, and compute $\operatorname{div}_x u_t(x\mid z)$ in $\mathbb R^d$. Which course symbol would be ambiguous if $\varepsilon$ were kept?

Solution

In course notation $X_t=tZ+(1-t)\boldsymbol\xi$ with $\boldsymbol\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, $u_t(x\mid z)=(z-x)/(1-t)$ and $V_t=Z-\boldsymbol\xi$. Substituting $x=X_t$ gives $Z-X_t=(1-t)(Z-\boldsymbol\xi)$, so $u_t(X_t\mid Z)=Z-\boldsymbol\xi=V_t$. The field is affine in $x$ with Jacobian matrix $-I/(1-t)$, so $\operatorname{div}_xu_t(x\mid z)=-d/(1-t)$ for $t\lt1$; chapter 2 uses this value in the log-density equation. If $\varepsilon$ were kept for noise, the dissipation rate $\varepsilon$ that appears in the same chapters, for example in a model spectrum $E\propto\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}$ used to build a training set, would share its letter with a random variable.

Exercise 1.3 — derive the three-dimensional conversion factors

Starting from (1.1) and (1.2) in three dimensions, derive the factor relating $\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}$ to $\widehat f$, the factor relating the two weights in Parseval's identity, and the factor by which $\Phi^{\mathrm M}_{ij}$ would differ from the essentials tensor (8.29). Give each numerically.

Solution

The forward integrals are identical except for the prefactor, so $\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}=(2\pi)^3\widehat f\approx248.05\,\widehat f$. By Proposition 1.1, $\int|f|^2=(2\pi)^3\int|\widehat f|^2=(2\pi)^{-3}\int|\widehat f^{\,\mathrm M}|^2$; the two weights differ by $(2\pi)^6\approx6.15\times10^4$, which equals the square of the amplitude factor, as it must because the integrands are squared transforms. Applying the modeling forward transform to $R_{ij}$ gives $\Phi^{\mathrm M}_{ij}=(2\pi)^3\Phi_{ij}$, and then $\langle u_iu_j\rangle=(2\pi)^{-3}\int\Phi^{\mathrm M}_{ij}d\boldsymbol\kappa$. The energy spectrum is unchanged, because the factor $(2\pi)^{-3}$ also enters the integral (8.32) that defines it.

Exercise 1.4 — transfer function of the box filter in both conventions

The box kernel is $G(r)=1/\Delta$ for $|r|\le\Delta/2$ and zero otherwise. Compute its transfer function $\hat G(\kappa)$, its course transform $\mathscr F\{G\}(\kappa)$ and the value of each at $\kappa=0$. At which smallest positive $\kappa$ does $\hat G$ vanish?

Solution

$\hat G(\kappa)=\Delta^{-1}\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}e^{-i\kappa r}dr=\Delta^{-1}\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}\cos(\kappa r)\,dr=\frac{2\sin(\kappa\Delta/2)}{\kappa\Delta}$ for $\kappa\ne0$, the sine part vanishing by symmetry. This is $\operatorname{sinc}(\kappa\Delta/2)$ with $\operatorname{sinc}y=\sin y/y$, as in Table 19.1, and $\hat G(0)=1$ by continuity or directly from $\int G=1$. The course transform is $\mathscr F\{G\}=\hat G/(2\pi)$, with $\mathscr F\{G\}(0)=1/(2\pi)\approx0.159$. $\hat G$ vanishes first where $\kappa\Delta/2=\pi$, that is $\kappa=2\pi/\Delta$, the mode whose wavelength equals the box width. The zero is a property of $\hat G$ and occurs at the same wavenumber in both conventions.

Exercise 1.5 — discrete coefficients and Parseval by hand

On a $16^3$ grid of $[0,2\pi)^3$ let $f=2\cos(3x)+\sin(5y)$. Give every nonzero coefficient $\widehat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}$ of (1.6), verify Proposition 1.3(i), and state what raw numpy.fft.fftn(f) returns at $\boldsymbol\kappa=(3,0,0)$. Why would $\sin(9y)$ not be represented correctly on this grid?

Solution

From $2\cos(3x)=e^{3ix}+e^{-3ix}$ and $\sin(5y)=(e^{5iy}-e^{-5iy})/(2i)$: $\widehat f_{(\pm3,0,0)}=1$, $\widehat f_{(0,5,0)}=-i/2$ and $\widehat f_{(0,-5,0)}=i/2$; all other coefficients vanish because $|n_i|\le5\lt8=N/2$ and Proposition 1.3(ii) applies. The sum of squared moduli is $1+1+\tfrac14+\tfrac14=2.5$, and the grid mean of $f^2$ is $2+\tfrac12+0=2.5$. Raw fftn returns $N^3\widehat f=4096$ at $(3,0,0)$. The mode $n=9$ lies outside $-8\le n\lt8$; on the grid $e^{9iy_j}=e^{-7iy_j}$, so $\sin(9y)$ is sampled as $-\sin(7y)$ and is reported at $|n|=7$. This is aliasing, and the Nyquist bound of self-test question 7 is $\pi N/\mathcal L=8$.

Exercise 1.6 — convert a modeling-edition formula before using it

A student takes the modeling edition's transform of the Gaussian kernel, $e^{-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24}$, and inserts it as $\mathscr F\{G\}$ into the course convolution identity written as $\widehat{\overline f}=(2\pi)^d\mathscr F\{G\}\,\widehat f$ in three dimensions. By what factor is the filtered amplitude wrong, and what is the correct statement?

Solution

The course identity $\widehat{\overline f}=(2\pi)^3\mathscr F\{G\}\,\widehat f$ needs the course transform $\mathscr F\{G\}=(2\pi)^{-3}e^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Delta^2/24}$. Inserting the modeling transform in its place multiplies every filtered amplitude by $(2\pi)^3\approx248.05$, so at $\boldsymbol\kappa=\mathbf 0$ the filter would multiply the mean by $248.05$ instead of preserving it. The correct statement is Proposition 1.2: $\widehat{\overline f}=\hat G\,\widehat f$ with $\hat G=e^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Delta^2/24}$, which needs no $2\pi$ because $\hat G$ is the same function in both editions. The check that the filter preserves the mean, $\widehat{\overline f}(\mathbf 0)=\widehat f(\mathbf 0)$, detects the error.

Exercise 1.7 — relate three clocks

A diffusion forward process is written $X_\tau=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau}\,z+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_\tau}\,\boldsymbol\xi$ with $\bar\alpha_0=1$, $\bar\alpha_1=0$ and $\bar\alpha_\tau$ decreasing. (a) Write it as a course probability path $X_t=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\boldsymbol\xi$, giving $\alpha_t$, $\beta_t$ and the relation between $t$ and $\tau$. (b) A Gaussian kernel of variance $\Delta^2/12$ per dimension equals the heat semigroup of diffusivity $\nu$ run for a time $s$ with variance $2\nu s$ per dimension. Express $s$ in terms of $\Delta$ and $\nu$ and check the transfer functions.

Solution

(a) The forward process starts at data when $\tau=0$ and ends at noise when $\tau=1$, while the course path starts at noise when $t=0$. Set $t=1-\tau$. Then $\alpha_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{1-t}}$ and $\beta_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{1-t}}$, which satisfy $\alpha_0=0$, $\beta_0=1$, $\alpha_1=1$, $\beta_1=0$, the endpoint conditions of MIT chapter 2 §2.2. (b) Equating variances, $\Delta^2/12=2\nu s$, so $s=\Delta^2/(24\nu)$ and $\Delta^2=24\nu s$. The heat semigroup multiplies the mode $\boldsymbol\kappa$ by $e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}=e^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Delta^2/24}$, which is the Gaussian transfer function of Table 19.1. Chapter 4 proves the equality of the two operators; the variance match alone shows only that their second moments agree.

This chapter establishes the route of the course, the prerequisites it assumes, one binding meaning for every symbol and a translation for each of the conflicts found on the route. It proves that the two Fourier conventions of the turbulence editions differ by $(2\pi)^d$ in the forward transform and by $(2\pi)^{2d}$ in the Parseval weight, that filter transfer functions are the same function in both, and that the course's discrete normalization fftn/N**3 returns Fourier-series coefficients with unit weight. The program checks the Gaussian transfer function, the Parseval identity, the mismatched inverse and the discrete coefficients against closed-form values, to the digits it prints; the ratio between its two forward transforms holds by construction. It does not teach any of the subjects: the kinematics, flow matching and turbulence statistics are taught by the linked chapters, and the four-part partition and the week assignment of outcomes are a plan, not evidence about learning.
