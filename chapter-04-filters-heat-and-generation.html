4. Filters, heat and generation

A large-eddy simulation smooths a velocity field with a Gaussian filter of width $\Delta$; an inverse-heat-dissipation generative model smooths an image by running the heat equation and then learns to undo the smoothing. The source chapters on filtering never connect the width to a diffusion time, and the generative paper, which identifies its blur with a Gaussian convolution and cites the Gaussian scale-space literature, does not mention large-eddy filters or turbulence. This chapter proves that the two operations are identical, with heat time $s=\Delta^2/(24\nu)$; that the Gaussian is the only family in the course's filter table with this property; that undoing it multiplies Fourier mode $\boldsymbol\kappa$ by $e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$, without bound; and that two devices cap that growth: added noise in the generative model, under a Gaussian model of the data, and truncation of the van Cittert inverse series in deconvolution.

From a filter width to a reversible-looking process that cannot be reversed. Identify Gaussian filtering with heat flow and convert widths to times. Classify the five filters of essentials Table 19.1 as heat semigroups, other semigroups, or no semigroup at all. Measure how fast the backward problem amplifies a perturbation, then see how noise turns each reverse step of a generative model into a regression whose optimal gain is bounded under a Gaussian model of the data. Compare the order in which blur and additive noise remove wavenumbers from a turbulence spectrum, and finish with the deconvolution operators of large-eddy simulation, which face the same instability.

4.1 A filter family is a heat flow only if its widths compose inside it

Work on the periodic box $[0,\mathcal L)^d$, with $d=1$ or $3$, or on $\mathbb R^d$ where stated. A homogeneous filter replaces a field $f$ by the convolution $\bar f=G*f$, $\bar f(\mathbf x)=\int G(\mathbf r)f(\mathbf x-\mathbf r)\,d\mathbf r$, with kernel normalized by $\int G\,d\mathbf r=1$, as in essentials (19.2)–(19.3). Its transfer function is $\hat G(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\int G(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}d\mathbf r$, which is $(2\pi)^d$ times the course's forward transform of $G$ and therefore equals one at $\boldsymbol\kappa=0$. With the course convention $\hat f(\boldsymbol\kappa)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int f e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}d\mathbf x$, the convolution theorem reads $\widehat{G*f}=\hat G\hat f$ with no extra constant; this is essentials (19.9). The modeling edition puts no prefactor on the forward transform (§20.1) but defines the transfer function by the same integral, so every transfer function below is identical in the two editions. On the periodic box the same statements hold mode by mode at the lattice wavenumbers $\boldsymbol\kappa\in(2\pi/\mathcal L)\mathbb Z^d$.

Essentials Table 19.1 lists five one-dimensional families, each indexed by a width $\Delta\gt0$ with characteristic wavenumber $\kappa_c=\pi/\Delta$:

$$\hat G^{\rm box}_\Delta=\frac{\sin(\kappa\Delta/2)}{\kappa\Delta/2},\quad \hat G^{\rm gauss}_\Delta=e^{-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24},\quad \hat G^{\rm sharp}_\Delta=H(\kappa_c-|\kappa|),\quad \hat G^{\rm cauchy}_\Delta=e^{-a\Delta|\kappa|},\quad \hat G^{\rm pao}_\Delta=e^{-\pi^{2/3}(\Delta|\kappa|)^{4/3}/24},$$

with $a=\pi/24$ and $H$ the Heaviside function; essentials leaves $H(0)$ open, and here $H(0)$ is 0 or 1. The box and Gaussian kernels have the same second moment $\Delta^2/12$; the Gaussian, Cauchy and Pao transfer functions agree at $\kappa_c$, where each equals $e^{-\pi^2/24}=0.6628$. These matching rules fix what the common symbol $\Delta$ means for each family; they do not make the families act alike at other wavenumbers.

The heat equation used throughout is $\partial_sf=\nu\nabla^2f$, where $s\ge0$ is the heat (blur) time of the course notation, $\nu\gt0$ is the diffusivity of this equation, and $\nabla^2$ is the Laplacian. The symbol $\Delta$ is reserved for the filter width and never denotes the Laplacian here. Only the product $\nu s$, which has units of length squared, enters any result below; $\nu$ acquires a physical value only when an example chooses one.

Definition 4.1 — semigroup and heat semigroup. A family of filters $\{T(s)\}_{s\ge0}$ is a one-parameter semigroup if $T(0)$ is the identity, $T(s_1)T(s_2)=T(s_1+s_2)$ for all $s_1,s_2\ge0$, and $s\mapsto T(s)$ is injective. A filter family $\{G_\Delta\}_{\Delta\gt0}$ is a semigroup if some strictly increasing bijection $\Delta\mapsto s(\Delta)$ of $(0,\infty)$ makes $T(s(\Delta))=G_\Delta*$ a one-parameter semigroup. It is a heat semigroup if, in addition, $\hat G_\Delta(\boldsymbol\kappa)=e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s(\Delta)}$ for every $\boldsymbol\kappa$ and some fixed $\nu\gt0$.

Injectivity excludes a trivial semigroup that stops changing, and closure of the family under composition is a necessary condition: if $G_{\Delta_1}*G_{\Delta_2}$ is not itself a member of the family, no reparametrization can produce the semigroup law. Essentials Exercise 3(a) proves the equal-width case $\sqrt2\Delta$, and modeling §20.4 states the combined width $\sqrt{\Delta_1^2+\Delta_2^2}$ and its grid-and-test-filter case; neither names the semigroup structure or relates $\Delta$ to a time. Sections 4.2–4.5 supply both.

4.2 Heat flow for a time s is convolution with a Gaussian of variance 2νs per coordinate

Define the heat kernel, for $s\gt0$,

$$K_s(\mathbf r)=(4\pi\nu s)^{-d/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{|\mathbf r|^2}{4\nu s}\right).$$

It is the density of a centered Gaussian with covariance $2\nu s\,I_d$, so it is nonnegative, integrates to one, and has second moment $2\nu s$ in each coordinate. The following proposition identifies convolution with $K_s$ as the solution operator of the heat equation.

Proposition 4.1 — heat flow is Gaussian convolution. (a) For every $\boldsymbol\kappa\in\mathbb R^d$, $\hat K_s(\boldsymbol\kappa)=e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$. (b) On the periodic box, let $f_0\in L^2$ and let $f(\cdot,s)$ be a solution of $\partial_sf=\nu\nabla^2f$ whose Fourier coefficients $\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}(s)$ are continuous on $[0,\infty)$, differentiable for $s\gt0$, and satisfy the equation mode by mode, with $f(\cdot,0)=f_0$. Then $\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}(s)=e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}\hat f_{0,\boldsymbol\kappa}$, the solution is unique in this class, and $f(\cdot,s)=K_s^{\rm per}*f_0$ with the periodized kernel $K_s^{\rm per}(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf m\in\mathbb Z^d}K_s(\mathbf r+\mathcal L\mathbf m)$.

Proof. (a) The kernel factorizes into $d$ one-dimensional Gaussians and $e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}$ factorizes likewise, so it suffices to treat $d=1$. Completing the square,

$$-\frac{r^2}{4\nu s}-i\kappa r=-\frac{(r+2i\nu s\kappa)^2}{4\nu s}-\nu s\kappa^2.$$

The function $e^{-z^2/(4\nu s)}$ is entire, and its integral along the horizontal line $\operatorname{Im}z=2\nu s\kappa$ equals its integral along the real axis, because the integrals over the two vertical segments at $\operatorname{Re}z=\pm R$ are bounded in modulus by $(4\pi\nu s)^{-1/2}\,2\nu s|\kappa|\,e^{-(R^2-4\nu^2s^2\kappa^2)/(4\nu s)}$ and vanish as $R\to\infty$. The real-axis integral of $(4\pi\nu s)^{-1/2}e^{-r^2/(4\nu s)}$ is one. Hence $\hat K_s(\kappa)=e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}$.

(b) Each Fourier mode satisfies $\nabla^2e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}=-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}$, so the equation for mode $\boldsymbol\kappa$ is the scalar linear ODE $d\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}/ds=-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}$. Its unique solution with the given initial value is $e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}\hat f_{0,\boldsymbol\kappa}$: the difference of two solutions satisfies the same ODE with zero initial value, and multiplying it by $e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$ gives a function with zero derivative and zero initial value. Since $|e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}|\le1$, the coefficients remain square-summable and define an $L^2$ field. Finally, periodic convolution with $K_s^{\rm per}$ acts on $e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}$ by the factor $\int_{[0,\mathcal L)^d}K_s^{\rm per}(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}d\mathbf r$. Unfolding the sum over images turns this into $\int_{\mathbb R^d}K_s(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}d\mathbf r$, because $e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot \mathcal L\mathbf m}=1$ for lattice wavenumbers; by (a) the factor is $e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$. Both operators therefore multiply every coefficient by the same number. ∎

From here on $T(s)$ denotes this solution operator, $T(s)f_0=f(\cdot,s)$, with $T(0)$ the identity. On $\mathbb R^d$ the formula $f(\cdot,s)=K_s*f_0$ also solves the equation for bounded continuous or square-integrable data, by part (a) and differentiation under the integral. Uniqueness on the whole space, however, requires a growth restriction: without one, Tychonoff's classical construction gives nonzero solutions with zero initial data that grow faster than $e^{c|\mathbf x|^2}$. Uniqueness among bounded solutions is a standard maximum-principle result that is quoted here, not proved. The periodic statement in (b), which is the one the program and the turbulence data use, needs no such qualification.

The inverse-heat-dissipation model of Rissanen, Heinonen and Solin (ICLR 2023) writes its PDE (1) as $\partial_tu=\Delta u$ on the image, with Neumann (zero-derivative) boundaries. In course notation their $t$ is the heat time $s$ with $\nu=1$, their $\Delta$ is $\nabla^2$, and their image $u$ is the field $f$ here; the course's $u_t$ remains reserved for the velocity of a probability flow. They state that on an infinite plane running the heat equation to time $t$ equals Gaussian convolution with variance $\sigma_B^2=2t$, which is Proposition 4.1 with $2\nu s=2t$; on their bounded image with Neumann boundaries the corresponding statement is the per-mode one below. Their solution (2), $\mathbf u(t)=\mathbf F(t)\mathbf u(0)=\mathbf V\exp(\boldsymbol\Lambda t)\mathbf V^\top\mathbf u(0)$, uses the cosine (DCT) eigenbasis of the Neumann Laplacian with eigenvalues $-\lambda_{n,m}=-\pi^2(n^2/W^2+m^2/H^2)$ for an image of width $W$ and height $H$. Their $\mathbf F(t)$ is the Neumann heat-flow operator with $\nu=1$, not the deformation gradient of the course notation. The periodic Fourier basis of this course has eigenvalues $-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2$ with $\kappa_j=2\pi n_j/\mathcal L$. The two settings carry the same per-mode law: extending a Neumann solution on $[0,W]$ evenly to $[-W,W]$ gives a periodic solution of period $2W$ whose modes are $\cos(n\pi x/W)$ with wavenumber $\kappa=n\pi/W$, so $\lambda_{n,m}$ is $|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2$ for those wavenumbers. Every per-mode statement below transfers to their basis with this identification.

4.3 The filter width and the heat time satisfy Δ² = 24νs

Corollary 4.2 — width–time relation. The Gaussian filter of width $\Delta$ in essentials Table 19.1 is the heat flow $T(s)$ with

$$\Delta^2=24\,\nu s,\qquad e^{-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24}=e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}\quad\text{for every }\kappa.$$

In $d$ dimensions the isotropic Gaussian filter of width $\Delta$, whose kernel is the product of $d$ one-dimensional kernels, is $T(s)$ with the same $s$.

Proof. The table's kernel $(6/(\pi\Delta^2))^{1/2}\exp(-6r^2/\Delta^2)$ is the normal density with variance $\Delta^2/12$ (essentials chapter 19 states $\sigma^2=\Delta^2/12$ with Table 19.1, and Exercise 3(a) uses it). By Proposition 4.1 the heat kernel is the normal density with variance $2\nu s$. Two centered normal densities are equal if and only if their variances are equal, so the kernels coincide exactly when $\Delta^2/12=2\nu s$. The transfer functions give the same condition, since $\kappa^2\Delta^2/24=\nu\kappa^2s$ for all $\kappa$ exactly when $\Delta^2=24\nu s$. In $d$ dimensions the product kernel has transfer $\prod_je^{-\kappa_j^2\Delta^2/24}=e^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Delta^2/24}$, which is $e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$ under the same condition. ∎

The identification is exact, not a second-moment match. The box filter also has second moment $\Delta^2/12$, and to second order in $\kappa$ its transfer function agrees with the Gaussian one (essentials Exercise 1), yet Section 4.5 proves that no heat time reproduces it. At the characteristic wavenumber, $\nu\kappa_c^2s=\pi^2/24$, so the transfer value $0.6628$ of the table is $e^{-\pi^2/24}$ read as heat decay. With grid spacing $h$, $\Delta=2h$ corresponds to $\nu s=h^2/6$ and $\Delta=4h$ to $\nu s=2h^2/3$: doubling the width quadruples the heat time.

Worked example 4.1 — the heat time of a large-eddy filter. Essentials Exercise 4 finds that for the air-jet model spectrum a Gaussian filter of width $\Delta=0.932\,\ell_{EI}=12.95\ \mathrm{mm}$ resolves 80 % of the kinetic energy, where $\ell_{EI}=L_{11}/6=13.90\ \mathrm{mm}$ is that chapter's length at the boundary between the energy-containing and inertial ranges and $L_{11}$ is its longitudinal integral length. Choose $\nu$ to be the kinematic viscosity of air used there, $1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$, which gives $s$ a physical reading. Then

$$s=\frac{\Delta^2}{24\nu}=\frac{(12.95\times10^{-3})^2}{24\times1.4607\times10^{-5}}\ \mathrm{s}=0.4784\ \mathrm{s},$$

while the large-eddy time of the same flow is $k/\varepsilon=6/80=0.0750\ \mathrm s$. Molecular diffusion acting alone on a frozen field would need $0.4784/0.0750=6.38$ large-eddy times to smooth it as much as the filter does. The turbulent field does not stay frozen for that long, so the filter is not a model of molecular smoothing in the flow. The relation $\Delta^2=24\nu s$ is a statement about operators; the time $s$ is a coordinate along a family of filters, and any value of $\nu$ only sets its unit.

4.4 Composing Gaussian filters adds heat times

Proposition 4.3 — Gaussian composition. For widths $\Delta_1,\Delta_2\gt0$, $G^{\rm gauss}_{\Delta_1}*G^{\rm gauss}_{\Delta_2}=G^{\rm gauss}_{\Delta}$ with $\Delta^2=\Delta_1^2+\Delta_2^2$. Equivalently, with $s_j=\Delta_j^2/(24\nu)$, $T(s_1)T(s_2)=T(s_1+s_2)$, and $s\mapsto T(s)$ is a one-parameter semigroup. The Gaussian family is a heat semigroup with $s(\Delta)=\Delta^2/(24\nu)$.

Proof. By the convolution theorem the composite transfer is $e^{-\kappa^2\Delta_1^2/24}e^{-\kappa^2\Delta_2^2/24}=e^{-\kappa^2(\Delta_1^2+\Delta_2^2)/24}$, the Gaussian transfer of width $\sqrt{\Delta_1^2+\Delta_2^2}$. Since a kernel with an integrable transfer function is determined by it, the kernels agree. In heat time the exponents add: $\nu\kappa^2s_1+\nu\kappa^2s_2=\nu\kappa^2(s_1+s_2)$. $T(0)$ has transfer one and is the identity. For $\kappa\ne0$ the transfer $e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}$ is strictly decreasing in $s$, so distinct times give distinct operators. The map $\Delta\mapsto\Delta^2/(24\nu)$ is a strictly increasing bijection of $(0,\infty)$, and Corollary 4.2 supplies the heat form. ∎

The semigroup is additive in $s$, not in $\Delta$: widths add in quadrature. Filtering twice with width $\Delta$ gives $\sqrt2\Delta$ (essentials Exercise 3(a)); a grid filter $\Delta$ followed by a test filter $2\Delta$ gives $\sqrt5\Delta$, as modeling §20.4 notes, which is a heat time $5\Delta^2/(24\nu)$. Composition also commutes, since the transfer functions are multiplied. The program composes the circulant matrices of two periodized Gaussian kernels with $\Delta_1=2\pi/16$ and $\Delta_2=2\pi/8$ on 256 points and finds a relative difference of $1.44\times10^{-15}$ from the single kernel of width $\sqrt{\Delta_1^2+\Delta_2^2}$.

4.5 Only the Gaussian family in the table is a heat semigroup

The question has three possible answers for each family: heat semigroup, semigroup with a different generator, or no semigroup. All three occur in Table 19.1.

Proposition 4.4 — classification of the filter table. Under the transfer functions of Section 4.1, on $\mathbb R$ or on a periodic interval:

  1. The Gaussian family is a heat semigroup (Proposition 4.3).
  2. The Cauchy family is a semigroup with $s(\Delta)=\Delta$ and multiplier $e^{-a|\kappa|s}$; it is not a heat semigroup.
  3. The Pao family is a semigroup with $s(\Delta)=\Delta^{4/3}$ and multiplier $e^{-\pi^{2/3}|\kappa|^{4/3}s/24}$; it is not a heat semigroup.
  4. The box family is not closed under composition and is therefore not a semigroup.
  5. The sharp spectral family is closed under composition, with $G_{\Delta_1}*G_{\Delta_2}=G_{\max(\Delta_1,\Delta_2)}$, but it is not a one-parameter semigroup under any parametrization and is not a heat semigroup.

Proof. (2) Multiplying transfers gives $e^{-a\Delta_1|\kappa|}e^{-a\Delta_2|\kappa|}=e^{-a(\Delta_1+\Delta_2)|\kappa|}$, so $s=\Delta$ satisfies the semigroup law; the transfer at any $\kappa\ne0$ is strictly decreasing in $\Delta$, which gives injectivity. If the family were a heat semigroup, then for each $\Delta$ some $s$ would satisfy $a\Delta|\kappa|=\nu s\kappa^2$ for every $\kappa$. At a wavenumber $\kappa\gt0$ of the domain and at $2\kappa$ this gives $a\Delta\kappa=\nu s\kappa^2$ and $2a\Delta\kappa=4\nu s\kappa^2$, hence $\nu s=0$ and $a\Delta=0$, which contradicts $\Delta\gt0$.

(3) The same multiplication gives the law $\Delta^{4/3}=\Delta_1^{4/3}+\Delta_2^{4/3}$, so $s=\Delta^{4/3}$ works and is injective for the same reason. A heat form would need $c|\kappa|^{4/3}=\nu s\kappa^2$ with $c=\pi^{2/3}\Delta^{4/3}/24\gt0$; at a wavenumber $\kappa\gt0$ of the domain and at $2\kappa$ this gives $c\kappa^{4/3}=\nu s\kappa^2$ and $2^{4/3}c\kappa^{4/3}=4\nu s\kappa^2$, so $2^{4/3}=4$, which is false.

(4) The self-composition $G^{\rm box}_\Delta*G^{\rm box}_\Delta$ has transfer $\operatorname{sinc}^2(\kappa\Delta/2)\ge0$ for every $\kappa$, with $\operatorname{sinc}z=\sin z/z$. Every box transfer $\operatorname{sinc}(\kappa b/2)$ with width $b\gt0$ takes the negative value $\operatorname{sinc}z_*=-0.217234$ at $\kappa=2z_*/b$, where $z_*\approx4.4934$ is the first positive root of $\tan z=z$, the first interior minimum of $\operatorname{sinc}$. A nonnegative function cannot equal a function that is negative somewhere, so the composite is not a box of any width. The family is not closed, and no reparametrization can satisfy the semigroup law within it. On $\mathbb R$ there is a second obstruction to the heat form: $\operatorname{sinc}(\kappa\Delta/2)=0$ at $\kappa\Delta=2\pi$, while $e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}\gt0$ everywhere.

On a periodic interval of length $\mathcal L$ only the lattice wavenumbers $\kappa_n=2\pi n/\mathcal L$ exist, and $2z_*/b$ need not be one of them, so the argument changes. Take $\Delta/\mathcal L\notin\mathbb Z$. The composite transfer $\operatorname{sinc}^2(\pi n\Delta/\mathcal L)$ is positive at $n=1$ and at most $(\mathcal L/(\pi n\Delta))^2$ for every $n\ge1$. A box of width $b$ with $b/\mathcal L\in\mathbb Z$ has transfer zero at every $n\ge1$, which differs from the composite at $n=1$. If $b/\mathcal L\notin\mathbb Z$, then $\sin(\pi nb/\mathcal L)$ does not tend to zero as $n\to\infty$: otherwise $nb/\mathcal L$ and $(n+1)b/\mathcal L$ would both approach integers, and so would their difference, the fixed non-integer $b/\mathcal L$. Hence $|\sin(\pi nb/\mathcal L)|\ge c\gt0$ for infinitely many $n$, so the box transfer has modulus at least $c\mathcal L/(\pi nb)$ at those $n$, which exceeds $(\mathcal L/(\pi n\Delta))^2$ once $n$ is large. The composite is therefore no box of any width. The exception $\Delta/\mathcal L\in\mathbb Z$ is the projection onto the mean, with $G_{\mathcal L}*G_{\mathcal L}=G_{\mathcal L}$; it does not restore closure of the family.

(5) The product of $H(\kappa_{c1}-|\kappa|)$ and $H(\kappa_{c2}-|\kappa|)$ is $H(\min(\kappa_{c1},\kappa_{c2})-|\kappa|)$, the sharp filter of the larger width, which proves closure. Suppose an injective map $s\mapsto T(s)$ with $T(s_1)T(s_2)=T(s_1+s_2)$ took values in this family. Every member is a projection, since its transfer satisfies $\hat G^2=\hat G$, so $T(s)=T(s)^2=T(2s)$ for $s\gt0$; this contradicts injectivity because $2s\ne s$. Finally, a sharp transfer takes only the values $0$ and $1$, whereas $e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}\in(0,1)$ for $\kappa\ne0$ and $s\gt0$. ∎

The proof uses only transfer functions. Items (2), (3) and (5) carry over to a periodic grid that holds the modes $\kappa$ and $2\kappa$; the periodic argument for item (4) needs arbitrarily large lattice wavenumbers, so it is not claimed for a finite grid. The real-line argument extends to the spherical box of essentials (19.20), whose transfer $3(\sin x-x\cos x)/x^3$, $x=\kappa\Delta/2$, vanishes where $\tan x=x$ (Exercise 4.3). The program measures the box gap directly: over all box widths from $0.5\Delta$ to $3\Delta$, the smallest supremum distance on $0\lt\kappa\Delta\le6\pi$ between $\operatorname{sinc}^2(\kappa\Delta/2)$ and $\operatorname{sinc}(\kappa b/2)$ is $0.2172$. It equals $|\operatorname{sinc}z_*|$ to four digits, because the proof's lower bound is attained when the negative lobe of the candidate meets the zero of $\operatorname{sinc}^2$.

The Cauchy and Pao semigroups are generated by multipliers $-a|\kappa|$ and $-(\pi^{2/3}/24)|\kappa|^{4/3}$, that is, by the fractional powers $(-\nabla^2)^{1/2}$ and $(-\nabla^2)^{2/3}$ of the Laplacian; only the Gaussian generator $\nu\nabla^2$ is a differential operator. The semigroup property, which the sharp family lacks, has a practical consequence. Inverse heat dissipation defines the noise-free part of its intermediate states by $\mathbf u_k=\mathbf F(t_k)\mathbf u_0$; because $\mathbf F$ is a semigroup, $\mathbf F(t_k)=\mathbf F(t_k-t_{k-1})\mathbf F(t_{k-1})$ and each forward step is itself a blur. A sharp-cutoff family would instead give nested projections, $G_{\Delta_k}G_{\Delta_{k-1}}=G_{\Delta_k}$, in which each step deletes a band of modes outright; that is a different forward process, whose reverse steps receive no information about the deleted band.

4.6 Running the heat equation backward amplifies each mode exponentially in its squared wavenumber

A blurred field determines its source: by Proposition 4.1 the forward map multiplies every coefficient by the strictly positive number $e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$, so it is injective, and in exact arithmetic blur destroys no information. The inverse, defined on the range, multiplies coefficient $\boldsymbol\kappa$ by $e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$. Measure fields on the periodic box by the mean-square norm $\|f\|^2=\mathcal L^{-d}\int|f|^2d\mathbf x=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2$, which is Parseval's identity in the course convention.

Proposition 4.5 — the backward heat problem is ill-posed. Fix $s\gt0$. (a) For every $\gamma\gt0$ and $M\gt0$ there is a perturbation $w$ of the data with $\|w\|=\gamma$ whose backward image $T(s)^{-1}w$ has norm greater than $M$. (b) Data $g\in L^2$ have a backward solution in $L^2$ if and only if $\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}e^{2\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}|\hat g_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2\lt\infty$; square-integrable data exist that violate this.

Proof. (a) Take $w=\gamma e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}$ with $|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\gt\log(M/\gamma)/(\nu s)$. Its norm is $\gamma$, and its backward image $\gamma e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}$ has norm $\gamma e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}\gt M$. (b) A backward solution $f_0$ must have coefficients $e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}\hat g_{\boldsymbol\kappa}$, by the uniqueness in Proposition 4.1, and it lies in $L^2$ exactly when these are square-summable. In one dimension on $[0,2\pi)$ the square wave $\operatorname{sign}(\sin x)$ has coefficients of modulus $2/(\pi|\kappa|)$ at odd $\kappa$, which are square-summable, while $e^{2\nu\kappa^2s}\cdot4/(\pi^2\kappa^2)$ grows without bound. ∎

Hadamard's conditions for a well-posed problem are existence, uniqueness and continuous dependence on the data. Part (a) is the failure of continuous dependence; part (b) is the failure of existence for data outside the range; uniqueness holds, by Proposition 4.1. The two failures combine: any data perturbation of size $\sigma$ per mode, from measurement, storage or roundoff, is multiplied by $e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$. A mode can then be recovered to unit relative accuracy only where its blurred amplitude $e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}|\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|$ exceeds $\sigma$. Information loss under blur is therefore conditional on a noise floor; without a floor there is none.

On a grid with spacing $h$ the largest wavenumber in each direction is the Nyquist value $\pi/h$. With $\nu s=\Delta^2/24$ the amplification there is

$$e^{\nu(\pi/h)^2s}=\exp\!\left(\frac{\pi^2}{24}\Big(\frac{\Delta}{h}\Big)^2\right),$$

which depends only on the number of grid spacings per filter width and not on the number of points. The program evaluates it as $1.5087$, $5.1807$, $720.35$ and $2.6926\times10^{11}$ for $\Delta/h=1,2,4,8$. In three dimensions the corner of the grid's wavenumber cube has $|\boldsymbol\kappa|=\sqrt3\pi/h$ and the exponent triples: at $\Delta/h=4$ the corner factor is $720.35^3=3.74\times10^8$. Section 4.9 reads these factors at the grid ratios used in large-eddy simulation.

The program applies the inversion to a unit-rms periodic signal on 64 points with a $\kappa^{-5/3}$ per-mode spectrum, blurred and then perturbed by independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation $10^{-3}$ at each point. At $\Delta=4h$ the blurred data differ from the signal by a relative error of $0.1736$, and the exact inverse returns relative error $0.0984$: the factor 720 applied to a noise of $10^{-3}$ still leaves an error below that of the blurred data. At $\Delta=8h$ the data error is $0.2472$ and the exact inverse returns $2.352\times10^7$. With the same noise, doubling $\Delta/h$ from 4 to 8 raises the relative error of the exact inverse from $0.0984$ to $2.352\times10^7$, a factor of $2.39\times10^8$.

4.7 With added noise each reverse step is a regression whose optimal gain is bounded under a Gaussian data model

Rissanen et al. define the forward process by (3), $q(\mathbf u_{1:K}\mid\mathbf u_0)=\prod_k\mathcal N(\mathbf u_k\mid\mathbf F(t_k)\mathbf u_0,\sigma^2\mathbf I)$, with $\sigma$ "a small constant (e.g., 0.01 if data is scaled to [0,1])". The noise is needed to "formally break the reversibility" and make the reverse conditionals well defined; without it "the model fails even on MNIST". The reverse model (4) is $p_\theta(\mathbf u_{k-1}\mid\mathbf u_k)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_\theta(\mathbf u_k,k),\delta^2\mathbf I)$ with $\boldsymbol\mu_\theta=\mathbf u_k+f_\theta(\mathbf u_k,k)$, trained by the loss $\|\boldsymbol\mu_\theta(\hat{\mathbf u}_k,k)-\mathbf u_{k-1}\|_2^2$ with $\hat{\mathbf u}_k=\mathbf u_k+\boldsymbol\varepsilon$. In that loss, as their Algorithm 1 sets it out, $\mathbf u_k=\mathbf F(t_k)\mathbf u_0$ and $\mathbf u_{k-1}=\mathbf F(t_{k-1})\mathbf u_0$ are noise-free, so the noise enters only through $\hat{\mathbf u}_k$, whereas in (3) $\mathbf u_k$ is the noisy state. They report $\delta/\sigma\approx1.25$–$1.3$ and $K=100$ (MNIST), $200$ (CIFAR-10, AFHQ, FFHQ) and $400$ (LSUN) steps. In the course notation their noise $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ is $\sigma\boldsymbol\xi$ with $\boldsymbol\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$; $\delta$ here is their sampling standard deviation, not a width, $k$ is their step index, not the kinetic energy of Worked example 4.1, $K$ is their number of steps, not the heat kernel $K_s$, and $t_k$ is a heat time, not a generation time. How they draw the initial state $\mathbf u_K$, and their time schedule, were not verified and are not described here.

Without noise, $\mathbf u_k=\mathbf F(t_k)\mathbf u_0$ is a deterministic, invertible function of $\mathbf u_0$. The conditional law of $\mathbf u_{k-1}$ given $\mathbf u_k$ is then the point mass at $\mathbf F(t_{k-1})\mathbf F(t_k)^{-1}\mathbf u_k$, a backward heat step of duration $t_k-t_{k-1}$ that multiplies mode $(n,m)$ by $e^{\lambda_{n,m}(t_k-t_{k-1})}$. A Gaussian reverse model with $\delta\gt0$ would be fitted to a degenerate target, and composing the reverse steps would reproduce the full amplification of Proposition 4.5. The noisy forward process replaces this target by a regression whose optimum can be computed.

Write $a_k=e^{-\lambda_{n,m}t_k}$ for the blur factor of the mode $(n,m)$ under consideration, whose Laplacian eigenvalue is $-\lambda_{n,m}$; the bare symbol $\lambda$ is kept for the spectral slope of Section 4.8. The squared-error loss is minimized over all measurable predictors by the conditional mean $\boldsymbol\mu^*(\mathbf y,k)=\mathbb E[\mathbf u_{k-1}\mid\hat{\mathbf u}_k=\mathbf y]$; this is the regression-projection identity of MIT 6.S184 chapter 3, §3.3, applied to the target $\mathbf u_{k-1}$ in place of a score. To compute it, assume a model of the data that is convenient rather than true.

Proposition 4.6 — the noisy reverse step has bounded gain. Suppose the modal coefficients $z$ of $\mathbf u_0$ in the orthonormal Laplacian eigenbasis are independent, centered and Gaussian, with variance $S$ for the mode considered, and that the added noise $\sigma\boldsymbol\xi$ is independent of $\mathbf u_0$, so that its modal coefficients are independent $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. Then $\boldsymbol\mu^*$ acts on that mode by multiplication with

$$g=\frac{a_{k-1}a_kS}{a_k^2S+\sigma^2},\qquad 0\le g\le\min\!\left(\frac{a_{k-1}}{a_k},\ \frac{a_{k-1}\sqrt S}{2\sigma}\right),$$

and the predicted coefficient has variance $a_{k-1}^2S\cdot a_k^2S/(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)\le a_{k-1}^2S$.

Proof. For the mode considered, the observation is $y=a_kz+e$ with $e\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ independent of $z$, and the target is $a_{k-1}z$. The pair $(z,y)$ is jointly Gaussian with zero mean, $\operatorname{Var}y=a_k^2S+\sigma^2$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(z,y)=a_kS$. For a centered Gaussian pair, $z-[\operatorname{Cov}(z,y)/\operatorname{Var}y]\,y$ is uncorrelated with $y$, hence independent of it, so $\mathbb E[z\mid y]=a_kSy/(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)$. Multiplying by $a_{k-1}$ gives $g$. Independence across modes makes the conditional mean act mode by mode. The first bound follows from $a_k^2S+\sigma^2\ge a_k^2S$. The second follows from $a_k^2S+\sigma^2\ge2a_k\sqrt S\sigma$, the inequality between arithmetic and geometric means, with equality when $a_k\sqrt S=\sigma$. The variance of $gy$ is $g^2(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)=a_{k-1}^2a_k^2S^2/(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)$, which is at most $a_{k-1}^2S$. ∎

Three regimes follow. Where the blurred mode stands well above the noise, $a_k\sqrt S\gg\sigma$, the gain approaches $a_{k-1}/a_k$ and the reverse step is the backward heat step. Where the mode is buried, $a_k\sqrt S\ll\sigma$, the gain is approximately $a_{k-1}a_kS/\sigma^2$, which tends to zero: the optimal step returns the prior mean instead of amplifying noise. Between them the gain never exceeds $a_{k-1}\sqrt S/(2\sigma)$, however strongly the mode was blurred, and the bound is attained when the blurred standard deviation $a_k\sqrt S$ equals the noise standard deviation $\sigma$. For a buried mode under this prior, the reverse model (4) has one remaining source of content: the Gaussian term with standard deviation $\delta$, added at every step.

The bound is a theorem only for the Gaussian modal prior assumed in Proposition 4.6. A trained $\boldsymbol\mu_\theta$ approximates the posterior mean of the real data law, which is not Gaussian, so Proposition 4.6 does not bound its gain. The structure is the one met in MIT chapter 3, §3.2, where the posterior mean $D_t$ is the optimal denoiser of an additive-noise path: here the optimal predictor is a posterior mean that deblurs and denoises in one step. The program evaluates the bound in the full deconvolution case $a_{k-1}=1$, where $g$ is the gain that signal processing calls the Wiener gain. On the 64-point signal, the largest ratio of gain to the per-mode bound $\sqrt S/(2\sigma_{\rm mode})$, where $\sigma_{\rm mode}=\sqrt{64}\,\sigma$ is the standard deviation of one Fourier coefficient of the pointwise noise in the program's unnormalized real-FFT convention, is $0.9871$ at $\Delta=4h$ and $1.0000$ to four digits at $\Delta=8h$, where one mode lies close to the equality point.

4.8 Blur and additive noise remove wavenumbers in different orders

Describe a statistically homogeneous field by its per-mode spectral density $\Phi(\boldsymbol\kappa)$, the expected value of $|\hat f_{\boldsymbol\kappa}|^2$. For an isotropic three-dimensional velocity field with energy spectrum $E(\kappa)\propto\kappa^{-5/3}$, a shell of radius $\kappa$ and unit thickness holds a number of lattice modes proportional to $\kappa^2$, so $\Phi\propto E/\kappa^2\propto\kappa^{-11/3}$. For a one-dimensional record whose spectrum falls as $\kappa^{-5/3}$, $\Phi\propto\kappa^{-5/3}$. Write $\Phi(\kappa)=C\kappa^{-\lambda}$ with $\lambda=11/3$ or $5/3$; $\Phi(\kappa)$ is the modal variance $S$ of Proposition 4.6 at wavenumber $\kappa$, and chapter 5 writes it $\hat S(\kappa)$. Rissanen et al. report that natural images have power spectral density close to $1/f^\alpha$ with $\alpha\approx2$; the three-dimensional turbulence slope is steeper. A signal-to-noise ratio per mode is the ratio of signal to noise density at the same wavenumber; shell counting cancels from it, so the space dimension enters it only through $\lambda$.

Noise ordering. Along the course path $X_t=tz+(1-t)\boldsymbol\xi$ with white noise of per-mode density $\Phi_\xi$, the per-mode ratio is

$$\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm noise}(\kappa,t)=\frac{t^2C\kappa^{-\lambda}}{(1-t)^2\Phi_\xi},\qquad \kappa_\times(t)=\left(\frac{t^2C}{(1-t)^2\Phi_\xi}\right)^{1/\lambda},$$

where $\kappa_\times$ is the crossover wavenumber at which the ratio equals one. The symbol $\kappa_\times$ is distinct from the filter wavenumber $\kappa_c=\pi/\Delta$; chapter 5 uses the same symbol, $\kappa_\times(t)$, for this crossover. The ratio decreases as a power of $\kappa$, so high wavenumbers are lost first, which is the statement of Rissanen et al. that with added isotropic noise "the highest frequencies get drowned out by the noise while the lower frequencies stay intact". The transition is broad: the ratio falls from 10 to 0.1 over a factor $100^{1/\lambda}$ in wavenumber, $15.85$ for $\lambda=5/3$ and $3.51$ for $\lambda=11/3$, the same at every $t$. The signal itself is only rescaled by $t$; its spectral shape is unchanged. This ordering needs a decreasing spectrum: for a white spectrum, $\lambda=0$, the ratio is the same at every wavenumber and noise imposes no order, and a spectrum that rises below its energy-containing peak is ordered this way only above the peak.

Blur ordering. Heat flow to time $s$ followed by a floor $\sigma_f^2$ per mode gives

$$\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm blur}(\kappa,s)=\frac{e^{-2\nu\kappa^2s}C\kappa^{-\lambda}}{\sigma_f^2},\qquad 2\nu s\,\kappa_\times^2+\lambda\ln\kappa_\times=\ln\frac{C}{\sigma_f^2}.$$

Rissanen et al. state that heat dissipation decays the highest frequencies faster than the lower ones, and the formula makes the rate explicit: the logarithmic slope is $d\ln\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm blur}/d\ln\kappa=-(4\nu s\kappa^2+\lambda)$, steeper than the noise slope $-\lambda$ by $4\nu s\kappa^2$. When the floor is small the quadratic term dominates and $\kappa_\times\approx(\ln(C/\sigma_f^2)/(2\nu s))^{1/2}=(12\ln(C/\sigma_f^2))^{1/2}/\Delta$: the crossover moves as $1/\Delta$, with the spectral slope entering only through a logarithm. Near the crossover the band from ratio 10 to ratio 0.1 has relative width close to $\ln100/(4\nu s\kappa_\times^2+\lambda)$, which shrinks as the floor decreases. Blur with a floor behaves as a smoothed sharp cutoff.

The program matches the two orderings at $\kappa_\times=16$, with $C=\Phi_\xi=1$ and a blur floor $\sigma_f^2=10^{-8}$. This floor is an illustrative value: at $\kappa=16$ the signal density is $9.84\times10^{-3}$ for $\lambda=5/3$ and $3.84\times10^{-5}$ for $\lambda=11/3$, and a floor above the density of a given $\lambda$ would hold the crossover of that $\lambda$ below 16 for every $s\gt0$. The blur figures below depend on it. For $\lambda=5/3$ this requires $t=0.909742$ or $\nu s=0.026953$ ($\Delta=0.8043$ on a $2\pi$-periodic line); for $\lambda=11/3$, $t=0.993837$ or $\nu s=0.016122$ ($\Delta=0.6220$). The band ratio is $15.849$ for noise and $1.172$ for blur when $\lambda=5/3$, and $3.511$ for noise and $1.259$ for blur when $\lambda=11/3$. At $\kappa=32$, twice the crossover, the noise ratio is still $0.3150$ ($\lambda=5/3$) and $0.07875$ ($\lambda=11/3$), whereas the blur ratio is $3.3\times10^{-19}$ and $1.4\times10^{-12}$. At $\kappa=4$ the blur ratio is $4.2\times10^6$ and $3.7\times10^5$, against $10.08$ and $161.3$ for noise.

For a decreasing spectrum both orderings therefore remove high wavenumbers first. Rissanen et al. reach the same conclusion for images, writing that standard diffusion models "implicitly perform a different type of coarse-to-fine generation". The measurable differences are these. First, the width of the transition: noise produces a band whose width in $\ln\kappa$ is fixed by $\lambda$ alone, blur a band that narrows as the floor falls. Second, the location law: the noise crossover depends on $t/(1-t)$ through the power $2/\lambda$, the blur crossover, for a small floor, on $\Delta$ through $1/\Delta$. Third, the treatment of the signal: noise leaves the spectral shape of the retained modes unchanged, blur attenuates every mode with $\kappa\ne0$, including those with large signal-to-noise ratio. For a turbulence field, the dissipation range lies at the high-wavenumber end of the spectrum and is lost first under both orderings. Chapter 5 develops the motion of $\kappa_\times(t)$ for the noise path and its consequence for generated spectra; this section supplies the blur counterpart for comparison.

4.9 Large-eddy deconvolution solves the same backward problem with a different cap

A large-eddy simulation with an explicit Gaussian filter carries $\bar u=G^{\rm gauss}_\Delta*u=T(s)u$, $s=\Delta^2/(24\nu)$. Estimating $u$ from $\bar u$, which a deconvolution-based subgrid model needs to evaluate the filtered nonlinear term, is the backward heat problem of Section 4.6. Its difficulty depends on $\Delta/h$. Pope (2004), as reported in essentials chapter 19, "How many Fourier modes a filtered field needs", states that physical large-eddy simulation is generally performed with $h/\Delta=1$ or $\tfrac12$, and that grid-independent explicitly filtered solutions need $h/\Delta\le\tfrac14$ for a second-order scheme. The corresponding Nyquist amplifications are $1.51$, $5.18$ and $720.35$ per direction. The finest of these grids, which resolves the filter best, multiplies a Nyquist-mode error by about 477 times the factor of the coarsest ($720.35/1.51$), and by $3.74\times10^8$ at the corner mode of a three-dimensional grid. Section 4.6 measures the consequence for a one-dimensional 64-point signal with noise $10^{-3}$: at $\Delta=4h$ the exact inverse has a smaller error than the blurred data, and at $\Delta=8h$ it does not.

The approximate deconvolution procedure of Stolz and Adams (Phys. Fluids 11(7), 1699–1701, 1999) is described in its abstract as an approximation of the nonfiltered field "by truncated series expansion of the inverse filter operator". The paper's own operator, truncation order and any additional terms were not verified, because its body was not read. The derivation below constructs one such truncated series, the van Cittert series, and analyses it.

Formally $G^{-1}=(I-(I-G))^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}(I-G)^n$ wherever the geometric series converges. Define the truncation $Q_N=\sum_{n=0}^N(I-G)^n$ of order $N$. In this section and in Exercise 4.7, $N$ is this order and not a number of grid points; in the program's output, "van Cittert N=" has this meaning and "N=64" counts points. On mode $\kappa$, $Q_N$ multiplies by $\hat Q_N=\sum_{n=0}^N(1-\hat G)^n$.

Proposition 4.7 — the truncated inverse series. Let $0\lt\hat G\le1$, as for the Gaussian filter. Then (i) $\hat Q_N\hat G=1-(1-\hat G)^{N+1}\in(0,1]$; (ii) $1\le\hat Q_N\le N+1$; (iii) $\hat Q_N\to1/\hat G$ as $N\to\infty$, with relative defect $(1-\hat G)^{N+1}$. If instead $\hat G\lt0$ at some $\kappa$, as for the box filter at $\kappa\Delta=3\pi$, then $|\hat Q_N|\to\infty$ at that $\kappa$.

Proof. Write $q=1-\hat G$. For $\hat G\ne0$ the finite geometric sum gives $\hat Q_N=(1-q^{N+1})/(1-q)=(1-q^{N+1})/\hat G$, which is (i); since $0\le q\lt1$, $q^{N+1}\in[0,1)$ and the product lies in $(0,1]$. Each of the $N+1$ terms of $\hat Q_N$ lies in $[0,1]$ and the first equals one, which gives (ii). As $N\to\infty$, $q^{N+1}\to0$, so $\hat Q_N\to1/\hat G$, and $1-\hat Q_N\hat G=q^{N+1}$ is the relative defect. If $\hat G\lt0$ then $q\gt1$, $q^{N+1}\to\infty$ and $|\hat Q_N|=(q^{N+1}-1)/|\hat G|\to\infty$. For the box, $\hat G(3\pi/\Delta)=\sin(3\pi/2)/(3\pi/2)=-0.2122$, so $q=1.2122$. ∎

The truncation caps the gain at $N+1$ for every mode, independently of the data. For the Gaussian filter the defect satisfies $(1-\hat G)^{N+1}\le e^{-(N+1)\hat G}$, with near equality for small $\hat G$, so a mode with $(N+1)\hat G\ll1$ keeps a defect close to one and remains largely unrecovered. The series also shows that the family matters: the Gaussian's positive transfer makes every truncation a bounded partial inverse, while the box filter's negative lobes make the series diverge on those modes.

The program compares the three inverses on the 64-point signal of Section 4.6. At $\Delta=4h$ the van Cittert truncations with $N=5$, $20$ and $100$ have maximum gains $5.979$, $20.711$ and $94.300$ and relative errors $0.1148$, $0.0909$ and $0.0750$; the Wiener estimate, the posterior mean of Proposition 4.6 with $a_{k-1}=1$ computed from the exact prior spectrum of the signal, gives $0.0744$. At $\Delta=8h$ the errors are $0.1957$, $0.1768$ and $0.1946$, against $0.1687$ for Wiener and $2.352\times10^7$ for the exact inverse. The error at $N=100$ exceeds the error at $N=20$: increasing the truncation order first removes blur and then amplifies noise, so the order acts as a regularization parameter with a best value that depends on $\Delta/h$ and on the noise. The Wiener gain is the same kind of cap, set mode by mode from the signal spectrum and the noise level instead of uniformly.

The comparison clarifies what a generative model adds. A deconvolution returns one estimate. Under the Gaussian modal prior, with $a_{k-1}=1$, Proposition 4.6 shows that a posterior-mean estimate has its per-mode variance reduced by the factor $a_k^2S/(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)$ relative to the true field: a posterior-mean field has too little energy in the modes that the blur pushed below the floor. A generative model of the conditional law of $u$ given $\bar u$ instead draws those modes at random, which can restore their expected energy at the price of a sample that differs from the true field mode by mode. Whether a trained model achieves this for turbulence is an empirical question, which chapter 5 takes up with physical statistics.

Part 2 calls no FFT: it takes explicit Euler steps of the second-order finite-difference heat equation on a fixed band-limited field, with $dt\propto h^2$, and compares them with the per-mode solution of Proposition 4.1(b). """Gaussian filters as heat flow, semigroup tests and backward growth.

Chapter 4 of Transport, Continua and Generative Models of Turbulent Flow.
Standard library and NumPy only; periodic box [0, 2*pi) in every direction.
"""
import numpy as np

NU = 1.0
TWO_PI = 2 * np.pi


def heat(f, s, nu=NU):
    """Exact spectral heat flow d_s f = nu * lap f in any dimension."""
    axes = [np.fft.fftfreq(m, d=1.0 / m) for m in f.shape]
    ks = np.meshgrid(*axes, indexing="ij")
    k2 = sum(k * k for k in ks)
    return np.real(np.fft.ifftn(np.exp(-nu * k2 * s) * np.fft.fftn(f)))


def gauss_matrix(n, width):
    """Circulant matrix of the periodised Gaussian kernel (quadrature)."""
    h = TWO_PI / n
    x = np.arange(n) * h
    r = x[:, None] - x[None, :]
    images = r[..., None] + TWO_PI * np.arange(-3, 4)
    g = np.sqrt(6 / np.pi) / width * np.exp(-6 * images**2 / width**2)
    return h * g.sum(axis=-1)


def random_field_1d(n, rng, slope=5 / 6):
    """Unit-rms periodic signal with rfft amplitudes |k|**(-slope)."""
    k = np.arange(n // 2 + 1)
    amp = np.zeros(n // 2 + 1)
    amp[1:-1] = k[1:-1] ** (-slope)
    c = amp * (rng.normal(size=k.size) + 1j * rng.normal(size=k.size))
    c /= np.sqrt(2)
    f = np.fft.irfft(c, n)
    scale = 1 / f.std()
    return f * scale, amp * scale


def rel(g, f):
    """Relative L2 error of an estimate g of f."""
    return np.linalg.norm(g - f) / np.linalg.norm(f)


def snr(kap, lam, scale, nus, floor):
    """Per-mode SNR: scale * exp(-2 nu s kap^2) * kap^-lam / floor."""
    return scale * np.exp(-2 * nus * kap**2) * kap ** (-lam) / floor


def level_crossing(lam, scale, nus, floor, level):
    """Wavenumber where the monotone per-mode SNR equals level."""
    lo, hi = 1e-3, 1e4
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = np.sqrt(lo * hi)
        big = snr(mid, lam, scale, nus, floor) > level
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if big else (lo, mid)
    return mid

print("2. Independent check: explicit finite-difference heat equation")
phase = rng.uniform(0, TWO_PI, size=8)
modes = np.arange(1, 9)
previous = None
steps = int(np.ceil(s / (0.2 * (TWO_PI / 128) ** 2 / NU)))
for n in (128, 256, 512):
    h = TWO_PI / n
    x = np.arange(n) * h

    exact = np.sum(modes[:, None] ** (-5 / 6)

    err = np.abs(u - exact).max()

    gain = np.exp(-NU * k**2 * s)
    d_hat = gain * np.fft.rfft(f) + np.fft.rfft(noise)
    data = np.fft.irfft(d_hat, n)
    naive = np.fft.irfft(d_hat / gain, n)
    print(f"  N={n}, Delta={ratio}h, sigma={sigma}: data error "
          f"{rel(data, f):.4f}, naive inverse {rel(naive, f):.3e}")
    for order in (5, 20, 100):
        q_n = sum((1 - gain) ** j for j in range(order + 1))
        est = np.fft.irfft(q_n * d_hat, n)
        print(f"    van Cittert N={order:3d}: max gain {q_n.max():8.3f}, "
              f"error {rel(est, f):.4f}")
    prior = amp**2
    wiener = gain * prior / (gain**2 * prior + n * sigma**2)
    bound = amp[1:-1] / (2 * np.sqrt(n) * sigma)
    print(f"    Wiener (Gaussian prior): max gain {wiener.max():.3f}, "

4.11 Exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 4.1 — the three-dimensional width–time relation (derivation)

Show that the isotropic three-dimensional Gaussian filter of width $\Delta$ is $T(s)$ with $s=\Delta^2/(24\nu)$, and compute the mean of $|\mathbf r|^2$ under its kernel in terms of $\Delta$ and of $\nu s$.

Solution

The kernel is $\prod_{j=1}^3(6/(\pi\Delta^2))^{1/2}e^{-6r_j^2/\Delta^2}$, a product of normal densities of variance $\Delta^2/12$. Its transfer function is the product $\prod_je^{-\kappa_j^2\Delta^2/24}=e^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Delta^2/24}$. Proposition 4.1(a) gives the heat transfer $e^{-\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$, and the two coincide for all $\boldsymbol\kappa$ exactly when $\Delta^2=24\nu s$. The coordinates are independent with variance $\Delta^2/12$ each, so $\mathbb E|\mathbf r|^2=3\Delta^2/12=\Delta^2/4=6\nu s$. The spherical box of essentials (19.20) has per-coordinate second moment $\Delta^2/20$, so the same name $\Delta$ carries a smaller second moment there, and no heat time represents it (Exercise 4.3).

Exercise 4.2 — grid and test filters in heat time (computation)

A dynamic procedure applies a Gaussian grid filter of width $\Delta$ and then a Gaussian test filter of width $2\Delta$. Find the composite width and its heat time. Which test width gives a composite width of exactly $2\Delta$, and what heat time does the test filter then add?

Solution

By Proposition 4.3 the composite width is $\sqrt{\Delta^2+4\Delta^2}=\sqrt5\Delta$, as in modeling §20.4, and its heat time is $5\Delta^2/(24\nu)$, the sum of $\Delta^2/(24\nu)$ and $4\Delta^2/(24\nu)$. For a composite $2\Delta$ the heat times must sum to $4\Delta^2/(24\nu)$, so the test filter must add $3\Delta^2/(24\nu)=\Delta^2/(8\nu)$, which is width $\sqrt3\Delta$. The ratio of composite to grid width is then $2$; calling the second filter "width $2\Delta$" gives the ratio $\sqrt5=2.236$ instead, a distinction that modeling §20.4 notes for a dynamic model's scale ratio.

Exercise 4.3 — the spherical box is not a heat flow (derivation)

The spherical box filter of width $\Delta$ has transfer $\hat G(\kappa)=3(\sin x-x\cos x)/x^3$ with $x=\kappa\Delta/2$ (essentials (19.20)). Prove that the family is not a heat semigroup and is not closed under composition.

Solution

The transfer vanishes where $\sin x=x\cos x$, that is, where $\tan x=x$; the first positive root is $x\approx4.4934$. A heat transfer $e^{-\nu\kappa^2s}$ is never zero, so no $s$ represents the filter. For closure, the self-composition has transfer $\hat G^2\ge0$. Every member of the family takes a negative value: at $x=6$, $\sin6=-0.2794$ and $\cos6=0.9602$, so $\sin x-x\cos x=-0.2794-5.7610=-6.0404$ and $\hat G=3(-6.0404)/216=-0.0839$. For a member of width $b$ this value occurs at $\kappa=12/b$. A nonnegative function cannot equal a function that is negative somewhere, so $\hat G^2$ belongs to no spherical box, and the family admits no semigroup law.

Exercise 4.4 — the Cauchy semigroup (derivation, computation and conceptual)

Show that Cauchy widths add linearly under composition, and write the evolution equation of $\hat f$ in the parameter $\Delta$. The Cauchy and Gaussian transfers agree at $\kappa_c$; compare them at $3\kappa_c$, and explain why agreement at one wavenumber cannot make the Cauchy filter a heat flow.

Solution

The transfers multiply to $e^{-a(\Delta_1+\Delta_2)|\kappa|}$, the Cauchy transfer of width $\Delta_1+\Delta_2$. Differentiating $\hat f(\kappa,\Delta)=e^{-a\Delta|\kappa|}\hat f(\kappa,0)$ gives $\partial_\Delta\hat f=-a|\kappa|\hat f$, the Fourier form of $\partial_\Delta f=-a(-\nabla^2)^{1/2}f$. At $\kappa_c=\pi/\Delta$ both transfers equal $e^{-\pi^2/24}=0.6628$. At $3\kappa_c$ the Cauchy value is $e^{-3\pi^2/24}=0.2912$ and the Gaussian value is $e^{-9\pi^2/24}=0.0247$ (essentials Table 19.2): the Cauchy filter passes $11.8$ times the Gaussian amplitude there. One equation $a\Delta\kappa_c=\nu s\kappa_c^2$ fixes $s$, but the heat form requires $a\Delta|\kappa|=\nu s\kappa^2$ at every $\kappa$; the two sides grow as $|\kappa|$ and $\kappa^2$, so they agree at most at one nonzero $|\kappa|$.

Exercise 4.5 — when roundoff alone defeats exact deconvolution (computation)

A three-dimensional field filtered with a Gaussian of width $\Delta$ is stored in double precision, with relative rounding error near $1.1\times10^{-16}$. For $\Delta/h=2$ and $\Delta/h=8$, compute the exact-inverse amplification at the one-dimensional Nyquist mode and at the corner mode $|\boldsymbol\kappa|=\sqrt3\pi/h$. In which case does rounding alone make the corner modes unrecoverable?

Solution

The amplification is $\exp(\pi^2(\Delta/h)^2m/24)$ with $m=1$ at the Nyquist mode of one axis and $m=3$ at the corner. For $\Delta/h=2$: $e^{\pi^2/6}=5.18$ and $e^{\pi^2/2}=139.0$. Rounding errors grow to about $1.5\times10^{-14}$ relative to the field scale, which is harmless. For $\Delta/h=8$: $e^{8\pi^2/3}=2.69\times10^{11}$ and $e^{8\pi^2}=1.95\times10^{34}$. The corner factor exceeds the reciprocal of the rounding error, about $9\times10^{15}$, by more than eighteen orders of magnitude, so even a field stored without any measurement noise cannot be deconvolved exactly at the corner. The blurred corner amplitude itself is below $10^{-34}$ of the original, so the stored value carries no information about it.

Exercise 4.6 — a buried mode in a noisy reverse step (derivation and computation)

In the setting of Proposition 4.6, take a mode with prior standard deviation $\sqrt S=0.05$, noise $\sigma=0.01$, $a_{k-1}=1$ and $a_k=e^{-\pi^2\cdot16/24}$, the Nyquist factor for $\Delta/h=4$. Compute the optimal gain, the bound $\sqrt S/(2\sigma)$, and the gain of the exact backward step. Then show that the predicted coefficient never has more variance than the true one.

Solution

Here $a_k=e^{-6.5797}=1.388\times10^{-3}$, $S=2.5\times10^{-3}$, $a_kS=3.470\times10^{-6}$ and $a_k^2S=4.82\times10^{-9}$. The gain is $3.470\times10^{-6}/(4.82\times10^{-9}+10^{-4})=0.0347$. The bound is $0.05/0.02=2.5$, and the exact backward gain is $1/a_k=720.4$. The mode is buried, since $a_k\sqrt S=6.9\times10^{-5}\ll\sigma$, and the optimal step nearly returns the prior mean zero instead of multiplying the noise by 720. The predicted variance is $g^2(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)=a_k^2S^2/(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)=S\cdot a_k^2S/(a_k^2S+\sigma^2)\le S$, with ratio $4.82\times10^{-5}$ for these numbers: the posterior mean keeps almost none of this mode's energy.

Exercise 4.7 — the van Cittert defect (computation)

For $N=5$, compute the relative defect $(1-\hat G)^{N+1}$ of Proposition 4.7 for the Gaussian filter at $\kappa\Delta=\pi$ and $\kappa\Delta=2\pi$, and the factor $(1-\hat G)^{N+1}$ for the box filter at $\kappa\Delta=3\pi$. Interpret the three numbers.

Solution

At $\kappa\Delta=\pi$ the Gaussian has $\hat G=0.66283$, so $(0.33717)^6=1.47\times10^{-3}$: the mode is recovered to about 0.15 %. At $\kappa\Delta=2\pi$, $\hat G=e^{-\pi^2/6}=0.19302$ and $(0.80698)^6=0.276$: about 28 % of the deconvolution is still missing after six terms. For the box at $3\pi$, $\hat G=-0.21221$ and $(1.21221)^6=3.17$, which grows with $N$: the partial sums diverge. They stay positive while the true inverse $1/\hat G$ is negative, so the reconstruction multiplies the mode by $1-3.17=-2.17$ instead of by one. Approximate deconvolution by this series requires a positive transfer function, which the Gaussian has and the box does not.

Exercise 4.8 — matching the two orderings at a lower crossover (computation)

Use $\Phi(\kappa)=\kappa^{-11/3}$, $\Phi_\xi=1$ and a blur floor $\sigma_f^2=10^{-8}$. Find the generation time $t$ and the heat time $\nu s$ that place the crossover at $\kappa_\times=8$. Estimate the blur band ratio $\kappa(\mathrm{SNR}=0.1)/\kappa(\mathrm{SNR}=10)$ by linearizing $\ln\mathrm{SNR}$ in $\ln\kappa$, and compare with the noise band ratio.

Solution

Noise: $(t/(1-t))^2=8^{11/3}$, so $t/(1-t)=8^{11/6}=2^{5.5}=45.25$ and $t=45.25/46.25=0.97838$. Blur: $2\nu s\cdot64=\ln10^8-\tfrac{11}3\ln8=18.4207-7.6246=10.7961$, so $\nu s=0.084344$ and $\Delta=\sqrt{24\nu s}=1.423$. The blur slope at the crossover is $-(4\nu s\kappa_\times^2+\lambda)=-(21.592+3.667)=-25.259$, so the band spans $\ln100/25.259=0.1823$ in $\ln\kappa$, a ratio of $e^{0.1823}=1.20$. The noise ratio is $100^{3/11}=3.51$, independent of the crossover. The same linearization at $\kappa_\times=16$ gives $1.256$, close to the program's exact $1.259$. Lowering the crossover from 16 to 8 makes the blur band slightly narrower, because $\nu s$ rises more than $\kappa_\times^2$ falls.

This chapter establishes that a Gaussian large-eddy filter of width $\Delta$ is heat flow to time $s=\Delta^2/(24\nu)$, that composition adds heat times, and that among the course's filter families only the Gaussian has this property: Cauchy and Pao are semigroups with fractional generators, the box is not closed under composition, and the sharp cutoff is idempotent. It proves that reversing the blur multiplies mode $\boldsymbol\kappa$ by $e^{\nu|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2s}$, a factor of $e^{\pi^2(\Delta/h)^2/24}$ at the grid Nyquist mode whatever the number of points, and that noise in training, under a Gaussian modal prior, and truncation of the van Cittert series both cap the per-mode gain, at $a_{k-1}\sqrt S/(2\sigma)$ and at $N+1$ respectively.

It does not establish that a trained inverse-heat-dissipation network reaches the posterior mean, that the Gaussian-prior bound holds for real data, or that blur-based generation reproduces turbulence statistics; nor does it reproduce the operator of Stolz and Adams. Chapter 5 takes up the noise ordering in detail, follows the crossover $\kappa_\times(t)$ through generation, and tests generative models of turbulence against physical statistics, among them the energy of the high-wavenumber modes, which under the Gaussian modal prior of Proposition 4.6 a posterior-mean estimate underestimates.
